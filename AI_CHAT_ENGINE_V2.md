# AI chat engine v2 (21 Oct 2026): what was built and what each part improves

The four steps from the design talk, built in order on top of everything decided before. Nothing you decided changed: the first-sale path, the cadence, the pause after a big spend, the dry rule, the refusal list and the rest still hold as hard rules. What changed is how the reply is reached in the moments between those rules, and what the engine learns from your practice.

## Step 1. Practice conversations are saved; a starred reply teaches her

**What it does.** Every practice chat is saved as it goes (1.5 seconds after each change): the messages, every reply with its decision map, the session, and what you did as him after each reply (bought, didn't buy, ignored, wrote on). "Saved conversations" in the Practice sidebar lists them; opening one brings it back exactly where it was, decision maps included, so you can continue it or mark it. Under each of her kept replies there is "Star it": the reply joins her example library (the same library the 35k analysed chats feed), tagged with the moment, his message and how sexual both were, and it counts more than the analysed examples when examples are picked for a similar moment. A reply whose PPV he bought is stored as a selling example. A replay script runs saved conversations through the engine after every change and lists each turn where today's decision differs from the one made then.

**What it improves.** Before, a practice chat vanished on reload and the engine learned only from single marks. Now your chatting today is a test suite (every future change is checked against your real conversations, not only my made-up ones) and a training set (the replies you star become the examples she imitates in that kind of moment). Teaching by example is faster than teaching by rule: star three good replies to a shy new subscriber and she has three models for shy new subscribers.

## Step 2. Claude reads the moment; code keeps the numbers

**What it does.** The small planner (Ministral, 160 tokens, "level 0 to 3, mood, pulled back") is replaced by a reading step on Claude Haiku, with Ministral kept as the fallback. For each message Claude answers: what he is doing (small talk, sharing, asking, flirting, sexting, testing, provoking, joking, venting, negotiating, buying, leaving, complaining), what is new or surprising, up to three ways his message can be read with how sure it is, what a real girl in her place would want to ask, the stance that fits (steady, curious, playful, match, deflect, comfort, firm), and whether the line is odd for a man chatting up a girl (about other men, a non-sequitur, a provocation). Code checks the read against his own words as before (short messages with nothing sexual are flirty at most, unmistakable intent is at least suggestive, politics gets no curiosity, doubt needs his own doubt words). The writer gets the reading in plain lines: what is new, what she is not sure about, what she would want to know, her stance. A question she asked is remembered in the session, so she never asks it twice. Cost: about $0.0004 a reply on Haiku (three times the old planner, still a fraction of a cent).

**What it improves.** The old planner read context, not the message: "hey babe" from a spender came back as sexual, "did you think about it?" as sexual, "I'm broke" as pulling back. The reading separates what he did from how it sounds. "I love hard dicks" from a stranger is now read as an odd, possibly testing line with two readings (wants a reaction 60%, into it 50%), and she gets a question to ask ("what is it about hard ones that gets you going?") instead of a tease. "My wife doesn't know I'm here" is read as sharing something heavy, stance comfort, question "are you okay being here?". The decision map shows the reading, so you can see what she thought he meant.

## Step 3. Meters instead of if-then

**What it does.** Seven gauges, 0 to 100, kept in the session and moved every turn: his heat, his mood, rapport, readiness to pay, doubt, engagement, surprise. The numbers move them (his tier, a buy, a no, a price objection, being broke, the pause, the cadence, one-word replies, photos, tips) and the reading moves them (what he is doing, what is new, how sure, an odd line). Where the goal ladder only said "talk" (chat, get to know, flirt, the sexy vibe, tease, steer, the readiness check), every move her reply could make is scored against the gauges: react, a curious question, share something of hers, get to know him, flirt, tease, steer, offer, the readiness check, comfort, reassure. The best allowed move wins, and the ladder's own choice is the incumbent: another move has to beat it clearly. Every hard rule stays as a veto: no PPV before a new subscriber's direct ask, one attempt a day, the pause after a big spend, no selling to a sad or doubting man, the first-sale stages (in the vibe stage only talk moves are allowed, in the flirt stage flirting joins, in the sexy stage teasing joins), the warm-up. The Practice sidebar shows the meters live; the decision map has a step "The meters" with every gauge's move and reason and every move's score.

**What it improves.** A reply is no longer the first rule that matches. "I love hard dicks" from a stranger: surprise high, rapport low, readiness low, an odd line, so curiosity (76) beats teasing (57). "Fuck you look so good, I'm hard already" from a spender: heat up, nothing odd, so teasing (62) beats curiosity (56). A fishing story from a new subscriber in the vibe stage gets a real follow-up question instead of a generic "nice". A spender on a selling day who asks what she has: readiness 100, offer wins. A sad man: comfort wins whatever the planner called his mood. The same message gets a different reply with a different man and a different history, which is what a person does. The weights are in one file (gauges.js), readable, and tuned against your outcomes and starred replies rather than by rewriting rules.

## Step 4. Beats, and what stays open about him

**What it does.** The plan now tells the writer the beats of the reply: one to three things, in order ("react to what he just said; one real question about it: …"), from the goal and from the runner-up move when it fits (never two questions, never a tease with comfort). An "answer his question plainly" beat is added when he asked something, except for politics. A curious reply must ask the planned question, not a rhetorical one, or it is rewritten. Readings that did not settle ("he may be gay, 50%") stay open in the session for a few turns and the writer sees "still open about him"; a later reading that is sure closes them.

**What it improves.** Replies stop doing everything at once (greet, react, ask, tease, sell in one breath), which is the single most robotic trait in the old transcripts. Doubt becomes a state she carries, not a line she forgets: she can come back to it when it matters for the money ("wait, are you even into girls?") instead of selling nudes to a man who told her he isn't.

## What was tested

Thirty-seven simulated dialogues (the deep-dive set plus the screenshots' "I love hard dicks" fan and a married man sharing something heavy) run through the engine with the reader on and the writer off, each turn checked against your rules; plus three full written runs (reader, meters, beats, Mistral writing, Claude checking) to see her actual replies. Results and remaining weak spots are in the chat report.

## What is still the AI's call, and the next lever

Code decides every number (prices, cadence, stages, holds, budgets), checks the reading against his words, and scores the moves. Claude reads the human side (what he is doing, what is new, what it could mean). Mistral writes. The weakest link now is the writing: with a clear plan and clear beats, Mistral still sometimes stalls on a sexual line or asks a rhetorical question instead of the planned one; Claude's check catches it and the targeted rewrite fixes some. The next lever is yours to judge from the blind tests already run (Mistral against Haiku and against Sonnet on talk moments): a Claude writer for talk and curious moments, Mistral for selling and sexting, at about three cents a reply for Sonnet.
