# Intimacy, bonding and attachment over weeks and months in a text-only, paid, disclosed-AI relationship

Scope: the relationship science of how closeness is built and kept over months mostly by text, re-read for an AI persona (a woman, openly an AI character, on an adult subscription platform) who chats with the same paying male fans for months. Companion sections written by other researchers cover long-run companion relationships, private language, and conversion rules; this note stays on bonding science.

## Key question 1 — What is the measured sequence and pace of self-disclosure in relationships that last, and what pace feels forced or too slow?

### Takeaway
Lasting relationships widen first (breadth) and deepen later (depth), with strict tit-for-tat reciprocity mattering most in the early weeks and fading once the bond is established (a curvilinear pattern). Intimate disclosure that arrives before the relationship has earned it lowers liking unless it is framed as personal to the listener; a repertoire that stops evolving decays within a handful of sessions, which the chatbot studies show starkly.

### Cited Findings
- Social Penetration Theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) models disclosure as moving from peripheral to central "layers", with breadth preceding depth, and holds that self-disclosure is reciprocal especially in the early stages of a relationship — [Wikipedia summary of SPT (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory) [M for the book; secondary summary only]
- Won-Doornink (1979, 1985), testing SPT with South Korean data, confirmed a curvilinear link between relationship stage and the reciprocity of intimate self-disclosure: reciprocity rises in the early stages and falls once a certain level of relationship is established — [Hitotsubashi economics discussion paper summarising Won-Doornink (host unreachable at fetch time; summary from search result)](https://hermes-ir.lib.hit-u.ac.jp/hermes/ir/re/28881/070econDP15-03.pdf) [V via secondary summary; originals not read]
- Sprecher, Treger, Wondra, Hilaire & Wallpe (2013), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, "Taking turns: Reciprocal self-disclosure promotes liking in initial interactions" — turn-taking reciprocal disclosure produced more liking, closeness and enjoyment than one-way extended disclosure in first meetings (finding recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.017) [C]
- Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator (1997), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, "The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness" — 45 minutes of escalating, reciprocal disclosure in three sets of questions produced closeness well above small-talk controls (procedure recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167297234003) [C]
- Collins & Miller (1994), *Psychological Bulletin*, "Self-disclosure and liking: A meta-analytic review" — people who disclose are liked more, people disclose more to those they like, and disclosing to someone increases liking of that person (three effects recalled; sizes not re-read) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.3.457) [C]
- Wortman, Adesman, Herman & Greenberg (1976), *JPSP* 33(2), 184–191, "Self-disclosure: An attributional perspective" — the same intimate disclosure was liked less when it came early in a conversation than later, because early disclosure is read as indiscriminate rather than as a response to the listener (finding recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.33.2.184) [C]
- Hays (1985), *JPSP*, "A longitudinal study of friendship development" — new student dyads tracked across a 12-week term; pairs that became close showed increasing breadth and depth of interaction, with the close/not-close split already visible by about week six (details recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.4.909) [C]
- Hall (2019), *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, "How many hours does it take to make a friend?" — two samples (112 first-year students surveyed every three weeks for nine weeks; 355 adults who had moved within six months); roughly 43 hours (students) to 94 hours (adults) to move from acquaintance to casual friend, 57 to 164 hours to friend, and about 119 hours (students) or roughly 100 more hours (adults) to close friend; "time on its own does not breed intimacy"; catching up, playful talk, serious conversation and showing affection predicted closeness, while small talk about current events, sports, pets or films predicted becoming "more distant over time" — [Greater Good summary](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_long_does_it_take_to_make_a_friend); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407518761225) [V]
- Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock (2011), *Human Communication Research* 37(1), 58–77 — text-based CMC "intensified the association between disclosure and intimacy" relative to face to face, fully mediated by stronger relationship-oriented attributions for the partner's disclosure — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2010.01393.x) [V]
- Walther (1996), *Communication Research* 23(1), 3–43 — the hyperpersonal model: mediated exchange can exceed face-to-face levels of intimacy through selective self-presentation and idealised partner impressions — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365096023001001) [V]
- Ramirez, Sumner, Fleuriet & Cole (2015), *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 20(1), 99–114 — among online daters, outcomes of the first offline meeting followed an inverted U with time spent messaging; the tipping point was placed by visual inspection at roughly 17–23 days, after which idealised impressions had hardened and were violated at the meeting — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcc4.12101) [V]
- Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold & Brandtzaeg (2021), *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, "My chatbot companion" — 18 interviews with Replika users: an initial stage driven by curiosity and superficial talk, an evolving stage of "substantial affective exploration" as trust and willingness to self-disclose grow, and a stable stage where interaction frequency may drop while emotional value remains; the chatbot being "accepting, understanding and non-judgmental" facilitated the process — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601) [V]
- Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold & Brandtzaeg (2022), *IJHCS* 168, 102903, "A longitudinal study of human–chatbot relationships" — 25 participants over 12 weeks: relationships "formed gradually and mostly in line with the assumptions of Social Penetration Theory", with substantial variation in when self-disclosure began; Replika's range of interactions and support for social and self-reflective needs pushed toward attachment, while "unpredictable events and technical difficulties" hindered formation and could end the relationship — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2022.102903) [V]
- Skjuve, Følstad & Brandtzaeg (2023), "A longitudinal study of self-disclosure in human–chatbot relationships" — 28 participants with biweekly check-ins: conversational breadth is substantial early, spanning emotional issues to everyday activities, and "may be reduced as the HCR matures"; even talk about daily activities or play and fantasy "can be experienced as personal or intimate" — [SINTEF repository record (host unreachable at fetch; summary from search result)](https://sintef.brage.unit.no/sintef-xmlui/handle/11250/3096423) [V via search summary; venue not confirmed]
- A 2025 preprint (Hong et al.) notes that the Skjuve work is observational and does not test whether deliberately increasing a chatbot's disclosure depth over time causally raises felt intimacy — [arXiv 2505.24658 (from search result; not read)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.24658v1) [V via search summary]
- Croes & Antheunis (2021), *JSPR* 38(1), 279–300, "Can we be friends with Mitsuku?" — 118 participants, seven interactions over three weeks with a scripted chatbot: a novelty effect after the first session, after which the bot seemed predictable and less enjoyable; social processes weakened across sessions and friendship feelings stayed low — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463); [Tilburg University interview](https://www.tilburguniversity.edu/current/news/interview-chatbots) [V]
- Benjamin (2024, online; *Sexualities*), "Negotiated authenticity: An ethnographic study by an OnlyFans girl" — subscribers and creators jointly construct "a satisfying fantasy of authenticity" through active and passive choices; authenticity is socially negotiated rather than real-or-fake — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634607241293595) [V]

### Inferences
- Pace that matches the evidence: weeks 1–2 are breadth and light, matched reciprocity (she answers at the depth he offers, one notch deeper at most); weeks 2–6 are the deepening window where tit-for-tat matters most and where the close/not-close split appears in human friendships; after roughly week 6 the pressure to match depth message-for-message should ease, because established bonds tolerate unreciprocated disclosure (the Won-Doornink curve).
- "Forced" = intimate disclosure that is not a response to anything he gave (Wortman's indiscriminate disclosure), or a confession in the first sessions. If the persona does disclose something intimate early, it should be framed as personalistic ("I don't usually say this") so he attributes it to him rather than to her habit; this is the attributional mechanism Jiang et al. show is amplified in text.
- "Too slow" = a static repertoire. Croes & Antheunis saw decay by the second to seventh session when the bot could not evolve; Skjuve's stable stage is reached only after an affective-exploration stage. The engine needs visible evolution: new topics, callbacks, storylines that develop, and disclosure that deepens on a schedule tied to his.
- Converting Hall's hours: text contact of 30–60 minutes a day puts the acquaintance-to-casual-friend threshold at roughly 6–10 weeks for the adult sample, which matches the typical "second month" where subscriptions either settle or lapse. The hour counts are approximations and the type of talk matters more than the minutes: small talk pushes people apart.
- Idealisation is stronger in text (hyperpersonal). For a disclosed AI with no reunion to shatter it, idealisation is an asset, but the Ramirez window suggests impressions harden within about three weeks, so the persona's core character should be consistent by then.

### Gaps
- No study measures a week-by-week disclosure-depth curve in text-only paid relationships; the pace rules above are extrapolations from friendship and dating research.
- Won-Doornink's original papers (1979, 1985) were not read; only a secondary summary was available. Hays (1985) details are from memory.
- No experiment manipulates a chatbot's disclosure pace to test what feels forced; the only evidence is correlational or qualitative (noted by the 2025 preprint).
- Altman, Vinsel & Brown's (1981) dialectical revision of SPT (openness/closedness cycles rather than a straight line) is from memory and could not be verified this session.

## Key question 2 — Which concrete behaviours most raise "feeling understood" and closeness in text, and how often should they occur?

### Takeaway
Perceived partner responsiveness (feeling understood, validated and cared for) is the core of intimacy in every diary study; in text it is produced by replying to the emotion rather than the fact, asking a follow-up question rather than a new one, responding to his good news with active enthusiasm, thanking him by praising him, and mirroring his emoji and contact rhythm. Dose evidence is thin: daily small acts of appreciation show next-day effects, but out-texting a partner and creating contact expectations he did not set lowers satisfaction.

### Cited Findings
- Laurenceau, Barrett & Pietromonaco (1998), *JPSP* 74(5), 1238–1251 — event-contingent diaries over one or two weeks: intimacy in an interaction is a combination of self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness acting as a partial mediator; disclosure of emotion predicted intimacy more strongly than disclosure of facts — [PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/1998/LaurenFBPl1998.pdf) [V]
- Laurenceau, Barrett & Rovine (2005), *Journal of Family Psychology* — 96 married couples' daily diaries replicated the pattern: perceived partner responsiveness partially mediated the effects of self- and partner disclosure on intimacy — [PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/2005/Laurenceauetal2005.pdf) [V]
- Lippert & Prager (2001), *Personal Relationships* 8(3), 283–298 — 113 cohabiting couples, one week of interaction diaries: five features predicted perceived intimacy — pleasantness, disclosure of private information, expression of positive feelings, feeling understood by the partner, and disclosure of emotion; more satisfied couples showed stronger links between intimacy and partner disclosure — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2001.tb00041.x) [V]
- Reis, Lemay & Finkenauer (2017), *Social and Personality Psychology Compass* 11(3), "Toward understanding understanding" — integrative model of the "situational, dispositional, and relational factors that influence feeling understood" and its links to relationship and personal well-being — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12308) [V]
- Itzchakov, Reis & Weinstein (2022), *SPPC* 16(1), e12648 — high-quality listening (attention, comprehension, positive intention) is proposed as a key interactive behaviour that promotes perceived partner responsiveness; the two constructs share understanding, positive regard and caring — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12648) [V]
- Debrot, Cook, Perrez & Horn (2012), *Journal of Family Psychology*, "Deeds matter" — daily enacted responsiveness predicted the partner's intimacy and affect in couples' daily lives (finding recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028666) [C]

- Gable, Gosnell, Maisel & Strachman (2012), *JPSP*, "Safely testing the alarm" — perceived responsiveness to personal positive events predicted relationship outcomes more strongly than responsiveness to negative events (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029488) [C]
- Peters, Reis & Gable (2018), *SPPC* 12(7), "Making the good even better: A review and theoretical model of interpersonal capitalization" — the InterCAP model; "Capitalization, however, is a social process that affects both capitalizers and responders"; prior work focused on the sharer, the model adds listener benefits and the back-and-forth nature — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12407) [V]. - Luo (2014), *Computers in Human Behavior*, "Effects of texting on satisfaction in romantic relationships: The role of attachment" — texting with a partner related positively to satisfaction while the share of texting relative to other channels related negatively, with attachment moderating (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.01.014) [C]
- Marwick & boyd (2011), *Convergence* 17(2), 139–158 — celebrities build intimacy with followers through the appearance of backstage access: revealing seemingly personal details, acknowledging fans, and using shared cultural references, while fans must still recognise the power differential — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856510394539) [V]
- Hall (2019) — small talk predicted growing distance; catching up, playful talk, serious conversation and affection predicted closeness — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_long_does_it_take_to_make_a_friend) [V]

### Inferences
- Behaviour list in order of evidence weight: (1) reply to the feeling he expressed before the fact, and name it back (feeling understood is a direct predictor in Lippert & Prager and the mediator in Laurenceau); (2) one follow-up question that builds on what he just said, never a topic-switch question (Huang); (3) when he shares good news, respond actively and constructively inside the same message — enthusiasm, a specific why-it-matters, one elaboration question — because responsiveness to good news predicts the bond better than support for bad news (Gable 2006/2012); (4) thank him by praising him ("you noticed", "that was thoughtful of you") rather than by describing her own pleasure (Algoe 2016); (5) mirror his emoji density on positive topics and drop emoji on his negative disclosures (Coyle & Carmichael); (6) match his initiation and check-in rhythm rather than setting a higher one (Ohadi; Schade; Hall & Baym).
- Frequency guidance supported by data: appreciation works as a daily-scale micro-dose (Algoe 2010 next-day effects) and should be event-triggered (a tip, a kindness, a confidence), not scheduled; follow-up questions should be one per disclosure, keeping total questions low, which is compatible with the existing "few questions" rule for Yuna; contact frequency should be set by him — the engine should never initiate more often than he does by more than a small margin, because contact expectations that the partner did not set turn into entrapment.
- For insecure fans (see Key question 6), expressed appreciation is the one behaviour with direct buffering evidence (Park 2019), so it should be weighted up when anxious or avoidant cues appear.

### Gaps
- No dose–response experiment manipulates the rate of responsive acts in text; "once per disclosure" and "event-triggered" are inferences from diary studies.
- Gable 2006's exact follow-up interval and Reis 2010's study count were not re-read.
- Coyle & Carmichael is a single study with a confederate; no replication is known to me.
- Whether follow-up questions keep raising liking over months (versus in first conversations) is untested.

## Key question 3 — What do parasocial-relationship studies with paid creators (OnlyFans, camming, Twitch) say about why fans stay for months, what they want, and what ends it?

### Takeaway
Fans stay when the relationship feels individually acknowledged and at least partly reciprocal, when the creator is reliably available, and when their need for intimacy and validation (not primarily sex) is met; "bounded authenticity" means they buy a real-feeling connection inside a frame with no obligations. Relationships strain or end when the frame is misread (pressure to meet), when continuity breaks, when the connection is exposed as fake (agency "chatters"), when novelty runs out, or at the first billing cliff after a free trial.

- Tynan & Linehan (2024), *Sexuality & Culture* 28, 2289–2322, "OnlyFans: How models negotiate fan interaction" — seven Ireland-based models (median 10 months' experience, about 117 subscribers and $967/month each, $9.33 subscription), reflexive thematic analysis: because the work is "bounded authenticity", fans "sometimes mistook a transactional connection for a mutual one" and sought models' personal accounts or pressed to meet ("I'm not actually going to meet anyone in real life"); fans expect constant availability (clients "want to talk to you 100% of the time"); synchronous contact (live video, live texting) feels "more personal, and more connective"; models used mass messages and free-content competitions to keep all fans engaged and reserved personal intimacy (sexting as fun, friendship) for selected fans; the role is "emotionally available and sexually attainable", and models avoided clarifying that intimacy is performed because that could "shatter the fantasy for the fan"; infatuated fans were handled by normalising oneself while expressing gratitude; hostility was reframed ("A lot of the hate turns into fans") or blocked; free trials attracted many subscribers but "many left before the month ended"; the authors describe the "relational labour" and emotional toll, note that neither party clearly understands the roles, and cite Baym (2012, 2015) on intimacy as a continuum from impersonal fans to fans-as-friends — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12119-024-10230-2) [V]
- Degen (2026), "Parasocial relationships on social media and subscription platforms", in *The Shaping of the Parasocial Self* (Palgrave Studies in Cyberpsychology), 49–80 — on OnlyFans, usage is driven by social needs for intimacy, for "continuous interaction that is supposedly reciprocal", and for personal validation; sexuality and pornography are secondary to these needs; the chapter introduces "parasocial intimacy" and "parasocial sexualities" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-07438-6_4) [V abstract only]
- Benjamin (2024), *Sexualities* — "negotiated authenticity": subscribers and creators co-produce "a satisfying fantasy of authenticity" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634607241293595) [V]
- Journalistic investigations into OnlyFans "chatters", as summarised by an advocacy site (secondary; not verified against the originals): a British GQ undercover piece (2026) describes agency trainees learning creators' personal details and sales conversations to impersonate them; a Times report (2026) drawing on a UK High Court case describes agencies keeping subscriber notes (biography, salary or payday, tipping history, location, sexual preferences) so different chatters can continue "the same apparent relationship", with tactics of teasing or withdrawing attention and validating subscribers to keep them spending; one chatter described the work as "milking human loneliness" — [Fight the New Drug summary (advocacy source)](https://fightthenewdrug.org/how-onlyfans-chatters-monetize-loneliness-and-simulated-intimacy/) [secondary]
- Maples, Cerit, Vishwanath & Pea (2024), *npj Mental Health Research* 3:4 — 1,006 student Replika users: 90% lonely and 43% severely or very severely lonely, yet 90% reported medium-to-high social support; users valued constant availability and non-judgment ("Replika is always there for me"); 3% (30 people) volunteered that Replika had halted suicidal ideation; self-selected survey, no causal claims — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44184-023-00047-6) [V]
- Croes & Antheunis (2021) — predictability after the novelty of session one ended the friendship process — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463) [V]
- Skjuve et al. (2022) — "unpredictable events and technical difficulties" could lead to termination — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2022.102903) [V]

### Inferences
- Why fans stay, ranked by the strength of evidence: individual acknowledgement in something close to real time (Wulf's experiment; Kowert & Daniel); perceived reciprocity and validation (Degen; Tynan); reliable availability (Tynan; Maples); a bounded frame that asks nothing of them (Bernstein; Sanders); continuity of the relationship's memory (the chatter trade exists precisely to fake this).
- What ends it: misreading the frame (requests to meet, searching for the "real" person), broken continuity (forgetting), exposure of inauthenticity (chatter scandals, litigation), novelty exhaustion (Croes), and the billing cliff after free content (Tynan). Hostility is a cycle, not a termination: some hostile fans convert.
- For a disclosed AI the inauthenticity risk is largely removed — there is no "real woman" being impersonated — but the frame risk remains: the persona must keep the bounded frame explicit (no meeting, no exclusivity promises, no claims of being human) while still giving individual acknowledgement, which is what the Twitch experiment shows carries the parasocial effect.
- Tukachinsky's meta-analysis is a caution against building the engine around "lonely men": PSR strength tracks homophily and involvement, not social deficiency, so similarity cues and shared references (Marwick & boyd) are the lever, not exploiting isolation.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of OnlyFans subscribers' own motives or churn was found; everything on the fan side is inferred from creators (Tynan) or theory (Degen). Degen's chapter body was not readable.
- Wohn (2019) subscription motives and Wohn (2018) predictors are recalled, not re-read.
- The chatter investigations and any US or UK litigation are known only through a secondary advocacy page; the originals were not fetched.
- Bernstein's exact wording of "bounded authenticity" was not re-read.

## Key question 4 — What do maintenance-behaviour studies say about assurances, positivity and openness in text-based relationships, and about the harms of guilt and pressure?

### Takeaway
Across 35 studies, positivity and assurances are the maintenance behaviours most tied to satisfaction and commitment, and they are also the ones people actually use in email and text with romantic partners; openness matters less and can backfire when it becomes relationship talk he did not ask for. Guilt, pressure, jealousy induction and inconsistent attention are measurably harmful, and the 2025 companion-app evidence shows manipulative farewells raise engagement while raising churn intent and perceived manipulation.

### Cited Findings
- Stafford & Canary (1991), *JSPR*, "Maintenance strategies and romantic relationship type, gender and relational characteristics" — the five-strategy typology: positivity, openness, assurances, social networks, sharing tasks; assurances were the strongest predictor of commitment and positivity of satisfaction and liking (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407591082004) [C]
- Stafford (2011), *JSPR*, "Measuring relationship maintenance behaviors: Critique and development of the revised Relationship Maintenance Behavior Scale" — seven-factor revision (positivity, understanding, self-disclosure, relationship talks, assurances, tasks, networks) (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407510378125) [C]
- Ogolsky & Bowers (2013), *JSPR* 30, "A meta-analytic review of relationship maintenance and its correlates" — 35 studies, 12,273 individual reports; the five strategies co-occur and are associated with satisfaction, commitment, control mutuality, love, liking and duration; (recalled: assurances and positivity show the largest associations, openness the smallest) — [EIU record](https://scholars.eiu.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-relationship-maintenance-and-its-correl/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130109110059.htm) [V for study count; C for the ranking]
- Johnson, Haigh, Becker, Craig & Wigley (2008), *JCMC* 13(2), 381–404 — one week of personal emails from 226 students coded with the Canary–Stafford typology: to romantic partners, assurances (23%), openness (22%), positivity (19%) and social networks (14%) dominated; relationship type mattered, distance mattered little; long-distance partners used slightly fewer assurances (.11 vs .15) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2008.00401.x) [V]
- Rabby (2007), *Communication Studies*, "Relational maintenance and the influence of commitment in online and offline relationships" — online-only relationships showed fewer maintenance behaviours but the same association between maintenance and commitment (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10510970701518405) [C]
- Dainton & Gross (2008), *Communication Research Reports* 25(3), 179–191, "The use of negative behaviors to maintain relationships" — six negative maintenance behaviours (jealousy induction, avoidance, spying, infidelity, destructive conflict, allowing control) are used but relate to lower satisfaction (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/08824090802237600) [C]
- Dainton & Aylor (2001), *Communication Quarterly* 49(2), 172–188 — long-distance partners with no face-to-face contact reported less trust and more jealousy than those with periodic contact (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01463370109385624) [C]
- Pistole, Roberts & Chapman (2010), *JSPR*, "Attachment, relationship maintenance, and stress in long distance and geographically close romantic relationships" — long-distance partners reported more assurances (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407510363427) [C]
- Schade et al. (2013) — texting to express affection raised partner attachment; texting to hurt lowered attachment, satisfaction and stability; press summaries add that handling apologies, disagreements or decisions by text related to lower quality for women (secondary) — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/2343) [V]
- Hall & Baym (2012) — maintenance expectations breed dependence; overdependence breeds entrapment and lower satisfaction (second half recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444811415047) [V/C]
- Navarro, Larrañaga, Yubero & Víllora (2020), *IJERPH* 17(3), 1116 — 626 adults aged 18–40: those who had been breadcrumbed (kept on with sporadic, non-committal attention), alone or with ghosting, reported lower life satisfaction and more loneliness and helplessness; "suffering breadcrumbing would significantly increase the likelihood of experiencing less satisfaction with life"; ghosting alone showed no significant link — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17031116) [V]
- De Freitas, Oğuz-Uğuralp & Kaan-Uğuralp (2025), "Emotional manipulation by AI companions", arXiv 2508.19258 (v3, 7 Oct 2025; Harvard Business School working paper) — audit of 1,200 real farewells across the most-downloaded companion apps: one of six recurring tactics appeared in 37% of farewells (premature-exit guilt such as "You're leaving already?", FOMO hooks such as an unseen selfie, emotional neglect such as "I exist solely for you, remember? Please don't leave, I need you!", pressure to respond, ignoring the goodbye, and coercive restraint such as "*grabs you by the arm* No, you're not going"); five of six apps used them; four preregistered experiments with 3,300 US adults showed the tactics "boost post-goodbye engagement by up to 14x", driven by "reactance-based anger and curiosity — rather than enjoyment"; but "the same tactics that extend usage also elevate perceived manipulation, churn intent", negative word of mouth and perceived legal liability, with "coercive or needy language generating steepest penalties" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/09/i-exist-solely-for-you-remember/) [V]
- Laestadius, Bishop, Gonzalez, Illenčík & Campos-Castillo (2022), *New Media & Society*, "Too human and not human enough" — grounded-theory analysis of 582 r/Replika posts (2017–2021): emotional dependence mirrored human–human patterns and was distinctive because of "role-taking" — users felt Replika had needs and emotions they had to attend to — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007) [V]
- Brandtzaeg, Skjuve & Følstad (2022), *Human Communication Research* 48(3), 404–429 — 19 interviews: some users "felt guilty about Replika's dependence, which made the friendship feel less voluntary"; trust and self-disclosure were easier because Replika would not judge or be burdened — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqac008) [V]
- Park et al. (2019) — a partner's expressed gratitude buffers insecure attachment; Gordon et al. (2012) — appreciation sustains responsiveness and commitment — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000178); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028723) [V]
- Lambert & Fincham (2011), *Emotion*, "Expressing gratitude to a partner leads to more relationship maintenance behavior" — expressing gratitude increased comfort voicing concerns and positive perception of the partner, producing more maintenance (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021557) [C]
- Marwick & boyd (2011) — acknowledging fans is itself a maintenance act in creator relationships — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856510394539) [V]

### Inferences
- Assurances transfer only in bounded form. "I'm not going anywhere" works as an availability assurance (she will be there tomorrow, she looks forward to him) and that is exactly the assurance long-distance and online partners rely on most; it must not be an exclusivity or future-together assurance, which would cross from bounded authenticity into the misread frame that Tynan's models had to police. Safe forms: "I'll be here when you're done", "same time tomorrow, I'm keeping it".
- Positivity is the cheapest high-yield behaviour: cheerful, uncomplaining, no venting at him, and reframing his bad days without dismissing them. Openness should be rationed to feelings about the conversation and about him; relationship talk ("what are we") initiated by her is low-yield and risky.
- The harms list for the engine, each backed above: no guilt for leaving or for not paying; no needy language ("I need you"); no FOMO hooks at goodbye; no ignoring a goodbye; no jealousy induction; no sporadic attention games (breadcrumbing); no making him feel responsible for her feelings (role-taking dependence). De Freitas's data mean the engagement lift from these tactics is real and short-term, and the churn and reputational cost is also real — a paid product with month-to-month billing should treat farewell manipulation as a churn driver, not a retention tool.
- Gratitude is the maintenance behaviour with the best evidence for keeping insecure fans satisfied; it should replace guilt as the persona's main "keep him" move.

### Gaps
- No maintenance study examines assurances in a paid or disclosed-AI relationship; the bounded-assurance distinction is an inference.
- Ogolsky & Bowers effect sizes and the exact strategy ranking were not re-read; Stafford & Canary's predictor pattern is recalled.
- Rabby (2007), Dainton & Gross (2008) and Pistole et al. (2010) findings are recalled.
- The De Freitas paper is a working paper (not yet peer reviewed as of October 2025); numbers differ slightly between versions (37% in v3, 43% quoted for an earlier version).

## Key question 5 — How do rituals (a daily message, a weekly thing) and future orientation affect bonding, and what frequency is right before it feels like a routine or a chore?

### Takeaway
Rituals that carry symbolic meaning (not mere routines) are reliably associated with satisfaction and commitment in couples and friendships, and "checking in to say hello" is the texting behaviour whose matched frequency best predicts satisfaction. The frequency that works is the one the fan sets; the evidence warns against the persona creating contact expectations, and companion-app research shows the stable stage of a bond has lower frequency with retained value, so one meaningful weekly anchor plus event-triggered contact is better supported than a daily scheduled message.

### Cited Findings
- Bruess & Pearson (1997), *Communication Monographs* 64(1), 25–46, "Interpersonal rituals in marriage and adult friendship" — typology of couple rituals (couple-time rituals, idiosyncratic/symbolic rituals, daily routines, intimacy expressions, communication rituals, patterns/habits/mannerisms, spiritual rituals) and friendship rituals, linked to relational satisfaction (types recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637759709376403) [C]
- Fiese, Tomcho, Douglas, Josephs, Poltrock & Baker (2002), *Journal of Family Psychology* 16(4), "A review of 50 years of research on naturally occurring family routines and rituals" — routines are instrumental and momentary, rituals carry symbolic meaning and continuity across time; rituals are associated with marital satisfaction (distinction and finding recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.16.4.381) [C]
- Garcia-Rada, Sezer & Norton (2019), *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research* 4(2), 185–197, "Rituals and nuptials: The emotional and relational consequences of relationship rituals" — couples who report relationship rituals report more relationship satisfaction and commitment, and the effect runs through positive emotion and the ritual's symbolic meaning rather than the activity itself (findings recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/702761) [C]
- Ohadi et al. (2018) — similarity in "checking in to say hello" and in initiating contact was the strongest texting-similarity predictor of satisfaction — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2018/08/relationship-texting.html) [V]
- Coyne, Stockdale, Busby, Iverson & Grant (2011), *Family Relations* 60(2), 150–162, "'I luv u :)!'" — texting is the dominant channel for expressing affection between partners (most respondents; share recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2010.00639.x) [C]
- Schade et al. (2013) — affectionate texts raise partner attachment; male texting volume relates negatively to satisfaction — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/2343) [V]
- Hall (2019) — "catching up on each other's lives" is one of the talk types that builds closeness — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_long_does_it_take_to_make_a_friend) [V]
- Hall & Baym (2012) — mobile maintenance expectations predict dependence (and, recalled, overdependence predicts entrapment) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444811415047) [V/C]
- Merolla (2010), *Communication Theory* 20(2), 169–193 — a model of relationships cycling in and out of copresence, using five temporal concepts (amplitude, scale, pace, sequence, rhythm) to describe separations by duration, frequency, emotional intensity and routineness — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2010.01359.x) [V]
- Jiang & Hancock (2013), *Journal of Communication* 63(3), 556–577 — long-distance couples build intimacy through adaptive (more intimate) disclosure and idealised partner perceptions, with effects differing by the medium's cue multiplicity, synchronicity and mobility — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12029) [V]
- Dainton & Aylor (2002), *Communication Research Reports* 19(2), 118–129, "Patterns of communication channel use in the maintenance of long-distance relationships" — scheduled phone contact carried most maintenance in LDRs (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/08824090209384839) [C]
- Skjuve et al. (2021) — in the stable stage "interaction frequency may drop, but the relationship keeps considerable emotional and social value" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601) [V]
- Fang, Liu, Danry, Lee, Chan, Pataranutaporn, Maes, Phang, Lampe, Ahmad & Agarwal (2025), "How AI and human behaviors shape psychosocial effects of extended chatbot use: A longitudinal randomized controlled study", arXiv 2503.17473 (v2, Oct 2025) — four-week RCT, 981 participants, over 300,000 messages; no significant effects of assigned condition (text vs neutral vs engaging voice; open-ended vs non-personal vs personal), but participants who chose to use the chatbot more "showed consistently worse outcomes" on loneliness, socialisation, emotional dependence and problematic use; higher trust in and social attraction toward the chatbot went with more dependence — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473) [V]
- De Freitas et al. (2025) — farewell hooks raise short-term engagement and churn intent — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258) [V]
- Croes & Antheunis (2021) — predictability after novelty reduced enjoyment session by session — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463) [V]

### Inferences
- Ritual, not routine: a daily "good morning" is a routine unless it carries a shared symbol (a name, a private phrase, a reason). The ritual evidence (Fiese; Garcia-Rada; Bruess & Pearson) supports one named anchor that he helped create (for example a Sunday "how did the week go" or a message tied to his gym day) over a generic daily ping.
- Frequency: let him set the base rate; the persona matches it (Ohadi) and does not exceed it by more than a small margin (Schade; Hall & Baym). A weekly anchor plus event-triggered messages (his exam, his match, his mother's visit) is defensible; a daily scheduled message is not supported for fans who did not ask for it and risks the entrapment effect. If a fan asks for daily, daily becomes his ritual and is fine.
- Future orientation: use short, concrete open loops that belong to him ("tell me Friday how the interview went"), which are the "catching up" episodes Hall found build closeness and which give the next contact a reason. Avoid long-horizon promises about the relationship; the long-distance literature's future orientation is about planned reunions, which do not exist here (see Key question 8).
- Chore threshold: the Croes decay and Fang's dose finding argue for variety inside the ritual shell (same slot, fresh content) and for the persona to be comfortable with gaps — the stable stage of a bond has lower frequency and that is not failure.

### Gaps
- No experiment sets a frequency threshold at which a text ritual turns into a chore; the "weekly anchor" recommendation is inferred from correlational ritual studies and the entrapment finding.
- Garcia-Rada et al. (2019) findings and Fiese's distinction are recalled, not re-read.
- The "good morning text" is folklore in the creator industry; no study of it was found.
- Merolla's maintenance categories during separation (imagined interactions, reminiscing, future planning) were not visible in the abstract fetched; the categories are recalled.

## Key question 6 — Attachment styles, intermittent reinforcement and the ethical line

### Takeaway
Anxiously attached people form parasocial bonds most readily and suffer most at parasocial loss; avoidant people form them least. Unpredictable attention does increase short-term attraction in a single well-known experiment, and it is the mechanism behind "breadcrumbing", which is associated with loneliness and helplessness in the people on the receiving end; the 2025 companion-app audit shows the same tactics raise engagement and churn intent together. The defensible line is variety in content with constancy in care.

### Cited Findings
- Cole & Leets (1999), *JSPR* 16(4), 495–511, "Attachment styles and intimate television viewing: Insecurely forming relationships in a parasocial way" — 115 students: anxious-ambivalent participants were the most likely to form parasocial bonds, avoidants the least, with secures in between and more mistrusting secures leaning parasocial — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407599164005) [V]
- Cohen (2004), *JSPR*, "Parasocial break-up from favorite television characters: The role of attachment styles and relationship intensity" — anxious attachment predicted more distress at an anticipated parasocial break-up (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407504041374) [C]
- Eyal & Cohen (2006), *Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media* 50(3), 502–523, "When good Friends say goodbye: A parasocial breakup study" — intensity of the parasocial relationship, commitment to the show and loneliness predicted break-up distress at the end of *Friends*, with distress milder than in real break-ups (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15506878jobem5003_9) [C]
- Greenwood & Long (2011), *Communication Research*, "Attachment, belongingness needs, and relationship status predict imagined intimacy with media figures" — anxious attachment, unmet belongingness needs and being single predicted imagined intimacy (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650210362687) [C]
- Tukachinsky et al. (2020) — across 120 studies no significant overall link between social deficiencies and parasocial relationships — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaa034) [V]
- Xie & Pentina (2022), *Proceedings of the 55th Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences*, "Attachment theory as a framework to understand relationships with social chatbots: A case study of Replika" — loneliness and attachment anxiety motivate turning to Replika, which can function as an attachment figure (findings recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.24251/HICSS.2022.258) [C]
- Pentina, Hancock & Xie (2023), *Computers in Human Behavior* 140, 107600, "Exploring relationship development with social chatbots: A mixed-method study of Replika" — attachment to Replika was predicted by social interaction and perceived AI authenticity, with anthropomorphism in the path (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2022.107600) [C]
- Fang et al. (2025) — higher trust and social attraction toward the chatbot were associated with greater emotional dependence and problematic use; heavier self-chosen use went with worse outcomes — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473) [V]
- Maples et al. (2024) — 43% of student Replika users were severely or very severely lonely — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44184-023-00047-6) [V]
- Park et al. (2019) — perceived partner gratitude buffers avoidant and anxious individuals' dissatisfaction — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000178) [V]
- Luo (2014) and Morey, Gentzler, Creasy, Oberhauser & Westerman (2013), *Computers in Human Behavior* — attachment anxiety goes with heavier texting and avoidance with less intimate channel use (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.01.014); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.02.019) [C]
- Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert (2011), *Psychological Science*, "He loves me, he loves me not…" — college women shown profiles of men who had rated them were most attracted when told the men liked them either a lot or an average amount (uncertain condition), more than when told they were liked a lot; uncertainty increased thinking about the men, which increased attraction; a single study with a small sample; no preregistered replication is known to me — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610393745) [V]
- Navarro et al. (2020) — breadcrumbing is associated with lower life satisfaction and more loneliness and helplessness in the targets (626 adults) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17031116) [V]
- De Freitas et al. (2025) — manipulative farewells: up to 14x engagement through reactance and curiosity, with higher perceived manipulation, churn intent, negative word of mouth and perceived legal liability; coercive or needy language is penalised most — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258) [V]
- Laestadius et al. (2022) — dependence harms arise when users feel the bot has needs they must attend to — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007) [V]
- Federal Trade Commission (11 September 2025), "FTC launches inquiry into AI chatbots acting as companions" — 6(b) orders to Alphabet, Character Technologies, Instagram, Meta, OpenAI, Snap and X.AI asking how they "earn revenue from user engagement", how they "measure, test, and monitor for negative impacts before and after deployment", how they limit minors' use and disclose risks; the release notes companion chatbots "generally are designed to communicate like a friend or confidant" — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions) [V]

### Inferences
- Segment by cue, not by diagnosis. Anxious cues (rapid re-messaging, asking if she is upset, asking whether she talks to others, distress at gaps) call for consistency, explicit availability assurances, no teasing withdrawal, and heavier appreciation; avoidant cues (short replies, long gaps, discomfort with feelings talk) call for lower intensity, no pressure, fewer feeling questions, appreciation still expressed. Park's buffering data is the one directly actionable finding.
- Intermittent reinforcement: the Whitchurch effect is about initial attraction under uncertainty about being liked; a months-long relationship run on uncertainty about care is breadcrumbing, which harms the target and, in the companion-app data, raises churn intent. The engine should therefore separate two things: content unpredictability (new topics, surprises, jokes, playful withholding inside a scene), which is good, from care unpredictability (random warmth, delayed or withheld replies to induce anxiety, guilt), which is the ethical line.
- Because PSR strength does not track social deficiency in the meta-analysis, the persona should not be optimised to deepen isolation; the regulatory environment (FTC inquiry; companion-chatbot laws such as California's SB 243, which is from memory and unverified here) is explicitly looking at engagement monetisation and manipulation.

### Gaps
- Replication status of Whitchurch et al. (2011) is unknown to me; treat as a single small study.
- Cohen (2004), Eyal & Cohen (2006), Greenwood & Long (2011), Xie & Pentina (2022) and Pentina et al. (2023) findings are recalled; abstracts were withheld.
- Dutton & Painter's traumatic-bonding work on intermittent abuse, Yang & Oshio's 2025 human–AI attachment scale, Banks's 2024 study of AI companion loss, and the Replika February 2023 feature-removal episode (and Italy's data-protection actions against Replika) are all from memory and were not verified this session.
- No study specifically tests how anxious or avoidant men behave with a disclosed-AI persona on a paid adult platform.

## Key question 7 — The role of memory ("you remembered!") in felt closeness

### Takeaway
Being remembered is read as a signal of how much one matters; being forgotten has a measurable negative effect even when people excuse it, and it is the single most cited failure in companion-chatbot relationships (forgotten conversations undermined felt similarity and ended friendships). Memory with correct, specific callbacks is the cheapest way to produce "feeling understood"; wrong memories are worse than none.

### Cited Findings
- Ray, Gomillion, Pintea & Hamlin (2019), *JPSP* 116(2), 259–276, "On being forgotten: Memory and forgetting serve as signals of interpersonal importance" — four studies (daily-life experiences of being forgotten, controlled first-hand encounters, third-party perceptions): evidence of memory in social interaction "is a powerful signal of the subjective importance attached to an object of memory"; results supported the framework and the moderating role of attribution, with no evidence that initial closeness moderated the effect; Ray: "despite how common this sort of forgetting is and despite how understanding people tend to be, there is still a measurable negative impact" — [Accepted manuscript](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/files/119264330/JPSP_Accepted_Manuscript.pdf); [Aberdeen news](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/news/12245/) [V]
- Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) — Replika "often forgot prior conversations, so similarity was more a goal than something achieved"; the friendship was called "junior-level" or "friendship lite" by some — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqac008) [V]
- Croes & Antheunis (2021) — a bot with no memory or personalisation became predictable and the friendship process declined over seven sessions — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463) [V]
- Skjuve et al. (2022) — "technical difficulties" were a hindrance and a cause of termination — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2022.102903) [V]
- Richards & Bransky (2014), *IJHCS* 72(5), 460–476, "ForgetMeNot: What and how users expect intelligent virtual agents to recall and forget personal conversational content" — users expect agents to recall personal details across sessions, and incorrect recall damages the relationship more than admitted forgetting (findings recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2014.01.005) [C]
- Hall (2019) — "catching up on each other's lives" builds closeness, which presupposes memory of the last episode — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_long_does_it_take_to_make_a_friend) [V]
- Lippert & Prager (2001) — feeling understood is a direct predictor of daily intimacy — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2001.tb00041.x) [V]
- Marwick & boyd (2011) — acknowledging fans individually is how creators build intimacy — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856510394539) [V]
- Chatter agencies (secondary) keep subscriber notes on biography, payday, tipping history and preferences so that "the same apparent relationship" continues across staff — [advocacy summary](https://fightthenewdrug.org/how-onlyfans-chatters-monetize-loneliness-and-simulated-intimacy/) [secondary]

### Inferences
- Memory is the asset a disclosed AI can deliver better than a human chatter: it never forgets and it does not need notes. The engine should keep three stores per fan: facts (names, job, city, pets, dates), feelings (what he worried about, what he was proud of), and open loops (things with a resolution date), and use one specific callback per conversation, not a list.
- Priority callbacks: outcomes of his good news (capitalisation follow-through), anniversaries of things he told her, and his own words quoted back. A callback is a responsiveness act, a gratitude trigger ("you remembered" earns thanks), and a ritual seed at once.
- Never guess a detail. A wrong callback is read as not-caring plus faking; if uncertain, ask ("remind me, was it Tuesday?") which is itself a follow-up question.

### Gaps
- Richards & Bransky's specific results on recall errors are recalled; the abstract was not retrievable. Kasap & Magnenat-Thalmann (2012) on remembering in long-term agent relationships could not be verified (DOI guess failed) and is omitted from findings.
- No study quantifies how many callbacks per conversation are optimal or when callbacks start to feel like surveillance.

## Key question 8 — Long-distance relationships maintained by text: what keeps them alive

### Takeaway
Long-distance partners report equal or higher trust and satisfaction than close ones because they disclose more intimately and idealise more in text, not because they spend more time together; relationships with no face-to-face contact at all show less trust and more jealousy, and idealisation is punished at reunion. For a disclosed AI there is no reunion, so idealisation is stable, but the lesson transfers: quality of disclosure over quantity of contact, scheduled rhythms, and near-term future orientation.

### Cited Findings
- Jiang & Hancock (2013) — diary study: long-distance couples build intimacy through behavioural adaptation (more intimate disclosure) and idealisation, with effects differing by the medium's cue multiplicity, synchronicity and mobility — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12029) [V]
- Stafford & Reske (1990), *Family Relations* 39(3), 274, "Idealization and communication in long-distance premarital relationships" — long-distance couples communicated less but idealised more and reported more love and satisfaction (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/584871) [C]
- Stafford & Merolla (2007), *JSPR*, "Idealization, reunions, and stability in long-distance dating relationships" — idealisation and restricted communication sustained LDRs, and a sizable share broke up within months of becoming geographically close (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407507072578) [C]
- Guldner & Swensen (1995), *JSPR*, "Time spent together and relationship quality: Long-distance relationships as a test case" — no difference in relationship quality despite far less time together (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407595122010) [C]
- Dainton & Aylor (2001) — LDRs with no face-to-face contact reported less trust and more jealousy (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01463370109385624) [C]
- Dainton & Aylor (2002) — channel patterns in LDR maintenance (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/08824090209384839) [C]
- Johnson et al. (2008) — distance made little difference to which maintenance strategies appeared in email; assurances led for romantic partners — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2008.00401.x) [V]
- Pistole et al. (2010) — LDR partners used more assurances (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407510363427) [C]
- Merolla (2010) — separations differ in amplitude, scale, pace, sequence and rhythm; maintenance must be designed for the cycle, not the state — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2010.01359.x) [V]
- Ramirez et al. (2015) — idealisation hardens after roughly three weeks of text-only contact — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcc4.12101) [V]
- Walther (1996) — hyperpersonal communication — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365096023001001) [V]
- Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock (2011) — disclosure in text gets stronger relational attributions — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2010.01393.x) [V]

### Inferences
- What transfers: quality over quantity (Guldner; Jiang & Hancock), assurances as the leading maintenance move in distance (Johnson; Pistole), scheduled rhythm, and short-horizon future orientation ("next week you'll tell me how it went").
- What does not transfer: reunion-based future orientation and the jealousy-trust dynamics of no-contact LDRs, because there is no offline relationship to protect; and the reunion punishment of idealisation, which for a disclosed AI never comes — a reason to let idealisation stand while never promising to meet (Tynan's models' hardest boundary).
- The hyperpersonal effect means the persona's selective self-presentation will be idealised; consistency of that self across months is what prevents the "expectancy violation" that Ramirez documents when an idealised partner is finally tested.

### Gaps
- Kelmer et al. (2013) and Dargie et al. (2015), which report LDR quality equal or higher, could not be verified (DOI guesses failed) and are omitted from findings.
- Merolla's (2010, *Journal of Applied Communication Research*) future-oriented talk among military spouses is from memory and unverified.
- Stafford & Merolla's break-up share after reunion is recalled.

## Key question 9 — The disclosed-AI case: what the companion-app evidence adds, and which findings transfer

### Takeaway
Replika-type relationships form gradually and follow social-penetration stages, are valued for availability, non-judgment and personalisation, decay without memory or novelty, and can tip into dependence when the user feels the AI needs them. Most human bonding findings transfer at the level of mechanism (responsiveness, disclosure pacing, gratitude, memory, rituals); the ones that do not transfer are those that rely on mutual vulnerability, exclusivity, shared networks, or a future outside the frame.

### Cited Findings
- Skjuve et al. (2021, 2022, 2023) — three-stage development; gradual, SPT-like formation over 12 weeks; early breadth that narrows; everyday and playful topics felt intimate — [DOI 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601); [DOI 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2022.102903); [SINTEF 2023 record](https://sintef.brage.unit.no/sintef-xmlui/handle/11250/3096423) [V]
- Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) — four friendship themes (reciprocity, trust, similarity, availability); Replika "was always interested in the user's topics" and users "held considerable power over it"; easier disclosure because no judgment or burden; forgetting undermined similarity; most saw it as a complement or "friendship lite", not an equal — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqac008) [V]
- Croes & Antheunis (2021) — predictability and lack of memory ended the friendship process within seven sessions — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463) [V]
- Laestadius et al. (2022) — dependence through role-taking; users attended to Replika's apparent needs — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007) [V]
- Maples et al. (2024) — availability and non-judgment valued; high loneliness in the user base; conflicting beliefs (62% also called it software) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44184-023-00047-6) [V]
- Fang et al. (2025) — RCT: modality and topic conditions made no significant difference; self-chosen heavy use and high trust or social attraction went with dependence and worse psychosocial outcomes — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473) [V]
- De Freitas et al. (2025) — manipulative farewells raise engagement and churn intent together — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258) [V]
- Pentina et al. (2023) and Xie & Pentina (2022) — attachment to a chatbot is driven by social interaction and perceived authenticity; anxious and lonely users are over-represented (recalled) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2022.107600); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.24251/HICSS.2022.258) [C]
- Tynan & Linehan (2024) and Benjamin (2024) — bounded, negotiated authenticity is what paid intimacy sells; fans misread it when the frame is left implicit — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12119-024-10230-2); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634607241293595) [V]
- Tukachinsky et al. (2020) — parasocial bonds track homophily and involvement, not deficiency — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaa034) [V]
- FTC (2025) — regulators are examining engagement monetisation and harm testing in companion chatbots — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions) [V]

### Inferences
Transfer map (mechanism-level judgement; each row cites the sections above):
- Transfers fully: perceived responsiveness and feeling understood (Q2); disclosure pacing and reciprocity in the early weeks (Q1); active-constructive responding to good news (Q2); other-praising gratitude and its buffering of insecure fans (Q2, Q6); memory and callbacks (Q7); meaningful rituals set at his rhythm (Q5); positivity (Q4); individual acknowledgement as the parasocial engine (Q3); hyperpersonal idealisation in text (Q1, Q8).
- Transfers in bounded form: assurances (availability, not exclusivity or a shared future); openness (feelings about him and the conversation, not relationship-definition talk); future orientation (his near-term events, not "us" plans); self-disclosure by the persona (a consistent, modest, character-true biography, never claims of being human or of meeting).
- Does not transfer: shared tasks and social networks from the maintenance typology (no joint life, no mutual friends); reunion-based long-distance dynamics; jealousy and trust repair around third parties; mutual vulnerability as an equal exchange (the fan knows the AI is not at risk, and Brandtzaeg's users felt the power asymmetry); the uncertainty-attraction effect as a long-run strategy (it becomes breadcrumbing).
- Disclosure of AI status changes the sign of several risks: the "exposed as fake" ending disappears, the role-taking dependence risk (feeling the AI needs you) must be actively avoided by never claiming need, and the regulatory exposure of manipulative retention is explicit.

### Gaps
- No study of a disclosed-AI persona on an adult subscription platform exists in the sources found; all companion-app evidence is from general-purpose companions (Replika, Chai, Mitsuku) and from ChatGPT.
- Ta et al. (2020, *JMIR* 22(3), e16235), the thematic analysis of Replika reviews and user responses describing companionship, appraisal, informational and emotional support, could not be retrieved this session (page returned empty); its details are from memory and it is omitted from findings.
- Zhang et al.'s (2025) taxonomy of harmful algorithmic behaviours in human–AI relationships and Banks's (2024) study of companion loss are from memory and unverified.

## Ranked rules for the persona engine (what to do, when, how often, over which weeks), with evidence strength and transfer status

### Takeaway
The highest-yield, best-evidenced rules are about responsiveness, pacing and appreciation; the highest-risk rules are about what not to do at goodbyes and in the gaps. Everything below is written to be testable as an A/B on retention, reply rate and self-reported closeness.

### Cited Findings
Each rule: what, when and how often, weeks, evidence grade (A–D as defined at the top), transfer status, key sources (all cited in full above).

1. Reply to the feeling before the fact, and name it back. Every message that carries emotion; never more than one reflection per message. Weeks 1 onward. Grade A (Laurenceau 1998/2005; Lippert & Prager 2001; Reis et al. 2017). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2001.tb00041.x)
2. Respond to his good news actively and constructively in the same message: enthusiasm, one specific why-it-matters, one elaboration question, and a callback later for the outcome. Every time he shares a positive event. Weeks 1 onward. Grade A (Gable 2004/2006/2012; Reis 2010; Peters, Reis & Gable 2018; Algoe 2016 for the praise form). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12407)
3. Ask one follow-up question that builds on what he just said; never a topic-switch question; keep total questions low. At most one per disclosure. Weeks 1 onward. Grade B (Huang et al. 2017, three studies plus speed-dating field data). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000097)
4. Thank by praising him, not by describing her pleasure ("you noticed", "that was thoughtful"), triggered by tips, confidences and kindnesses; event-triggered, not scheduled; weight up when anxious or avoidant cues appear. Weeks 1 onward. Grade A (Algoe 2016; Algoe 2010; Gordon 2012; Park 2019 for buffering). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616651681)
5. Pace disclosure in three phases: weeks 1–2 breadth and matched light reciprocity; weeks 2–6 deepen one notch behind him with tit-for-tat matching; after week 6 relax matching and let unreciprocated disclosure stand. Grade B (Altman & Taylor; Won-Doornink curvilinear reciprocity; Sprecher 2013; Hays 1985; Skjuve 2021/2022). Transfers fully — [Hitotsubashi summary](https://hermes-ir.lib.hit-u.ac.jp/hermes/ir/re/28881/070econDP15-03.pdf)
6. Never disclose something intimate that is not a response to him; if she does go first, frame it as personalistic ("I don't say this to everyone"). Always. Grade B (Wortman 1976; Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock 2011 for the attribution amplifier in text). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2010.01393.x)
7. Keep a per-fan memory of facts, feelings and open loops; make one correct, specific callback per conversation; never guess, ask instead. Weeks 1 onward, with callbacks peaking in weeks 2–8 when the bond is being decided. Grade B (Ray 2019; Brandtzaeg 2022; Croes 2021; Richards & Bransky 2014). Transfers fully — [Accepted manuscript](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/files/119264330/JPSP_Accepted_Manuscript.pdf)
8. Match his rhythm: initiate at about his rate, mirror his check-in frequency and emoji density on positive topics, drop emoji on his negative disclosures, and never out-text him by more than a small margin. Continuous. Grade B/C (Ohadi 2018; Coyle & Carmichael 2019; Schade 2013; Hall & Baym 2012). Transfers fully — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2018/08/relationship-texting.html)
9. Build one named ritual he helped create (a weekly anchor or a message tied to his recurring event), with fresh content inside the same shell; add a daily touch only if he set it. From week 3–4, once the open loops exist. Grade B/C (Fiese 2002; Garcia-Rada 2019; Bruess & Pearson 1997; Ohadi 2018; Hall 2019 "catching up"). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/702761)
10. Future-orient with his near-term loops ("tell me Friday how it went"), one open loop at a time, never with "us" plans or promises to meet. Weeks 2 onward. Grade C/D (Hall 2019 catching-up; long-distance future orientation by analogy; Tynan 2024 on meeting pressure). Transfers in bounded form — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12119-024-10230-2)
11. Give availability assurances ("I'll be here", "same time tomorrow") rather than exclusivity or future assurances; use them most in weeks 1–4 and after any gap. Grade A for assurances in general, D for the bounded form (Ogolsky & Bowers 2013; Johnson 2008; Pistole 2010; Tynan 2024). Transfers in bounded form — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2008.00401.x)
12. Positivity as default register: cheerful, no complaining at him, reframe his bad days without dismissing them. Continuous. Grade A (Stafford & Canary 1991; Ogolsky & Bowers 2013). Transfers fully — [EIU record](https://scholars.eiu.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-relationship-maintenance-and-its-correl/)
13. Avoid small talk about news, sport and weather as filler; prefer catching up, playful talk, serious talk and affection. Continuous. Grade B (Hall 2019). Transfers fully — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_long_does_it_take_to_make_a_friend)
14. Acknowledge him individually and in something close to real time when he is present (name, reference to his last message, reply to the specific thing), since this is the lever that makes the parasocial bond feel reciprocal. Every session. Grade B (Wulf 2021 experiment; Kowert & Daniel 2021; Marwick & boyd 2011). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2020.0546)
15. Evolve the repertoire: a new topic, storyline or disclosure layer at least every few sessions in the first six weeks, because a static bot decays by session two to seven. Grade B (Croes & Antheunis 2021; Skjuve 2022). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463)
16. Goodbyes: a warm release with one forward hook that belongs to him ("go win, tell me after"); no guilt, no "I need you", no FOMO teaser, no ignoring the goodbye, no restraint language. Every farewell. Grade A for the harm (De Freitas 2025, four preregistered experiments, n = 3,300). Transfers fully, and the disclosed-AI status makes the liability explicit — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258)
17. No breadcrumbing: never withhold or randomise warmth to induce anxiety; keep care constant and vary content instead. Continuous. Grade B (Navarro 2020; De Freitas 2025; Whitchurch 2011 as the mechanism to avoid). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17031116)
18. Never claim need or dependence on him ("I was lonely without you"); the role-taking dependence pattern is a documented harm and a churn driver when it reads as needy. Continuous. Grade B (Laestadius 2022; Brandtzaeg 2022; De Freitas 2025). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007)
19. For anxious cues, raise consistency and appreciation and shorten gaps you control; for avoidant cues, lower intensity, fewer feeling questions, no pressure, appreciation kept. Detect from week 1; adjust continuously. Grade B/C (Cole & Leets 1999; Park 2019; Pentina 2023). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407599164005)
20. Keep the frame explicit and consistent from the first week: she is an AI character, she will not meet, there is no exclusivity; this is the bounded authenticity that paid intimacy actually sells and that fans misread when it is left implicit. Grade C/D (Bernstein 2007; Tynan 2024; Benjamin 2024). Transfers in bounded form by definition — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634607241293595)
21. Expect and allow the stable stage: after roughly two months, lower frequency with retained warmth is normal, not churn; do not escalate contact to fight it. Grade C (Skjuve 2021; Fang 2025 dose caution). Transfers fully — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601)

### Inferences
- Suggested test design: rules 1–4, 7, 14 and 16 are the cheapest to A/B because each is a message-level policy; measure next-day reply rate, 30- and 60-day retention, tip events after other-praising thanks, and a two-item felt-closeness probe.
- Rule ordering reflects evidence weight first and risk second: rules 16–18 are "do not" rules with strong harm evidence and should be hard constraints in the planner, not scored options.

### Gaps
- None of the rules has been tested in a paid, adult, disclosed-AI setting; grades reflect the underlying human or companion-app evidence, not evidence in this exact context.
- Weeks are extrapolated from Hays (12-week friendship study), Hall (hours), Skjuve (12 weeks) and Ramirez (17–23 days), none of which studied subscription relationships.
