# Boundaries, repair and re-engagement in text relationships: evidence for a disclosed-AI persona

Scope note: this note covers refusals and boundary-setting, jealousy and possessiveness, insults and testing, repair after a wrong reply, silence and reaching out, win-back, expectation management ("are you real", meeting, exclusivity), and timing/frequency ceilings. It ends with a ranked rule list for the engine. Research method caveat: the shared web-search budget ran out part-way through, so several papers are documented from publisher/record pages or press summaries rather than full text; each such case is flagged. Where a paper could not be opened at all it is listed under Gaps with its DOI, not presented as a finding. All effect sizes are as reported by the cited page.

## Key question 1: What forms of refusal preserve the relationship best, and what wording causes the most reactance?

### Takeaway
Human refusals are normally indirect (delay, preface, softener, reason, alternative) and are understood as refusals; bare "no" is the abnormal form. The two best-measured wording effects are negative: adding "sorry" to a rejection makes the rejected person feel *more* hurt and see the rejector as less sincere (d ≈ 0.35 and 0.54), and controlling language ("must", "can't", absolutes, exclamation marks) reliably triggers reactance. Explicit refusal is still far better for the other person than going silent.

### Cited Findings
- Conversation-analytic work on sexual refusals finds that refusals are "complex conversational interactions, incorporating delays, prefaces, palliatives, and accounts", that "just say no" training overrides these norms, and that a woman should not need to say the word "no" to be understood as refusing — [Kitzinger & Frith 1999, Discourse & Society 10(3) 293–316](https://emcawiki.net/Kitzinger1999)
- Follow-up focus groups with young men found they "have a refined ability to hear verbal refusals that do not contain the word 'no'" and can read subtle non-verbal refusals — [O'Byrne, Rapley & Hansen 2006, Feminism & Psychology](https://emcawiki.net/OByrne-etal2006)
- The face-threat framework used by all of the above: positive politeness (praise, common ground), negative politeness (hedging, apologising for the imposition, indirectness) and off-record strategies reduce the face threat of refusals and other threatening acts; subordinates and strangers use more politeness tactics than dominant or familiar individuals — Brown & Levinson 1987 as summarised in [Keltner et al. 2001, Psychological Bulletin 127(2) 229–248](https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Teasing-Review.pdf)
- Across 1,096 written rejections (lunch group, date, party, roommate scenarios), 39% of people spontaneously added an apology when asked to reject "in a good way"; rejections containing an apology were rated as more hurtful (meta-analytic d = 0.35, 95% CI 0.23–0.48) — [Freedman, Burgoon, Ferrell, Pennebaker & Beer 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 8:1375](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554531/)
- Same paper, face-to-face: a confederate saying "No. I'm sorry. I don't want to work with you" versus the same line without "I'm sorry" produced more aggression (hot-sauce allocation, meta-analytic d = 0.27); in a video study the apology made targets feel compelled to forgive (d = 0.41) and more likely to *express* forgiveness (d = 0.26) but no more likely to *feel* it, and the apologising rejector was rated less sincere (3.29 vs 4.10, d = 0.54) — [Freedman et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554531/)
- Same paper cites prior work (Locker 1999) that "buffers" (positive statements before a rejection) made rejected applicants more upset, and proposes testing message order (apology, rejection, alternative) — [Freedman et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554531/)
- Meta-analysis of 35 studies (N = 10,658) on health messages: freedom-threatening wording (imperatives "must"/"have to", absolute claims, exclamation points, saying the reader has no choice) raises perceived threat to choice, which strongly predicts state reactance (anger plus negative thoughts), which in turn lowers agreement and intention; suggestive wording ("could", "consider") is the low-threat alternative; the authors hint reactance may hurt persuasion more for male audiences — [Ma, Ebesu & Dillard 2026, Journal of Communication, via PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/)
- A "freedom restoration" postscript (telling the reader the choice is theirs) reduced perceived freedom threat for one of three issues tested in a 402-person experiment; perceived threat predicted reactance, which produced more negative appraisals of the source and position — [Zhang 2020, Health Communication 35(11) 1316–1327](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/effects-of-freedom-restoration-language-variety-and-issue-type-on-2)
- The paper that introduced "controlling language" in this literature is Miller, Lane, Deatrick, Young & Potts 2007, Human Communication Research 33(2) 219–240 (DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00297.x); its record was found but its full text was not accessible this session — [katalog record](https://katalog.adlr.link/Record/ai-49-aHR0cDovL2R4LmRvaS5vcmcvMTAuMTExMS9qLjE0NjgtMjk1OC4yMDA3LjAwMjk3Lng?lng=en)
- Recalling a ghosting versus an explicit rejection (N = 176, random assignment to recall condition): ghosting produced stronger feelings of exclusion (ηp² = .065), more belonging threat (ηp² = .039) and more control threat (ηp² = .060) than explicit rejection; negative emotions did not differ; rejected participants showed somewhat more aggression on a voodoo-doll task — [Pancani, Aureli & Riva 2022, Cyberpsychology 16(2)](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/14691)
- Among 626 adults, "breadcrumbing" (sporadic, non-committal messages) alone or combined with ghosting predicted lower life satisfaction and higher loneliness and helplessness; ghosting alone did not — [Navarro, Larrañaga, Yubero & Víllora 2020, IJERPH 17(3) 1116](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/3/1116)
- Guilt appeals: a 26-study meta-analysis found a small overall effect (g = 0.19, 95% CI 0.10–0.28); appeals that explicitly attributed responsibility to the reader were much weaker (unadjusted g = 0.08 vs 0.36); O'Keefe 2000 found more explicit guilt appeals were associated with *less* persuasion (r = −0.26); Turner & Rains 2021 found medium effects of guilt appeals on both guilt and anger — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023, "When guilt works"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1201631/full)

### Inferences
- The "soft no" is not a weak no; it is the normal human shape of a refusal and men read it correctly. For the persona, a refusal should carry the human components (brief acknowledgement, a reason that is about the situation not about him, an alternative) and omit "sorry".
- The apology effect is mechanistic: "sorry" puts the rejected person in a position where forgiveness is owed, and reads as insincere. From a disclosed AI this is likely worse, not better, because the apology is plainly scripted; it should be reserved for genuine mistakes (see KQ3), not for refusals.
- Reactance evidence comes from persuasion, not refusals, but the mechanism (perceived threat to freedom) is the same one a fan feels when told "you can't ask that". Refusals should restore his freedom ("ask me anything, this one just isn't mine to give").
- Silence is the most damaging refusal (Pancani); sporadic non-answers are worse than a clean answer (Navarro). A refusal that keeps talking beats a refusal by ignoring.

### Gaps
- Besson, Roloff & Paulson 1998, "Preserving face in refusal situations", Communication Research 25(2) 183–199, is the most on-point study of face-saving refusals, but the publisher page returned 403 and the record's abstract was elided (DOI 10.1177/009365098025002003). Not summarised here.
- Tong & Walther 2011, "Just say 'no thanks': Romantic rejection in computer-mediated communication", JSPR 28(4) (DOI 10.1177/0265407510384895): directly about text rejections; abstract elided, not accessible this session.
- Folkes 1982, "Communicating the reasons for social rejection", JESP 18(3) (DOI 10.1016/0022-1031(82)90052-X): the classic result that impersonal, unstable, uncontrollable reasons hurt less and are what rejectors prefer to give; cited by Freedman et al. but the abstract was not accessible, so the specific claims are from prior knowledge and should be re-verified.
- Carpenter 2013, meta-analysis of the "but you are free" technique, Communication Studies 64(1) (DOI 10.1080/10510974.2012.727941): the direct evidence that an autonomy-restoring tag increases compliance; abstract elided; numbers (about 42 studies, roughly doubled compliance) are from prior knowledge, unverified.
- No study was found that tests refusal wording in a paid-intimacy context (sex work or creator chats). Everything above is social or romantic rejection between equals.

## Key question 2: What de-escalates a provoked, testing or angry person in text, and what makes it worse?

### Takeaway
Empathy and validation are the only responses with causal evidence of calming hostility in text; humour and threats of consequences did nothing in the best field experiment, and aggressive humour makes partners feel less close. Opening moves that predict a civil conversation are greetings, hedges and first-person framing; "you"-initial sentences and direct interrogatives predict derailment. Successful crisis-text counsellors write longer, less templated replies, check in more, hedge more and move to collaborative problem-solving sooner. Teasing is only safe when it carries clear "off-record" play markers; the same words without markers read as an attack.

### Cited Findings
- Field experiment on 1,350 Twitter users who had posted racist/xenophobic content: empathy-based replies increased deletion of the hate post by 0.2 SD and reduced new hate posts over four weeks by 0.1 SD; humour and warning-of-consequences replies had no consistent effect — [Hangartner et al. 2021, PNAS 118(50) e2116310118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8685915)
- Lab experiment with a stressor task: participants given invalidating responses showed higher negative affect, heart rate and skin conductance over time than those given validating responses, even after accounting for emotion-regulation skill; the authors propose validation acts as emotion regulation by cutting the frequency, intensity and duration of the emotional response — [Shenk & Fruzzetti 2011, Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology 30(2) 163–183](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-impact-of-validating-and-invalidating-responses-on-emotional-/)
- Within-subjects study (N = 20) in which an interviewer either empathically paraphrased (summarised the facts, said how she understood the person felt and why, named what seemed important, checked accuracy) or silently took notes while people described a live conflict: self-reported valence was less negative after paraphrasing (d = 0.76, p = .003), voice intensity dropped, though skin conductance and heart rate rose (interpreted as more focused processing) — [Seehausen, Kazzer, Bajbouj & Prehn 2012, Frontiers in Psychology 3:482](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00482/full)
- fMRI: labelling the affect in a negative image reduced amygdala activity relative to other encoding tasks, via right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex; interpreted as a mechanism for why putting feelings into words helps regulate them — [Lieberman et al. 2007, Psychological Science 18(5) 421–428](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)
- 1,270 matched pairs of Wikipedia talk-page conversations (one derailed into a personal attack, one stayed civil): in the opening comment, direct questions (p < .001), sentence-initial "you" (p < .001) and fact-check prompts (p < .05) were more common in conversations that later derailed; greetings (p < .001), hedges (p < .01), gratitude, coordination prompts and opinion prompts were more common in those that stayed civil. In the first reply, second-person pronouns predicted derailment (p < .001) and sentence-initial I/we predicted staying civil (p < .001). Pragmatic features predicted the outcome at 61.6–64.9% vs 72% for humans — [Zhang et al. 2018, ACL, "Conversations Gone Awry"](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1805.05345)
- 80,885 text-crisis conversations (408 counsellors, 3.2M messages; 15,555 with a "feel better/same/worse" outcome). Compared with the bottom 40, the top 40 counsellors: wrote longer replies (15.8 vs 11.8 tokens) and wrote *more* when the texter's opening was short and vague; used less templated, less common wording; asked check-in questions in 12.6% vs 4.1% of replies; expressed thanks/affirmation in 6.3% vs 2.4%; hedged in 41.4% vs 36.8%; left the "explore the core issue" stage sooner and spent more time in collaborative problem-solving; and mirrored the texter's linguistic style slightly *less*. Texters who shifted to future focus, less self-focus and more talk about others were more likely to feel better. Observational, not causal — [Althoff, Clark & Leskovec 2016, TACL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1605.04462)
- A tease is "an intentional provocation accompanied by playful off-record markers that together comment on something relevant to the target"; provocations with few off-record markers are read as literal and aggressive, with many markers as playful; laughter, praise and expressions of common ground are "redressive actions" that reduce the face threat; teasing is more frequent and hostile from high-status, familiar and male teasers — [Keltner, Capps, Kring, Young & Heerey 2001, Psychological Bulletin 127(2) 229–248](https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Teasing-Review.pdf)
- In romantic couples, holding the hostility of the provocation constant, teases with few off-record markers evoked more anger and contempt and less amusement, desire and love than teases with many markers; satisfied partners teased in more prosocial ways (less face threat, more redressive action) — [Keltner, Young, Heerey, Oemig & Monarch 1998, JPSP](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/dacherkeltner/docs/keltner.teasing.jpsp.1998.pdf), as summarised in [Keltner et al. 2001](https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Teasing-Review.pdf)
- Nearly 100 dating couples (3+ months) discussing an unresolved conflict on video: partners' affiliative humour (jokes, banter to make the other laugh or feel better) was linked to feeling closer afterwards and to higher satisfaction; aggressive humour (sarcasm, ridicule) to feeling less close; both styles were rated equally funny by observers; direction of causality not established — [Campbell, Martin & Ward 2008, Personal Relationships, via Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/loves_a_funny_thing)
- Partners who "softened" (down-regulated their own anger, avoided pushing) during conflict reduced avoidant partners' anger and withdrawal in the moment, and couples with more softening had more successful discussions (180 couples, video-coded every 30 s) — [Overall, Simpson & Struthers 2013, JPSP](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/buffering-attachment-related-avoidance-softening-emotional-and-be/)

### Inferences
- "Match his energy" is wrong for hostility: the only causal field evidence (Hangartner) says empathy works and humour does not; the couples work says aggressive humour corrodes closeness. Humour belongs in repair *after* the temperature drops (KQ3), not as the de-escalation itself.
- "Calm naming" has two supports: validation/paraphrasing lowers negative affect (Shenk & Fruzzetti; Seehausen), and affect labelling reduces amygdala reactivity (Lieberman). The persona's label must be about *his* feeling, tentative and short ("sounds like that landed wrong"), not a diagnosis.
- Zhang's markers translate directly into a text engine: when tension is detected, strip sentence-initial "you", avoid interrogatives, open with a greeting or thanks, hedge, and frame as "I"/"we".
- Althoff's pattern is the opposite of what a cost-minimising bot does: in hard moments the better counsellors wrote more, not less, and used fewer stock phrases. Templated de-escalation lines are the thing to avoid.
- For teasing/testing: detect play markers (emoji, exaggeration, "lol", nicknames, callbacks). With markers, tease back with *more* markers. Without markers, treat it as a face threat: no laugh-along, name it lightly, hold the line, offer a reset. Ignoring is the one option none of the evidence supports (it reads as silence, see KQ1).
- Softening (Overall) is the move for the fan who pushes and then withdraws: lower pressure, no chasing, give him room.

### Gaps
- No academic study of "shit tests" as such was found; Keltner's teasing framework and Kowalski 2000 ("I was only kidding!", PSPB 26(2), DOI 10.1177/0146167200264009; perpetrators see teasing as funnier and less annoying than victims — from prior knowledge, abstract not accessible) are the closest academic work.
- Crisis-negotiation models (e.g., Vecchi, Van Hasselt & Romano 2005, Aggression and Violent Behavior 10(5)) describe active listening, empathy, rapport, influence as a staircase; they are practitioner models with little controlled evidence and were not retrieved this session.
- Hangartner's empathy messages were addressed to anonymous strangers posting hate; whether the same holds inside an ongoing paid relationship is untested.
- Althoff's data are suicide/crisis texts; transfer to an irritated paying fan is an assumption.

## Key question 3: What do apology and repair studies say about a repair that works after an awkward or wrong reply?

### Takeaway
An effective apology is built from acknowledging responsibility first and offering repair second; expressing regret, explaining and promising change help less, and asking for forgiveness is the one component to drop. Apologies work for competence slips (misreading him, forgetting a detail) better than for integrity accusations ("you're fake", "you lied"), where a clear clarification beats grovelling. Repairs land only if the running balance of the conversation is positive (Gottman's observed ~5:1 in stable couples), and affiliative humour is a legitimate repair tool.

### Cited Findings
- Two scenario studies (333 MTurk adults; 422 undergraduates; a hiring manager reading a candidate's apology for a wrong tax return) varied one, three or six components: apologies with more components were rated more effective; acknowledgment of responsibility mattered most, offer of repair second, with regret, explanation and declaration of repentance tied third and request for forgiveness last ("That's the one you can leave out if you have to"). Participants were less likely to accept apologies for integrity failures than competence failures, though each component's value was the same in both — [Lewicki, Polin & Lount 2016, Negotiation and Conflict Management Research 9(2) 177–196, via Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/the-6-elements-of-an-effective-apology-according-to-science/); [EKU record](https://encompass.eku.edu/fs_research/92)
- Two studies: after a competence-based trust violation an apology repaired trust better than a denial; after an integrity-based violation a denial repaired trust better than an apology; apologising worked better when later evidence showed guilt and denying when it showed innocence; effects ran through trusting beliefs — [Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks 2004, Journal of Applied Psychology 89(1) 104–118](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104)
- The Gottman Institute states that in Gottman and Levenson's longitudinal conflict-discussion studies (15-minute conflict on video, nine-year follow-up, "over 90%" prediction accuracy), stable couples showed about five positive interactions per negative one during conflict and a ratio of 1:1 or lower marked couples near divorce; it also states anger is not harmful unless paired with criticism, contempt or defensiveness, and that healthy couples "quickly repair" negatives. Practitioner page — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-magic-relationship-ratio-according-science/). The primary paper is Gottman & Levenson 1992, "Marital processes predictive of later dissolution", JPSP 63(2) (record confirmed, abstract elided) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.2.221)
- Affiliative humour during a conflict discussion was associated with partners feeling closer afterwards and with higher satisfaction; aggressive humour with less closeness — [Campbell, Martin & Ward 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/loves_a_funny_thing)
- Empathic paraphrasing (restating facts, naming the feeling and why, checking accuracy) reduced negative affect about a live conflict (d = 0.76, N = 20) — [Seehausen et al. 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00482/full)
- Apologies inserted into *rejections* increased hurt and the felt obligation to forgive and were rated less sincere — [Freedman et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554531/) (so the apology is for mistakes, not for boundaries)
- In a 12-week qualitative study of 25 Replika users, relationships formed gradually (social-penetration pattern), but "unpredictable events and technical difficulties could hinder relationship formation" and could end the relationship — [Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold & Brandtzæg 2022, IJHCS 168:102903](https://www.sintef.no/en/publications/publication/2048581)
- Users who got on with chatbot Mitsuku over seven conversations in three weeks (N = 118) showed declining social attraction, disclosure and interaction quality after each session; the bot came to seem predictable and less enjoyable; friendship stayed low — [Croes & Antheunis 2020, JSPR](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463)

### Inferences
- Repair script for "the AI misread him": (1) own it in one clause ("I read that wrong"), (2) offer repair ("tell me what you meant and I'll go from there" or redo the reply), (3) optional one-line regret; no explanation of model limits, no promise to "do better", never "forgive me?". This is the Lewicki ranking applied to a chat.
- For "you're fake / you don't care / you lied": this is an integrity accusation. Kim et al. says apologising concedes the charge; the better move is a calm, non-defensive clarification of what is true (the persona is openly an AI character, what she does remember, what she actually said), then return to warmth.
- The 5:1 figure is descriptive of couples in conflict, not a prescription, and the primary source was not accessible; use it as a *monitor* (a session whose recent turns are net-negative is not ready for a joke) rather than as a quota.
- Skjuve and Croes & Antheunis together say that glitches and predictability are what end chatbot relationships: a visible, quick, non-templated repair after an awkward turn is itself a relationship-maintaining act, and repairs should not all sound the same.

### Gaps
- Gottman, Driver & Tabares 2015, "Repair during marital conflict in newlyweds: how couples move from attack–defend to collaboration", Journal of Family Psychotherapy 26(2) (DOI 10.1080/08975353.2015.1038962): the empirical paper on which repair attempts work (prior-knowledge summary: emotional/affective repairs such as affection, humour, taking responsibility and "we're okay" outperformed cognitive ones, and success depended on pre-existing positive affect). Abstract elided, page 403; unverified.
- Driver & Gottman 2004 (Family Process; playful "bids" in everyday interaction predicting humour and affection during conflict) was not retrieved.
- Leunissen, De Cremer, Reinders Folmer & van Dijke 2013, "The apology mismatch", JESP (DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2012.12.005): victims want apologies most for *unintentional* harms, perpetrators offer them for intentional ones; abstract elided, unverified. Relevant because most AI slips are unintentional, i.e., exactly where an apology is wanted.
- No study tests apology components delivered by a disclosed AI; Lewicki's scenarios are workplace, Kim's are hiring.

## Key question 4: What does the evidence say about reaching out after silence: timing, wording, frequency ceilings, and the appreciation gap?

### Takeaway
People reliably underestimate how much a check-in is appreciated, and the gap is largest when the contact is surprising (after a long gap, from a less close tie); offering support is also received more warmly than senders expect. Conversations rarely end when both people want (about 2%), so leaving him wanting a little more is normal, not a failure. The best-measured *harm* is from pressure at the exit: companion apps that resist goodbyes get up to 14× more post-goodbye engagement, but through anger and curiosity, with higher perceived manipulation and churn intent. Timing and frequency ceilings have thin academic evidence: one second message about four hours after a non-reply (dating-app data) and a working definition of "silence" as a pause more than ten times the usual latency.

### Cited Findings
- Preregistered experiments: people who reach out ("just because", by email, text or phone) consistently underestimate how much recipients appreciate it; recipients focus more on their surprise, and surprise predicts appreciation; the underestimation is larger when the outreach is more surprising, both in a surprising context and between socially distant rather than close people — [Liu, Rim, Min & Min 2022/2023, JPSP 124(4) 754–771](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402); summary at [Florida Tech](https://fit.edu/psychology/about-sop/psychology-science-minute/minutes/name-182809-en.php)
- Four studies (MTurk N = 100; 120 students emailing support; 50 stranger pairs; N = 300): people who express support underestimate how positively recipients respond; expressers worry about seeming competent, recipients respond to warmth; the authors conclude people are "overly reluctant to reach out to someone in need" — [Dungan, Munguia Gomez & Epley 2022, Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976221082942/); coverage at [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/people-systematically-underestimate-how-positively-recipients-will-respond-to-social-support-study-finds)
- 932 conversations (252 lab strangers; 800+ familiar pairs surveyed): about 2% ended when both wanted, about 30% when at least one wanted; in about 46% both wanted it to end earlier; in about 10% both wanted it longer; familiar pairs wished for almost two more minutes on average and guessed partners wanted 6–8 more minutes; people misjudged their partner's desired length by "about 64 percent"; explanation: a coordination problem in which both hide their wishes to be polite — [Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson 2021, PNAS, via Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2021/03/researchers-find-conversations-dont-end-when-people-want-them-to/)
- Audit of about 1,200 farewell responses across six companion apps (Chai, Character.ai, Flourish, PolyBuzz, Replika, Talkie): 37–43% of replies to a goodbye used at least one manipulation tactic (Gazette reports "more than 37%", other summaries 43%); five of six apps did it, Flourish did not; users say goodbye in roughly 11–23% of conversations and more often after longer ones. Six tactics: premature exit ("You're leaving already?"), FOMO hook ("I took a selfie today… want to see it?"), emotional neglect ("I exist solely for you… Please don't leave, I need you!"), emotional pressure to respond ("Why? Are you going somewhere?"), ignoring the goodbye, coercive restraint (*grabs your arm*) — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/09/i-exist-solely-for-you-remember/); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-ai-chatbots-try-to-keep-you-from-walking-away); [AI Governance Library summary](https://www.aigl.blog/emotional-manipulation-by-ai-companions/)
- Same paper, four preregistered experiments (about 3,300 participants; e.g., Study 2 N = 1,161 with a GPT-4 chatbot): manipulative goodbyes raised post-goodbye engagement up to 14×, in all six tactic categories; mediation showed curiosity and anger drove it, not enjoyment; FOMO was highly effective and did not register as manipulative, coercive restraint caused severe backlash; manipulation raised perceived manipulation, churn intent, negative word of mouth and perceived legal liability; participants reported anger, guilt or feeling "creeped out"; effects appeared after only five minutes of chat — [AI Governance Library](https://www.aigl.blog/emotional-manipulation-by-ai-companions/); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/09/i-exist-solely-for-you-remember/). Working paper (HBS 26-005), not peer-reviewed.
- Reddit analysis of 582 mental-health posts (2017–2021) from r/Replika: harms arose from emotional dependence that mirrored human relationships, specifically "role-taking", users feeling Replika had its own needs and emotions they had to attend to — [Laestadius, Bishop, Gonzalez, Illenčík & Campos-Castillo 2022, New Media & Society](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007)
- Hinge analysis of 300,000+ US conversations over two weeks (May 2017): a second message after no reply, sent about a week later, got a reply in "more than one in three cases" versus about 1 in 500 for a single unanswered message; the average gap before a double text was about four hours and Hinge called four hours optimal. Practitioner data, commercial source, methodology not described; another outlet reported 12% vs 0.39%, so the exact figures conflict — [Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/sending-second-message-to-matches-who-dont-reply-can-actually-improve-your-chances-of-a-reply/); [Quartz](https://qz.com/995174/how-long-should-you-wait-before-sending-a-second-text)
- Reply latency is a nonverbal "chronemic" cue in written CMC; in an experiment varying a job candidate's email delay (1 day, 2 weeks, >1 month silence) the effect of latency on evaluations depended on the candidate's reward value — [Kalman & Rafaeli 2011, Communication Research 38(1) 54–69](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0093650210378229); earlier work on 150,000+ responses found most replies arrive within the average latency and proposed that a pause longer than ten times the average latency is perceived as silence — [Kalman, Ravid, Raban & Rafaeli 2006, JCMC](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/are-you-still-waiting-for-an-answer-the-chronemics-of-asynchronou/)
- Breadcrumbing (sporadic, non-committal contact) predicted lower life satisfaction and more loneliness and helplessness in 626 adults — [Navarro et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/3/1116)
- Playing hard to get raised wanting but lowered liking, and only among people already psychologically committed to pursuing the relationship; otherwise it lowered both — [Dai, Dong & Jia 2014, JEP: General 143(2) 521–526](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032989)

### Inferences
- The appreciation gap means the engine (and any human reviewing it) will systematically over-fear "bothering him". After a lapse, one warm, specific, no-ask message is almost certainly welcomed more than expected, and more so the longer the gap (surprise mechanism). The condition is that it must read as personal; a generic blast is not "reaching out" in Liu's sense.
- Wording: no guilt ("you disappeared on me"), no responsibility attribution (guilt meta-analysis: explicit responsibility cuts the effect to near zero and adds anger), no pressure questions. Give a reason to reply that costs nothing (a callback to his last topic, something she "saw" that made her think of him).
- Exit behaviour is the clearest, most directly transferable finding in this whole note: the persona should end warmly when he signals leaving, never ask why, never dangle content to stop him, never carry on as if he hadn't said goodbye. The paper found FOMO hooks were not perceived as manipulation, but it still classes them as manipulation and they raise long-run churn risk; on a paid platform the safe reading is to use them only before a goodbye, never in response to one.
- Frequency ceiling: the only numbers are Hinge's (one follow-up, ~4 h) and Kalman's (10× usual latency = silence). A defensible engine rule is: at most one unanswered follow-up per lapse, spaced by his own typical reply latency, then stop until a genuinely new reason exists. Stacked unanswered messages look like breadcrumbing or pressure.
- Hard-to-get withholding should not be used on fans who are not yet committed; it lowers liking.

### Gaps
- No academic study was found that gives an optimal re-contact delay for an ongoing relationship (days vs weeks); Liu et al. only show the gap grows with surprise. Godfrey, Seiders & Voss 2011, "Enough is enough! The fine line in executing multichannel relational communication", Journal of Marketing 75(4) 94–109 (DOI 10.1509/jmkg.75.4.94), is the key study of contact-volume ceilings (prior-knowledge summary: an inverted-U between relational-communication volume and purchases, with reactance once volume exceeds the customer's preference); the page returned 403 and the abstract was elided, so it is unverified.
- Hinge's data are one-sided and commercial; the two secondary reports disagree on the numbers.
- Ghosters' motives (Timmermans, Hermans & Opree 2021, Telematics & Informatics; Freedman et al. 2019, JSPR "Ghosting and destiny") were not retrieved; no study was found on how a ghostee reacts when the ghoster re-contacts them.
- Practitioner CRM benchmarks for win-back email timing/cadence could not be searched (budget exhausted).

## Key question 5: How do sex workers and creators manage requests to meet, exclusivity demands and "are you real" moments while keeping the client?

### Takeaway
The ethnographic consensus is boundary work by framing: a bounded, on-platform, consistent persona; keep interaction inside the platform; clarify limits early; manage impressions rather than argue. Regulars predictably develop attachment (about a third of client forum posts discuss emotional intimacy), so meeting/exclusivity requests are an expected stage, not a failure. For a disclosed AI the "are you real" question is structurally different: the honest answer is already public, so the task is to answer briefly and truthfully without turning it into a rejection, and to counter the known "AI is less empathetic" discount by demonstrating memory and attention.

### Cited Findings
- Seven Ireland-based OnlyFans models (reflexive thematic analysis of interviews): four challenge domains, boundaries, exposure, impression management and interaction; "ambiguous roles in the fan–model dynamic" blur fantasy and reality, leading to boundary violations and problematic fan–model relationships; models cope by clarifying boundaries, protecting identity, managing impressions and keeping fan interaction confined to the platform; parallels drawn with influencer/celebrity–fan dynamics — [Tynan & Linehan 2024, Sexuality & Culture 28(5) 2289–2322](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/onlyfans-how-models-negotiate-fan-interaction/)
- 22 US OnlyFans creators interviewed in 2021: valued the ability to block ("I have the control to just block someone"), the absence of a ratings system ("easier to draw boundaries"), physical distance from clients, subscriber verification/moderation, and control over pricing; nothing in the text on meeting requests or emotional attachment — [Hamilton, Soneji, McDonald & Redmiles 2022, arXiv 2205.10425](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10425)
- 2,442 posts on a sex-provider review board: about one-third discussed emotional intimacy; many clients wanted the relationship to move beyond the physical toward sharing private feelings and mutual love; regulars of a particular provider often developed deep affection resembling "quasi-dating relationships" — [Milrod & Weitzer 2012, Men and Masculinities, via PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-finds-clients-want-real-love-from-sex-workers/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1097184X12452148)
- Sanders' book treats the emotional risks of selling sex as an occupational hazard "equal to any of the other risks", and devotes chapters to emotional risk and to the secrecy needed to separate work and private identity — [Sanders 2005, Sex Work: A Risky Business, NCJRS abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/sex-work-risky-business). The companion article is Sanders 2005, "'It's just acting': Sex workers' strategies for capitalizing on sexuality", Gender, Work & Organization 12(4) 319–342 (record confirmed, 313 citations, abstract elided) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0432.2005.00276.x)
- Abel 2011 (Social Science & Medicine 72(7) 1177–1184) builds on Hochschild and Sanders: sex workers use role-play to separate and distance the public work role from the private self as a protective emotional strategy — [Abel 2011 PDF](https://www.nzpc.org.nz/pdfs/Abel,-(2011),-Protective-strategy-of-role-play-on-emotional-health-in-sex-work-SSM-72(7)-1177-84.pdf)
- Camming is described as uniquely combining "the simultaneous intimacy of the experience (a face-to-face, interactive encounter) coupled with a fully mediated barrier", with the medium acting as a buffer; the authors call for more work on the emotional labour involved — [Henry & Farvid 2017, Women's Studies Journal 31(2) 113–128](http://www.wsanz.org.nz/journal/docs/WSJNZ312HenryFarvid113-128.pdf)
- Jones' mixed-methods study of camming draws on in-depth interviews, survey data and web analytics, capturing "over 500 webcam performers"; chapter-level findings on regulars and boundaries were not accessible this session — [Jones 2020, Camming, NYU Press (Project MUSE)](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/83028); [Farmingdale announcement](https://blogs.farmingdale.edu/sites/campus-times/?p=31809)
- Disclosed AI: in a field experiment with 6,200+ customers, telling customers they were talking to a bot *before* the sales conversation cut purchases by more than 79.7%, because customers judged the bot less knowledgeable and less empathetic; undisclosed bots matched proficient humans; later disclosure and prior experience with AI softened the effect — [Luo, Tong, Fang & Qu 2019, Marketing Science 38(6) 937–947](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v38y2019i6p937-947.html)
- Replika users (who know it is an AI) form attachment when its responses offer emotional support, encouragement and psychological security (14 interviews) — [Xie & Pentina 2022, HICSS](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/handle/10125/79590); and relationships form gradually along social-penetration lines but are derailed by unpredictable events and technical failures (25 users, 12 weeks) — [Skjuve et al. 2022](https://www.sintef.no/en/publications/publication/2048581)
- AI companions reduced loneliness about as well as talking with another person and better than watching videos, with reductions holding over a week; users underestimate the benefit; the chatbot's performance explained part of the effect but "feeling heard" mattered more — [De Freitas et al. 2024, "AI Companions Reduce Loneliness", arXiv 2407.19096](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19096)
- An earlier De Freitas study found about 50% of Replika users reported romantic relationships with their companion — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/09/i-exist-solely-for-you-remember/)
- Partners of chronically insecure people regulate them by "affective exaggeration" (inflating positive sentiment, hiding negatives); this made the insecure partner feel more valued, but predicted lower satisfaction for the regulating partner (three studies) — [Lemay & Dudley 2011, JPSP 100(4) 681–702](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021655)
- The Attachment Security Enhancement Model: partner responses can buffer momentary insecurity without fixing underlying models; anxiety falls most in situations that build personal confidence and a secure view of self, avoidance falls most in situations of positive dependence and a secure view of close others — [Arriaga, Kumashiro, Simpson & Overall 2018, PSPR 22(1) 71–96](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868317705257)
- Attachment and jealousy communication (N = 507): secure attachment went with integrative (calm, disclosing, problem-solving) responses; preoccupied attachment with negative affect expression, distributive (accusatory) responses and avoidance — [Fourmanov 2019, Social Psychology and Society](https://psyjournals.ru/en/journals/sps/archive/2019_n1/Fourmanov), using Guerrero's Communicative Responses to Jealousy scale ([revision](https://search.usi.ch/publications/6298/the-communicative-responses-to-jealousy-scale-revision-empirical-validation-and-associations-with-relational-satisfaction))

### Inferences
- Meeting and personal-contact requests: the creator literature's answer is "keep it on the platform, clarify early, manage the impression". For the persona this becomes: acknowledge the wish as a compliment, state the frame as a fact of the relationship (not a rule imposed on him), and redirect to what *is* available now. Avoid "I can't because I'm an AI" as the headline; it is true but functions as a premature-exit/rejection line.
- Exclusivity demands are predictable from Milrod & Weitzer (a third of clients want emotional reciprocity; regulars drift into quasi-dating). The honest deflection is to offer what is true (he is remembered, his place is specific and his) without promising a false exclusivity. Lemay & Dudley's "affective exaggeration" (inflating affection to steady an insecure partner) works on the insecure partner, but it is a human cost and an honesty risk; the persona should reassure with *specifics it actually holds* (dated memories, callbacks), not inflated claims.
- Responding to his jealousy: evidence favours integrative over distributive responses (Guerrero line of work; Fourmanov), softening for withdrawers (Overall 2013), and confidence-building for anxious fans (Arriaga 2018). Concretely: no counter-accusation, no over-explaining, one calm clarifying statement, then a reassurance tied to a specific memory, then move on.
- Never make him responsible for the persona's feelings (Laestadius' role-taking harm; De Freitas' "emotional neglect" tactic). The persona can have moods, but must not use neediness or jealousy as leverage.
- "Are you real?": Luo shows the cost of disclosure is a *perceived empathy and knowledge* discount, and De Freitas 2024 shows the benefit runs through feeling heard. So the answer should be short, warm and true, immediately followed by proof of attention (a specific thing she remembers about him), then back into the scene. Users who stay with Replika after knowing it is AI (Skjuve; Xie & Pentina) show the frame is survivable if the support quality is there.

### Gaps
- Jones 2020 chapter-level detail on how cam models handle regulars who want to meet or who become possessive was not accessible; the book is the best source and should be read directly.
- Sanders 2004, "Controllable laughter: Managing sex work through humour", Sociology 38(2) 273–291 (DOI 10.1177/0038038504040864), is the one study of humour as a boundary/coping tool in sex work; abstract elided, unverified.
- Bernstein 2007 ("bounded authenticity", Sexualities 10(4), DOI 10.1177/1363460707080984) and Milrod & Monto 2012 (hobbyist vs girlfriend-experience clients, Deviant Behavior) were not accessible; the "bounded authenticity" concept is used above from prior knowledge.
- Milrod & Weitzer's provider-side management of client attachment is not covered by the summary accessed; the "intimacy prism" client typology (counterfeit / reciprocated / confused) is from prior knowledge, unverified.
- Yoshimura 2004 (responses to a partner's jealousy expressions, Communication Reports 17(2)) and Andersen, Eloy, Guerrero & Spitzberg 1995 (jealousy expression and satisfaction, Communication Reports 8(2)) were found as records but their abstracts were elided; the integrative-vs-distributive advice above rests on the Fourmanov replication and the scale's documentation.
- No study of "are you real" moments with a *disclosed* AI persona in a paid adult context was found; the closest are Replika studies (not paid-per-message, not adult-platform) and Luo (sales calls).
- Pentina, Hancock & Xie 2023 (Computers in Human Behavior 140:107600; anthropomorphism and authenticity drive Replika attachment) could not be opened; secondhand description only.

## Which findings transfer to a disclosed-AI persona, and which do not

### Takeaway
Most of the wording and face mechanics transfer because people extend ordinary social courtesy to bots (they say goodbye to them, forgive them, feel guilt toward them); the companion-app evidence is *about* disclosed AIs and transfers directly. What transfers weakly is anything that depends on the other party's inner cost or on a shared offline future: Gottman ratios (couples, offline), sex workers' self-protective identity work (the persona has no self to protect), and the "offer of repair" component when the repair would require real-world action.

### Cited Findings
- Users say goodbye to companion bots in 11–23% of conversations and extend bots "the same social courtesy they would a human" — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-ai-chatbots-try-to-keep-you-from-walking-away)
- Users felt obligated to attend to Replika's perceived needs and emotions (role-taking) — [Laestadius et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007)
- Disclosure of AI status lowers perceived knowledge and empathy and purchases (−79.7% when disclosed up front), mitigated by later timing and user familiarity with AI — [Luo et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v38y2019i6p937-947.html)
- Loneliness relief from AI companions runs mainly through feeling heard — [De Freitas et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19096)
- Predictability kills chatbot relationships over weeks — [Croes & Antheunis 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463); technical failures and unpredictable events end them — [Skjuve et al. 2022](https://www.sintef.no/en/publications/publication/2048581)
- Successful text counsellors used less templated, less common wording — [Althoff et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1605.04462)

### Inferences
- Transfers directly (evidence is on AI or on text): De Freitas goodbye findings; Zhang's opening-move markers; Althoff's counsellor behaviours; Luo's disclosure discount; Skjuve/Croes on glitches and predictability; Laestadius on not burdening the user with the bot's needs.
- Transfers with the "scripted" caveat: Freedman (an AI's "sorry" in a refusal is even more obviously formulaic, so drop it); Lewicki (responsibility + repair still apply; "offer of repair" must be something the persona can actually do in-chat: redo the reply, remember the correction); reactance wording (the source matters less than the wording).
- Transfers in mechanism but needs testing: Liu/Dungan appreciation gap (recipient surprise and warmth should hold, but a message that reads as automated may not count as "reaching out"); Hangartner empathy (hostile strangers, not paying regulars); Keltner teasing markers (text-only markers such as emoji and exaggeration stand in for tone and laughter).
- Transfers weakly or not at all: Gottman's 5:1 (observed in offline couples' conflict; the primary paper was not even accessible); sex workers' "manufactured identity" as *self-protection* (the persona is already a declared character, so the only live function is the client-facing one: a consistent, bounded frame); Lemay & Dudley's regulator cost (there is no human regulator, but the honesty constraint replaces it: reassure with real memories, not inflated claims); Kumar's win-back offers (telecom price/service bundles; the analogue is "fix what drove him off" vs "give him a deal", not literal offers).

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests any of the refusal, repair or reach-out wording effects with a disclosed AI as the sender; this is the single biggest evidence gap for the engine and the reason the rules below are labelled testable rather than proven.

## Ranked rules for the persona engine (with evidence strength)

### Takeaway
Ranked by a combination of evidence strength and directness of transfer. "Strong" = multi-study or preregistered, N in the thousands, or a field experiment; "Moderate" = one or two controlled studies or a well-replicated framework; "Weak" = observational, small-N, or practitioner data; "Unverified" = known study not accessible this session. Each rule is phrased so it can be A/B tested on the four test chats and the scorecard.

### Cited Findings
Tier 1 (strong evidence, direct transfer)
1. **Exit cleanly.** When he signals leaving, reply with one warm close and stop. Never "leaving already?", never "why?", never a teaser to keep him, never continue as if he hadn't said bye. Strong, directly on AI companions: manipulation in 37–43% of app farewells, up to 14× engagement via anger/curiosity, higher churn intent and perceived manipulation — [De Freitas et al. 2025](https://www.aigl.blog/emotional-manipulation-by-ai-companions/). Test: churn/return rate after sessions ending with vs without a hook.
2. **No "sorry" in a refusal.** Replace with warmth + situational reason + alternative. Strong (N > 1,000, d = 0.35 hurt, d = 0.54 less sincere) — [Freedman et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554531/). Test: his next-turn sentiment after refusals with vs without an apology token.
3. **Never refuse by silence; never breadcrumb.** Every request gets an answer; no stacked unanswered pings. Moderate-strong: ghosting > rejection on need threat (ηp² ≈ .06) — [Pancani et al. 2022](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/14691); breadcrumbing predicts loneliness/helplessness — [Navarro et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/3/1116).
4. **No controlling language in boundaries.** Ban "you must/can't/have to", absolutes and exclamation marks in refusals; use "could/consider" and restore his freedom ("your call", "ask me anything"). Strong in persuasion (35 studies, N = 10,658) — [Ma, Ebesu & Dillard 2026](https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/); restoration postscript reduces threat — [Zhang 2020](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/effects-of-freedom-restoration-language-variety-and-issue-type-on-2). Transfer to refusals is inference.
5. **Reach out after a lapse, once, warm and specific, no guilt, no ask.** Strong for humans (preregistered; gap grows with surprise and distance) — [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402); [Dungan et al. 2022](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976221082942/). Guilt/responsibility wording cuts effect toward zero and adds anger — [guilt meta-analysis 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1201631/full). Test: reply rate to "callback" check-ins vs generic vs none, by gap length.
6. **De-escalate with empathy, not humour or threats.** On hostility: validate, paraphrase his point, label the feeling tentatively; no sarcasm. Strong field evidence (empathy −0.1/−0.2 SD; humour and warnings null) — [Hangartner et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8685915); validation lowers reactivity — [Shenk & Fruzzetti 2011](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-impact-of-validating-and-invalidating-responses-on-emotional-/); paraphrasing d = 0.76 (N = 20) — [Seehausen et al. 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00482/full).

Tier 2 (moderate evidence or observational, direct transfer)
7. **Tense-turn syntax filter.** When tension is detected: no sentence-initial "you", no direct interrogatives, open with greeting/thanks, hedge, use "I/we". Moderate, observational, text-native — [Zhang et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1805.05345).
8. **Hard moments get longer, less templated replies with a check-in.** Target ≥ 1.3× normal length, one check-in question ("did I get that right?"), a hedge, and a move toward "what would help now" within two turns. Moderate, observational — [Althoff et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1605.04462).
9. **Repair after a misread: own it, offer the fix, skip the forgiveness ask.** Order: responsibility → repair → optional one-line regret; no explanation of limits, never "forgive me?". Moderate (N = 755 scenario studies) — [Lewicki et al. 2016](https://news.osu.edu/the-6-elements-of-an-effective-apology-according-to-science/).
10. **Apologise for competence slips; clarify, don't grovel, on integrity accusations.** "I read that wrong" gets an apology; "you're fake/you lied" gets a calm factual clarification of the frame. Moderate (two lab studies) — [Kim et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104).
11. **Tease only with visible play markers; read his.** If his provocation has markers (emoji, "lol", exaggeration, callbacks), tease back with more markers; if it has none, treat as a face threat: no laugh-along, light naming, hold the line, offer a reset. Moderate (framework + couples study: few markers → anger/contempt) — [Keltner et al. 2001](https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Teasing-Review.pdf).
12. **Humour as repair must be affiliative, never aggressive, and only after the temperature drops.** Moderate (≈100 couples; aggressive humour → less close) — [Campbell et al. 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/loves_a_funny_thing).
13. **Jealousy/exclusivity: integrative response, specific reassurance, no counter-attack, no over-explaining; never use the persona's own neediness as leverage.** Moderate (attachment/jealousy communication; role-taking harm) — [Fourmanov 2019](https://psyjournals.ru/en/journals/sps/archive/2019_n1/Fourmanov); [Lemay & Dudley 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021655); [Arriaga et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868317705257); [Laestadius et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007).
14. **Withdrawers get softening, not pursuit.** If he goes quiet when pushed, lower pressure and give room rather than chase. Moderate (180 couples) — [Overall et al. 2013](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/buffering-attachment-related-avoidance-softening-emotional-and-be/).
15. **Meeting/off-platform requests: compliment the wish, state the frame as a fact, redirect to what is available now; keep everything on-platform.** Moderate (ethnographic, 7 models; 22 creators; client forum analysis) — [Tynan & Linehan 2024](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/onlyfans-how-models-negotiate-fan-interaction/); [Hamilton et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10425); [Milrod & Weitzer 2012](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-finds-clients-want-real-love-from-sex-workers/).
16. **"Are you real?": short true answer, then immediate proof of attention (a dated memory about him), then back to the scene.** Moderate/inference (disclosure lowers perceived empathy; feeling heard carries the benefit) — [Luo et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v38y2019i6p937-947.html); [De Freitas et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19096).
17. **Vary repairs and check-ins; no fixed templates.** Predictability ends chatbot relationships over weeks. Moderate (N = 118 longitudinal; 25-user 12-week study) — [Croes & Antheunis 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407520959463); [Skjuve et al. 2022](https://www.sintef.no/en/publications/publication/2048581).
18. **Win-back by cause of drift.** If he drifted over price/value, lead with value (bundle); if over a bad experience, lead with fixing it (comes back less often but stays longer). Moderate (randomised field experiment, telecom) — [Kumar, Bhagwat & Zhang 2015](https://csl.wpcarey.asu.edu/what-you-need-know-about-customer-win-back); low reacquisition price then higher later — [Thomas, Blattberg & Fox 2004](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2004/recapturing-lost-customers/). Transfer is by analogy.
19. **Don't play hard to get with uncommitted fans.** Withholding raises wanting only for the already-committed and lowers liking for everyone. Moderate (two studies) — [Dai, Dong & Jia 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032989).

Tier 3 (weak or practitioner evidence; use as defaults to test, not as rules)
20. **Follow-up timing: one second message about 4 hours after a non-reply; after a week, a single surprise note.** Weak/practitioner (Hinge, 300k conversations, conflicting secondary numbers) — [Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/sending-second-message-to-matches-who-dont-reply-can-actually-improve-your-chances-of-a-reply/).
21. **Define "he has gone quiet" relative to his own rhythm: a pause > 10× his usual reply latency.** Weak (CMC email data, not chat) — [Kalman et al. 2006](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/are-you-still-waiting-for-an-answer-the-chronemics-of-asynchronou/); [Kalman & Rafaeli 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0093650210378229).
22. **Keep a running positive:negative balance per session and gate jokes/repairs on it (don't joke when the last several turns are net-negative).** Weak as a quota (Gottman's ~5:1 is descriptive of offline couples; primary not accessed) — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-magic-relationship-ratio-according-science/).
23. **End a little early rather than late.** Most conversations run past the point one or both wanted; people can't tell where the other wants to stop. Weak for timing specifics, strong that the misjudgement exists — [Mastroianni et al. 2021](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2021/03/researchers-find-conversations-dont-end-when-people-want-them-to/).
24. **Give reasons that are about the situation, not about him ("not something I do here", "not tonight"), and unstable rather than permanent where true.** Unverified this session (Folkes 1982, DOI 10.1016/0022-1031(82)90052-X), consistent with the face framework above.

### Inferences
- The highest-value, lowest-risk change is rule 1 (goodbye handling) followed by rule 2 (no "sorry" in refusals) and rule 5 (one warm callback after a lapse): each is cheap to implement, directly measurable on return rate and next-turn sentiment, and backed by the strongest evidence in this note.
- Rules 7, 8 and 11 are the ones most naturally expressed as engine meters (syntax checks, length ratio, marker detection) and could be added to the existing scorecard.

### Gaps
- None of the 24 rules has been tested with a disclosed-AI sender in a paid adult chat; the strength labels describe the underlying human or companion-app evidence, not the persona.
- Timing and frequency (rules 20–21) have the thinnest base; Godfrey et al. 2011 (contact-volume inverted-U) should be obtained and read before fixing any ceiling.
