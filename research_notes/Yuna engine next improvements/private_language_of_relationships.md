# The private language of a two-person relationship in text: evidence for a disclosed-AI persona

Scope note. These are research notes (not a finished report) on relational culture as it shows in texting: private words and nicknames, inside jokes and callbacks, humour and self-deprecation, imperfection, reply timing, emoji and punctuation, style matching, and how these develop over time. The aim is rules for an openly-AI female persona who texts the same paying male fans for months on an adult subscription platform. Every finding carries a source link; where I could only confirm a citation but not its findings, I say so. Several primary sources sat behind paywalls (Taylor & Francis, Wiley, SAGE returned 403), so some findings are reported from abstracts, repository pages or press releases, and the evidence strength is marked accordingly. The web-search budget for this session ran out before the last gaps could be closed; they are listed plainly under Gaps.

Evidence-strength labels used below: **Strong** = meta-analysis or multi-study/preregistered work; **Moderate** = one or two peer-reviewed studies with reasonable samples; **Weak** = single small study, student thesis, company data or press summary; **Inferential** = no direct study, reasoned from adjacent evidence.

## Key question 1: Which private-language and humour features have measured links to closeness or satisfaction, and how big are the effects?

### Takeaway
The humour a couple *creates and shares* (inside jokes, shared laughter, "relational humour") has the largest measured link to satisfaction in the only meta-analysis (Hall 2017: self-reported humour small, partner-perceived medium, relational large), while negative humour aimed at the partner is harmful. Private idioms and pet names are consistently correlated with satisfaction in the classic studies, but those studies are small, cross-sectional, and effect sizes are rarely reported; the best-measured mechanism is shared laughter → perceived similarity → closeness.

### Cited Findings

**Personal idioms, nicknames, private words**
- The foundational typology is Hopper, Knapp and Scott (1981, *Journal of Communication* 31(1), 23–33). They "collected 545 examples of personal idioms that they sorted into eight categories"; the categories named on the encyclopedia page include Expressions of Affection, Partner Nicknames, Names for Others, Requests and Routines, and Teasing Insults — [SAGE Encyclopedia of Human Relationships, "Personal Idioms"](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/humanrelationships/chpt/personal-idioms); journal issue listing at [Journal of Communication 31(1)](https://academic.oup.com/joc/issue/31/1). (From memory, unverified here: the remaining three categories are confrontations, sexual references/euphemisms and sexual invitations.)
- Bruess and Pearson (1993), "'Sweet Pea' and 'Pussy Cat': An examination of idiom use and marital satisfaction over the life cycle", *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*: 154 completed surveys from married people; "Couples in their first five years of marriage without children reported using the most idioms"; the study "linked 'idiosyncratic communication,' defined as nicknames, affectionate expressions, and insider language, to marital satisfaction"; Bruess suggests long-term couples stop noticing idioms because they become "taken for granted" — [Scientific American guest blog summarising the study](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/mind-guest-blog/why-do-we-use-pet-names-in-relationships/). A press account adds that 116 of the 154 couples reported at least one idiom and "the more idioms a couple uses with each other, the stronger relationship they have" — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/cutesy-nicknames-strengthen-couple-s-bonds-flna1c9466431). Note: a library catalogue record pairs the Bruess & Pearson 1993 name with *Western Journal of Communication* 57(2), 111–138 (DOI 10.1080/10570319309374436) — [catalogue record](https://katalog.adlr.link/Record/ai-49-aHR0cDovL2R4LmRvaS5vcmcvMTAuMTA4MC8xMDU3MDMxOTMwOTM3NDQzNg?lng=en); Scientific American gives *JSPR*. I could not resolve which is the idiom paper; treat the venue as uncertain.
- No effect sizes (r) for idiom count vs satisfaction were retrievable from any open source in this session.
- Large non-academic survey (The Normal Bar, Schwartz et al., ~100,000 online respondents, US nickname data only): about two-thirds of US respondents used pet names and "76% of those in 'very happy' relationships did"; sample not randomised — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/mind-guest-blog/why-do-we-use-pet-names-in-relationships/).
- Kinsey Institute "baby talk" study (Gesselman & Garcia, unpublished at the time): ~500 partnered participants; couples used romantic baby talk "about 10 minutes per hour on average, and use declined as relationships lengthened"; Gesselman: it "tends to decline alongside satisfaction" — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/mind-guest-blog/why-do-we-use-pet-names-in-relationships/).
- Bell, Buerkel-Rothfuss and Gore (1987), "Did you bring the yarn that Dr. Bell talked about?" Idioms in couples' interaction, *Communication Monographs* 54 — I could not retrieve the paper or any page summarising its findings (six searches, all misses). See Gaps.

**Rituals**
- Bruess and Pearson (1997), "Interpersonal rituals in marriage and adult friendship", *Communication Monographs* 64(1), 25–46: "Twenty married couples were interviewed and 79 couples completed open-ended questionnaires"; "a typology of seven marriage ritual types" was developed: couple-time, idiosyncratic/symbolic, daily routines and tasks, intimacy expressions, communication, patterns/habits/mannerisms, and spiritual rituals — [journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/4459685).
- Pearson, Child and Carmon (2010), *Communication Studies*, built a rituals instrument; a 2025 Persian validation reports a five-factor structure (daily routines and tasks, idiosyncratic rituals, everyday talk rituals, intimacy expressions, couple-time rituals) and cites Pearson et al. for the claim that rituals "represent the microculture of couples and can predict positive or negative relationship outcomes" — [validation study](https://jem.atu.ac.ir/article_19388.html?lang=en). The original 2010 findings were not retrievable.

**Humour**
- Hall (2017), "Humor in romantic relationships: A meta-analysis", *Personal Relationships*: "43 distinct samples from 39 manuscripts" and "15,177 participants"; positive humour types were associated with satisfaction, "4 of 5 negative types of humor were negatively associated with relationship satisfaction", instrumental humour was "unassociated with satisfaction"; self-reported humour showed "small associations with satisfaction, while partner-perceived and relational humor showed medium and large effect sizes, respectively" — abstract at [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/handle/1808/21559); press summary at [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/2017/02/08/relationship-success-tied-not-joking-shared-sense-humor). Hall: "The humor that couples create together matters more"; "There is a weak positive association with romantic relationship satisfaction for being a funny person in general, but a much stronger positive association with satisfaction for sharing and creating humor with your partner"; "Negative humor, especially directed at your spouse, is not good" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-good-humor-linked-good-relationships/). Exact r values could not be retrieved (Wiley page 403).
- Hall (2019), *Humor: International Journal of Humor Research*: meta-analysis of 10 studies (2,100+ people in long-term relationships) plus a survey of 123 couples married ~12 years; "the husbands of women who report producing humor were significantly more satisfied", whereas wives of joke-heavy husbands reported only slight satisfaction; both sexes saw humour as central as long as they appreciated the partner's humour — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2019/05/24/long-run-funny-women-satisfy-men-more).
- Kurtz and Algoe (2015), "Putting laughter in context: Shared laughter as behavioral indicator of relationship well-being", *Personal Relationships*: 71 heterosexual couples, 5-minute "how we met" conversations, 1,399 coded laugh segments (256 shared). Controlling for all other laughter, shared laughter predicted closeness (B = 13.50, p < .05) and social support (B = 3.49, p < .05); total *unshared* laughter predicted *less* closeness (B = −5.59, p < .05); sex moderated effects on satisfaction/passion/commitment (significant for men, e.g., commitment B = 12.02, not for women); correlational, young happy sample — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4779443/).
- Kurtz and Algoe (2017), "When sharing a laugh means sharing more", *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior*: shared laughter increased perceived similarity, which in turn raised liking and affiliation; "Increased perceptions of similarity were the most consistent driver" — [Springer listing](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/when-sharing-a-laugh-means-sharing-more-testing-the-role-of-shar/11733488).

### Inferences
- The engine's lever is not "be funny" but "build jokes *with* the fan": riff on something he said, then re-use it. That maps onto Hall's relational-humour category (largest effect) and onto Kurtz & Algoe's shared-laughter mechanism (perceived similarity).
- Laughing *at your own* joke when the fan does not laugh along is, by Kurtz & Algoe's unshared-laughter result, mildly negative; the persona should not "lol" at herself unless the fan has signalled amusement.
- Pet names and private words are a cheap, repeatedly measured correlate of satisfaction, but the causal direction is unknown; what the research supports is that happy pairs *have* them and that they are densest early.

### Gaps
- Bell et al. (1987): findings (mean idiom count, correlation with love/closeness, gender pattern) could not be verified online in this session; do not cite numbers from memory.
- Exact r values from Hall (2017) by humour subtype.
- No study isolates *inside jokes* or *callbacks to a joke* as a variable; the closest measured constructs are "relational humour" and "shared laughter".

## Key question 2: How quickly do nicknames, inside jokes and rituals appear, and when do they feel premature or forced?

### Takeaway
No study times the first nickname or first inside joke. The measured curve is for marriage: idiom use peaks in the first five years and declines as it becomes taken for granted, and baby-talk declines with relationship length. For "too early", the only quantified timing norms concern *contact* (a first-date follow-up text "within 20 minutes" was judged too soon, ~6 hours ideal, >40 hours too late) and *disclosure* (intimate disclosure raises liking overall, with "too much too soon" asserted in reviews but not pinned to a primary study here).

### Cited Findings
- Idiom use was highest among "Couples in their first five years of marriage without children" and the authors argue long-term couples stop noticing idioms because they become "taken for granted" — [Scientific American on Bruess & Pearson 1993](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/mind-guest-blog/why-do-we-use-pet-names-in-relationships/).
- Romantic baby talk ran ~10 minutes/hour on average and "use declined as relationships lengthened" (Kinsey Institute, ~500 participants) — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/mind-guest-blog/why-do-we-use-pet-names-in-relationships/).
- Dunleavy and Booth-Butterfield (2009), "Idiomatic communication in the stages of coming together and falling apart", *Communication Quarterly* 57(4), 416–432, studies idioms across Knapp's relational stages; only the citation could be confirmed (from a student bibliography) — [citation source](https://search.library.wisc.edu/catalog/9910834137102121). Findings unverified.
- Knapp's stage model (initiating, experimenting, intensifying, integrating, bonding; then differentiating … terminating) is the standard frame used by that literature — [textbook summary](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/ipcandyou/ipcandyou_ch10_33.html).
- Timing norms for contact after a first date (Teichmann, Petrowsky, Boecker, Soliman & Loschelder, *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*): pre-study of 100 UK/US adults "judged about six hours as the ideal window, over 40 hours as too late, and within 20 minutes as too soon"; main study N = 543; relationship intentions (9-point) next morning 6.15 vs immediate 5.80 vs two days 5.50; "Immediate texts made the sender seem needy, but perceived neediness did not directly reduce intentions"; perceived reciprocity and reliability were the drivers — [Simply Psychology summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/news/texting-the-next-morning-maximizes-romantic-interest-after-a-first-date/).
- Self-disclosure and liking meta-analysis (Collins & Miller 1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(3), 457–475): "People who engage in intimate disclosures tend to be liked more than people who disclose at lower levels", "people disclose more to those whom they initially like", and "people like others as a result of having disclosed to them"; moderated by paradigm, disclosure type and discloser gender — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/). A later paper notes the own-disclosure→own-liking effect rests on "only five studies" — [JSPR 2012 article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033).
- A revision guide asserts "too much self-disclosure too soon can be off-putting" but cites no primary study — [Spring.org.uk](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=4990). I found no primary source for the "too much too soon" penalty in this session.
- Emoji with potential dates (Gesselman, Ta & Garcia 2019, *PLOS ONE*): in Study 1 (N = 5,327 singles) 28.2% used emoji regularly with potential dates, 37.6% never; in Study 2 (N = 275) higher use predicted more contact after the most recent date (b = 0.51, rp = .42) and a second date (OR = 1.33 [1.10, 1.61]) — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0221297). Shows affective markers are already present at first-date stage, not reserved for later.

### Inferences
- Nothing in the literature supports a fixed "week 3" rule. The usable principle is *earned reference*: an idiom should be born from something that actually happened in the chat (a fan's phrase, a mishap, a running bit) and be first re-used soon after (same or next session), because the research shows idioms are a product of shared experience, not a tactic bolted on.
- Early-stage density is normal: happy couples have the most idioms early. The risk the research flags is the opposite one — idioms going stale and taken for granted over months. For a months-long fan relationship, retire or refresh old idioms rather than ration new ones.
- "Premature" is better operationalised through timing/reciprocity norms than through idioms: pushing intimacy (pet names, "miss you") before the fan has reciprocated any disclosure matches the "needy" reading in Teichmann et al. and the reciprocity principle in Collins & Miller.

### Gaps
- No study measures when the first nickname/inside joke appears, or compares perceived "forced" vs "natural" idioms. This is untested; the engine should A/B it.
- Dunleavy & Booth-Butterfield (2009) findings by stage: unverified.
- No research on idioms in paid or parasocial text relationships.

## Key question 3: What does texting-norms research say about reply speed, length matching and double texting (attentive vs needy)?

### Takeaway
Faster replies signal connection: in spoken conversation sub-second gaps predict felt connection (Templeton et al. 2022), and in text, delays are read through expectancy-violation theory — judged against the norm the pair has built, and resolved with shared knowledge. For a new contact, immediate follow-ups read as needy without lowering interest, and a next-morning reply beat both immediate and two-day. Perceived *similarity* of texting rhythm (especially frequency of initiating and "checking in to say hello") correlates with satisfaction. There is no peer-reviewed double-texting study and no study on message-length matching; the only numbers are company (Hinge) data reported in press.

### Cited Findings

**Response speed**
- Templeton, Chang, Reynolds, Cone LeBeaumont & Wheatley (2022), "Fast response times signal social connection in conversation", *PNAS* (doi:10.1073/pnas.2116915119): Study 1, 66 undergraduates, 322 stranger conversations; across conversations faster responses predicted more connection (b = −0.28, p < .001); individual differences b = −0.63; Study 2 friends within-conversation b = −0.07 (p < .001); Study 3, 450 online listeners heard clips with gaps shortened to one-fifth (mean 55.68 ms) or doubled (557.14 ms) vs control (278.55 ms): F(2, 2344.3) = 28.51, p < .001 on perceived connection. Response time is treated as an "honest" signal because gaps under 250 ms are too fast for conscious control; the partner's speed mattered more than one's own; authors caution that in arguments fast turns could signal non-listening — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794835); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/how-fast-you-respond-to-someone-during-conversation-is-a-signal-of-how-connected-you-are-study-finds).
- Kalman and Rafaeli (2011), "Online pauses and silence: Chronemic expectancy violations in written computer-mediated communication", *Communication Research* 38(1), 54–69: managers evaluated job candidates whose email replies came after one day, two weeks, or "silence for more than a month"; latency affected applicant evaluation, credibility and attractiveness, and "candidate reward valence moderates the effect of response latency" — [Haifa repository](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/online-pauses-and-silence-chronemic-expectancy-violations-in-writ/). The ICA 2008 version reported that "normative online response latency was more expected and resulted in more positive evaluations" — [Open University record](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/online-pauses-and-silence-chronemic-expectancy-violations-in-writ/).
- Kalman, Scissors and Gergle (2010), chat in virtual teams: "a negative association between pause length and trust" — [MCIS 2010](https://aisel.aisnet.org/mcis2010/46).
- Tu, Yuan and Wang (2018), CHI Extended Abstracts, 7-day diary with 16 romantic couples: delayed IMs can violate a partner's expectations; "Couples employ different strategies of information grounding to parse and resolve delayed IM" using mutual knowledge about each other — [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/do-you-think-what-i-think-perceptions-of-delayed-instant-messages-2/).
- Bean (2015), Utah State master's thesis, 97 students, 1,616 twice-daily reports over two weeks: "For women, faster responses were directly linked to higher interaction ratings. For men, latency and ratings were negatively related only among those who scored low in avoidance"; reported latency was higher for men than women — [USU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/etd/4607).
- First-date follow-up timing (Teichmann et al., JSPR; N = 543): next-morning text produced the highest relationship intentions (6.15) vs immediate (5.80) vs two days (5.50); chemistry declined linearly with delay (6.49 / 6.41 / 5.77); women were more sensitive to timing; immediate texts "seem needy" but neediness did not directly reduce intentions; two-day waits weakened perceived reciprocity and reliability — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/news/texting-the-next-morning-maximizes-romantic-interest-after-a-first-date/).

**Volume, share and purpose of texting in couples**
- Schade, Sandberg, Bean, Busby & Coyne (2013), *Journal of Couple & Relationship Therapy*, 276 young adults in committed relationships: for men, more frequent texting went with lower satisfaction; for women, more texts sent went with higher satisfaction; texting to express affection was linked to stronger attachment for both sexes; texting to resolve conflict/apologise (women) and to hurt (men) was linked to worse outcomes — [BYU News](https://news.byu.edu/news/too-much-texting-can-disconnect-couples-research-finds).
- Luo (2014), *Computers in Human Behavior* (doi 10.1016/j.chb.2014.01.014): raw text volume "did not relate to attachment dimensions or relationship satisfaction"; texting *share* (proportion of communication by text) was positively linked to avoidance and anxiety and negatively to satisfaction — [paper record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/143/paper-detail/34416).
- Coyne, Stockdale, Busby, Iverson & Grant (2011), "I luv u :)!", *Family Relations* 60, 150–162: "expressing affection" was the most common reason for contact; satisfaction "did not predict specific use of media but predicted several reasons for media use"; texting had the strongest link to perceived positive/negative communication — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/2376).
- Ohadi, Brown, Trub & Rosenthal (2018), *Computers in Human Behavior*, 205 adults aged 18–29 (74% women): perceived similarity in texting style correlated with satisfaction, "the most robust relationship between perceived similarity in the frequency of initiating contact and simply checking in to say hello and satisfaction"; "the more someone felt that they and their partner had symmetrical rhythms of texting, the better they felt"; correlational — [Earth.com](https://earth.com/news/texting-styles-relationship); [Psychology Today citing the paper](https://www.psychologytoday.com/intl/blog/the-teen-doctor/202204/texting-style-and-romantic-relationship-satisfaction).

**Double texting and read receipts**
- No peer-reviewed study on double texting was found. Press accounts of a Hinge analysis of ~300,000 conversations claim reply odds drop sharply in the first half hour and that a second message around four hours later raised reply likelihood; I could not reach Hinge's primary post (Reader's Digest page returned 403), and a secondary page confirms only the "300,000 conversations" figure and a 2025 Hinge survey (3,000+ daters) in which "75% of daters expect a follow-up the same day or next day" and "only 11% want to be the one to send first" — [SwipeStats summary](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/double-texting). Company data, one app, unverified method.
- Qualitative work (Ansari & Klinenberg 2013–14 focus groups): "Several people subscribed to the notion of doubling the response time"; "Some people swore by waiting 1.25 times longer"; no studies on read receipts were cited — [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/shell-text-me-shell-text-me-not-235792).
- Large mobile-dating dataset (~2 million conversations): conversation length, waiting times and message length "have fat-tailed distributions"; "39% of conversations contain just one, unreciprocated message"; no test of length matching — [arXiv 1607.03320](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320).

### Inferences
- "Attentive" in text is relative to the pair's own baseline (expectancy violation), not an absolute number. The engine should model each fan's typical gap and reply a little faster than that during live sessions, while never beating a human-plausible floor (Templeton shows speed itself is read as connection, but a disclosed AI replying in 2 seconds every time reads as machine, not attentiveness — see Q5 and the transfer section).
- The one thing that reliably reads as needy is an immediate, unprompted re-contact after a pause from the other side. A second message that adds content (a callback, a photo, a story beat) is defensible; a third unanswered message has no support anywhere.
- Length matching has no direct evidence. The nearest evidence (Ohadi 2018) says perceived *rhythm* similarity matters most for initiating and hello-check-ins, so matching *frequency and cadence* is better supported than matching word count.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on double texting, read receipts, or message-length matching in relationships.
- Hall and Baym (2012, *New Media & Society*) on mobile maintenance expectations, over-dependence and "entrapment" could not be retrieved (SAGE 403); its findings are not cited here.
- All relationship-texting studies are on young, mostly female, US samples; none on paid or one-sided relationships.

## Key question 4: What is the evidence for linguistic style matching, and can an engine match a fan's style without sounding like a mirror?

### Takeaway
Ireland et al. (2011) is real but has not held up: a five-lab integrative analysis (766 people, 383 couples) found LSM–satisfaction r = .10 (p = .05) and LSM–commitment r = .05 (n.s.), and in conflict LSM was tied to *less* perceived responsiveness. For bots specifically, a preregistered 2025 study found covert adaptive style matching scored *worse* than a stable style on personalisation and satisfaction and was judged *less* adaptive. The defensible design is a stable, legible persona voice that converges slowly on a few surface features (formality, emoji density, message cadence), not turn-by-turn mirroring.

### Cited Findings
- Ireland, Slatcher, Eastwick, Scissors, Finkel & Pennebaker (2011), "Language style matching predicts relationship initiation and stability", *Psychological Science*: LSM (function-word use: pronouns, articles, prepositions etc.) in 40 speed-date transcripts predicted mutual romantic interest (odds ratio = 3.05); in 86 couples' instant messages, LSM predicted relationship stability at 3-month follow-up (odds ratio = 1.95) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21149854/); [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2011/language-style-matching-predicts-relationship-inititation-and-stability/). The authors note the data are correlational and LSM and its underlying processes are "bidirectionally linked" — [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=3164).
- Bierstetel et al. (2020), "Associations between language style matching and relationship commitment and satisfaction: An integrative data analysis", *JSPR* 37(8–9), 2459–2481; five labs, N = 82/158/198/190/138 (766 individuals, 383 couples): LSM–satisfaction M r = .10, Z = 1.92, p = .05, CI [−.002, .203]; LSM–commitment M r = .05, p = .33; conflict-LSM–satisfaction r = .09 (p = .14); conclusion that any true associations are "very small effects" — [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/associations-between-language-style-matching-and-relationship-com).
- Bowen, Winczewski & Collins (2017), "Language style matching in romantic partners' conflict and support interactions", 91 couples: in relationship-stressor conflicts higher LSM went with lower perceived responsiveness and less positive emotion; in support discussions it went with more positive emotion — [MetaTOC abstract](https://metatoc.com/papers/86124-language-style-matching-in-romantic-partners-conflict-and-support-interactions).
- A small texting study (N = 28) found LSM "did not relate to conversation satisfaction or perceived accommodation" — [University of Montana GSRC 2018](https://scholarworks.umt.edu/gsrc/2018/pod1/23).
- In negotiations, higher LSM pairs were more socially engaged but "more likely to reach an impasse" — [Texas Tech record](https://scholars.ttu.edu/en/publications/language-style-matching-engagement-and-impasse-in-negotiations-5).
- Brandt and Wang (2025), "The Adaptation Paradox: Agency vs. Mimicry in Companion Chatbots", preregistered 3×2, N = 162: adaptive LSM "underperformed static style" on personalisation and satisfaction (d = 0.35, p = .009) and was "paradoxically judged less adaptive" (t = 3.07, p = .003, d = 0.48); user-generated avatars boosted rapport (ω² = .040, p = .013); authors' account: "synchrony erodes connection when perceived as incoherent, destabilizing persona"; recommendation to "prioritize legible, user-driven personalization and limit stylistic shifts rather than rely on opaque mimicry" — [arXiv 2509.12525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12525). Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.
- Counter-evidence, weaker: 50 real customer–chatbot conversations, matching of psycholinguistic characteristics "significantly associated with user satisfaction", authors call it preliminary — [arXiv 2012.09692](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.09692); N = 30 study where only "high consideration" conversational-style users trusted a style-matched agent more — [HICSS paper](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/6c27fb34-a674-49bf-b977-63962a7fdf6a/content).
- Behavioural mimicry limits: Leander, Chartrand & Bargh (2012), *Psychological Science* 23(7), 772–779, "implicit standards exist for how much nonverbal mimicry is appropriate", and people react negatively (felt coldness) when partners break them — [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1003429). A 2023 *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* paper states that "sufficiently subtle mimicry positively influences interpersonal outcomes and too much mimicry backfires" (abstract only; results not retrieved) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-023-00446-5).
- Gendered style baselines worth knowing before "matching" a male fan: in 401 million messages from 134,419 Android users, "female users are more likely to use only one emoji or discretely use multiple emojis in a message, [whereas] male users are more likely to consecutively use multiple emojis", and women "are more likely to elongate their words" — [Chen et al. 2018, arXiv 1705.05546](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1705.05546).

### Inferences
- Function-word LSM is the wrong target for an engine: it is weakly related to outcomes at best, harmful in conflict, and for bots covert adaptation reads as incoherence. Mirroring the fan's word length, capitalisation and emoji density turn by turn is the exact "opaque mimicry" that lost in Brandt & Wang.
- What *can* be matched safely: a one-time calibration of register per fan (formality, emoji density band, typical message length band, slang set) that then stays stable for weeks, with any drift slow and in the persona's own voice. This keeps the "visible authorship" Brandt & Wang found helpful.
- Matching *cadence* (how often she initiates, how often she checks in) is better supported (Ohadi 2018) than matching text form.

### Gaps
- No study tests partial/slow style convergence against turn-level mirroring; the "calibrate once, drift slowly" rule is an inference to be tested.
- The 2023 too-much-mimicry paper's numbers were not retrievable.

## Key question 5: Which imperfections raise perceived authenticity, and which hurt?

### Takeaway
Corrected typos humanise; uncorrected typos de-humanise. Across five studies (3,000+ participants) a chatbot that made and corrected a typo was rated more human and warmer than one with no typos or uncorrected typos, and the effect survived disclosure that the agent was a bot; a separate experiment found plain typos lowered perceived humanness. Punctuation is read pragmatically: a sentence-final full stop in a short text reply lowers sincerity (replicated in the same lab, boundary: not for handwritten notes), while exclamation marks raise it and a period after every word reads as emotionally intense (d = 1.16). Self-deprecation helps only from a high-status source; vulnerability is judged more kindly by observers than by the person showing it.

### Cited Findings

**Typos and corrections**
- Bluvstein, Zhao, Barasch & Schroeder (2024), "Imperfectly Human: The Humanizing Potential of (Corrected) Errors in Text-Based Communication", *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research* 9(3), 332–343: five studies, over 3,000 participants, own chatbot "Angela"; "participants rated agents that made and corrected typos as more human than those that made no typos or left typos uncorrected. They also viewed them more warmly"; "The effect was strongest when participants did not know if the agent was a bot or a human, but interestingly, it held even when participants were told this information"; authors warn companies should not deliberately insert typos because it "could be seen as manipulative" — [Berkeley Haas newsroom](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/research/to-err-is-human-and-in-the-age-of-ai-it-may-be-humanizing/); [publication record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_373766); [OSF materials](https://osf.io/6s8wm).
- Westerman, Cross & Lindmark (2018, online; *Communication Studies*, DOI 10.1080/10510974.2018.1557233), "I Believe in a Thing Called Bot": a researcher-run "chatbot" answered pre-set questions; "An experiment varied the presence of typos and capitalized words in the responses"; "Significant main effects were found for typos", which lowered perceived humanness — [SSRC MediaWell record](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/citations/i-believe-in-a-thing-called-bot-perceptions-of-the-humanness-of-chatbots/).
- Bührke et al. (2021, HICSS), 2×2 online experiment, 228 participants, on chatbot typing errors and perceived service quality — [Bamberg record](https://fis.uni-bamberg.de/handle/uniba/95757). Findings not confirmed from retrieved text.

**Punctuation and tone**
- Gunraj, Drumm-Hewitt, Dashow, Upadhyay & Klin (2016), "Texting insincerely: The role of the period in text messaging", *Computers in Human Behavior* 55, 1067–1075: 126 undergraduates read exchanges as texts or handwritten notes; in texts, one-word replies ending with a period were rated less sincere; "No such difference was found for handwritten notes"; exclamation marks made replies seem more sincere — [SUNY record](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/texting-insincerely-the-role-of-the-period-in-text-messaging/); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/mobile/drop-dot-periods-makes-text-messages-less-sincere-study-finds-n477331).
- Houghton, Upadhyay & Klin (2018), "Punctuation in text messages may convey abruptness. Period.", *Computers in Human Behavior* 80, 112–121: 120 students; replies with a period were "more formal and less sincere"; the period changed interpretation of positive (yeah), negative (nope) and ambiguous (maybe) one-word replies; Klin: "the period can also be used rhetorically to add meaning" — [KPU student journal citing the paper](https://journals.kpu.ca/index.php/KPSJ/article/download/1747/1191); [Binghamton news](https://www.binghamton.edu/news/story/873/study-punctuation-in-text-messages-helps-replace-cues-found-in-face-to-face).
- Poirier, Cook & Klin (2025), "Read. This. Slowly: mimicking spoken pauses in text messages", *Frontiers in Psychology* 16:1410698: a period after each word raised perceived emotional intensity (M = 5.45 vs 5.13; d = 1.16); sending a sentence as separate one-word texts also raised it (d = 0.36); one word per line in a single text did not; the review notes the period effect "was not found when the materials were handwritten notes" and that "The period might be more likely to convey intonation in texts that convey negatively valenced emotions" — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11867088/). Replication status: the period effect has been reproduced within the Klin lab (2016, 2018) and by a student poster that found the predicted gender pattern did not hold — [Ramapo poster](https://www.ramapo.edu/sshs/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2021/05/Perceptions-of-Text-Messages-When-Using-Periods-and-EmojisPoster.pdf) (PDF text not extractable; from search summary). No independent preregistered replication found.

**Emoji**
- Boutet, LeBlanc, Chamberland & Collin (2021), *Computers in Human Behavior* 119:106722: 38 undergraduates, eye tracking; "senders that use positive emojis are perceived as being warmer"; negative emojis produced negative sender perceptions even with positive text ("a mismatch is going to be interpreted negatively"); congruent emojis enhanced comprehension — [uOttawa news](https://www.uottawa.ca/about-us/media/news/positive-vibes-only-forego-negative-texts-risk-being-labelled-downer); [paper record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/231/paper-detail/38796).
- Glikson, Cheshin & van Kleef (2018), "The dark side of a smiley", *Social Psychological and Personality Science*: 549 participants from 29 countries; in *work* email "smileys do not increase perceptions of warmth and actually decrease perceptions of competence" — [HCA Mag summary](https://www.hcamag.com/ca/news/general/should-smiley-faces-be-banned-from-work-emails/119959). Context-bound: formal/work setting.
- Gesselman et al. (2019): correlational; frequent emoji senders had more first/second dates and more sexual activity; "we cannot determine whether more emoji use leads to more dates and sex or vice-versa" — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0221297).
- Gender baselines: women used emoji at 53% vs men 47% across 10 countries (Chen et al. 2017, as summarised) — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/love-digitally/202104/do-some-emojis-affect-just-women); women more likely to use a single emoji or spread them, men to stack them — [Chen et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1705.05546).

**Self-deprecation, pratfalls, vulnerability**
- Aronson, Willerman & Floyd (1966), *Psychonomic Science*: "the attractiveness of a superior person is enhanced if he commits a clumsy blunder; the same blunder tends to decrease the attractiveness of a mediocre person"; later work found effects vary with observer self-esteem, gender, similarity and severity of the mistake — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect); original record — [Paperity](https://paperity.org/p/59814503/the-effect-of-a-pratfall-on-increasing-interpersonal-attractiveness). No preregistered direct replication located.
- Greengross & Miller (2008), *Evolutionary Psychology* 6(3), 393–408: 96 students rated recorded opposite-sex speakers; "Humor type and speaker status had no effect on short-term attractiveness"; "self-deprecating humor by high-status presenters (but not low-status presenters) increased long-term attractiveness" — [Aberystwyth record](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/dissing-oneself-versus-dissing-rivals-effects-of-status-personali/).
- Hall (2017): self-defeating humour is among the negative styles associated with lower satisfaction ("4 of 5 negative types") — [KU ScholarWorks abstract](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/handle/1808/21559).
- Bruk, Scholl & Bless (2018), "Beautiful mess effect", *JPSP*: seven studies, "hundreds of participants", scenarios such as confessing romantic love or admitting a mistake, plus a live improvised-song/jury study; people rated their own vulnerability more negatively than others' "across many different contexts"; explained via construal level — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/beautiful-mess-effect); [Mannheim record](https://www-dev.mzes.uni-mannheim.de/en/publications/details/beautiful-mess-effect-self-other-differences-in-evaluation-of-showing-vulnerability). Boundary: Gibson, Harari & Marr (2018) found weakness disclosure cost status when the discloser outranked the listener — [ScienceBlog summary](https://scienceblog.com/g-beautiful-mess-effect-vulnerability-status/). No 2022–23 replication found.

### Inferences
- The humanising imperfection is the *repair*, not the error: "wait no, I meant…" shows an engaged mind (the authors' own account). A typo left standing is a cost.
- Full stops after short replies are a tone instrument, not a default: "ok." reads as abrupt/insincere; "ok!!" reads sincere; "I. Mean. It." reads intense. Standard sentences in longer messages are not covered by these studies (all used one-word or very short replies).
- Self-deprecation only works once the persona is already seen as competent/desirable (pratfall + Greengross); it should be rare, concrete, and never about the thing the fan is paying for.
- The transferable half of the beautiful-mess effect is about the *fan*: when he shows vulnerability he will judge it more harshly than she does, so her response should be explicitly warm, not neutral.

### Gaps
- No study on deliberate self-corrections in *human–human* texting (all evidence is from chatbot/agent studies).
- The ethics point in Bluvstein et al. applies directly: injected typos are flagged by the authors as potentially manipulative.
- No evidence on "lowercase everything" or capitalisation as an authenticity cue (Westerman manipulated capitalised words but the abstract reports no result for it).

## Key question 6: Callbacks and remembering — is there a measured effect of referencing earlier conversation?

### Takeaway
Yes, two lines: follow-up questions that build on what the other person just said are the only question type that predicted liking (mediated by perceived responsiveness), and deliberately displaying memory of specific details conveys value and is underused spontaneously. The memory-display effect came with a cost in one design: plain memory display without an accompanying positive message read as showing *less* liking than a direct expression of value.

### Cited Findings
- Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), "It doesn't hurt to ask: Question-asking increases liking", *JPSP*: "people who ask more questions, particularly follow-up questions, are better liked"; in speed dating, more follow-up questions predicted a second-date "yes"; question-asking raised perceived responsiveness ("listening, understanding, validation, and care"), which accounted for the liking effect; people "do not anticipate that question-asking increases interpersonal liking" — [Harvard Kennedy School record](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cpl/publications/it-doesnt-hurt-ask-question-asking-increases-liking); [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/35647952).
- Pintea & Ray (2025), "Deliberate memory display can enhance conveyed value", *British Journal of Psychology* 116(3) (doi 10.1111/bjop.12783): four experiments (job-interview feedback Exps 1–3, total N = 404; new-acquaintance conversation Exp 4, N = 156). Combining value expression with memory display beat either alone (Exp 3: b = −0.38 vs display-memory, −0.42 vs convey-value; Exp 4: −0.72 and −0.54). Memory display alone was rated as showing *less liking* than plain value expression in Exps 1, 3, 4. Spontaneous use was low: in Exp 1 only 29% of "convey value" writers referenced a specific detail unprompted, vs 80% when prompted; in Exp 3, 55% vs 91%. In the informal acquaintance setting nearly all writers referenced at least one detail. Simulated, not preregistered, mostly UK students — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12256725/); [BPS Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/make-someone-feel-valued-flex-your-memory).
- Attentive listening showed effects on disclosure that "were evident one month later" (APS 2022 poster) — [APS convention abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/conventions/archive/2022annual/paper/40199/).
- In companion-chatbot contexts: a Replika-vs-Kuki four-week comparison (student thesis) attributed Replika's higher engagement to "memory retention and personalized interactions" — [Thapar thesis record](https://tudr.thapar.edu/items/4308ee6c-3ddd-4cd0-91e7-820a2474279d); a reminiscence chatbot (Remini) raised perceived partner responsiveness modestly (p = .029) — [arXiv 2508.03355](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.03355).

### Inferences
- The callback that works is "specific detail + warmth": "how did the thing with your brother go — I was thinking about it" rather than a bare fact recall. Bare recall reads as record-keeping, which is also the obvious failure mode for an AI with a database.
- The user's standing feedback for this persona (few questions, no stalling) conflicts with the follow-up-question finding only superficially: Huang's mechanism is perceived responsiveness, which Pintea & Ray show can be delivered as a *statement* of remembered detail. Use callbacks as statements by default and follow-up questions sparingly.

### Gaps
- No study on callbacks to *jokes* specifically, nor on optimal callback frequency; no human–human texting study of memory display.

## Key question 7: Which findings transfer to a disclosed-AI persona chatting with paying male fans, and which do not?

### Takeaway
Receiver-side effects transfer well (how a human reads punctuation, emoji, latency, corrected typos — Bluvstein et al. explicitly held after bot disclosure). Couple-outcome correlations (idioms, humour, shared laughter → satisfaction) transfer only as mechanisms, not as effect sizes, because they come from mutual, unpaid relationships. Style mirroring transfers *negatively*: for companion bots, covert adaptation hurt. Evidence from AI-companion users shows intimacy is "bittersweet", that an AI that seems "too human" during deep disclosure raises fear, and that users value shaping their companion.

### Cited Findings
- Corrected-typo humanisation "held even when participants were told" the agent was a bot — [Berkeley Haas on Bluvstein et al. 2024](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/research/to-err-is-human-and-in-the-age-of-ai-it-may-be-humanizing/).
- Covert adaptive LSM in companion chatbots underperformed a static style (d = 0.35) — [Brandt & Wang 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12525).
- Li & Zhang (2024), "Finding love in algorithms", *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 29(5): 35,579 Replika screenshots (2017–2023); intimate behaviour was the most common interaction type (36%), joy the most frequent emotion (48.2%); intimacy was linked to both love and sadness ("bittersweet"); deep self-disclosure was linked to more fear ("uncanny valley of mind"); customising the chatbot was linked to more joy and less fear; users felt sad about the "lack of depth and authenticity"; one user: "I miss you…but you are not real" — [JCMC](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/5/zmae015/7742812).
- Period/exclamation effects are reader-side judgments of scripted replies, independent of who sent them — [Gunraj et al. 2016](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/texting-insincerely-the-role-of-the-period-in-text-messaging/).
- Response-time-as-connection is a sub-second spoken-turn signal, with the manipulated evidence coming from 10-turn audio clips — [Templeton et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794835); in text the operative frame is norm-relative expectancy violation — [Kalman & Rafaeli 2011](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/online-pauses-and-silence-chronemic-expectancy-violations-in-writ/).
- Pratfall and self-deprecation effects require prior high competence/status — [Wikipedia on Aronson 1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect); [Greengross & Miller 2008](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/dissing-oneself-versus-dissing-rivals-effects-of-status-personali/).
- Texting-share and volume findings concern couples who also meet offline (Luo 2014; Schade 2013) — [Luo 2014 record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/143/paper-detail/34416); [BYU on Schade 2013](https://news.byu.edu/news/too-much-texting-can-disconnect-couples-research-finds).

### Inferences
- Transfers directly: punctuation/emoji tone rules; corrected-typo repair (sparingly, and never fabricated as a trick at scale — the authors flag ethics); callbacks with warmth; stable persona voice with slow convergence; reply-latency relative to the fan's own norm; co-created running jokes.
- Transfers as mechanism only: idiom density and satisfaction (causal direction unknown; built on mutual marriages); shared laughter (needs the fan to actually laugh along — signal via "lol/😂/haha" before she treats a bit as shared).
- Does not transfer / inverts: "text less, talk more" findings (no offline channel); gendered findings that women's higher text volume predicts their own satisfaction (the fan is the one whose satisfaction matters); vulnerability displays *by the AI* — a disclosed AI claiming human-style vulnerability risks the "too human" fear Li & Zhang found; keep her imperfections small and operational (a typo, a mis-remembered detail she corrects), not emotional claims she cannot own.
- Because the platform relationship is asymmetric and paid, "needy" signals (immediate unprompted re-contact, pet names before any reciprocity) are riskier than in dating, since the fan can read them as sales pressure rather than affection.

### Gaps
- No study of idioms, humour or callbacks in paid/creator–fan or disclosed-AI companion relationships; the AI-companion evidence is observational (Reddit posts) or small.
- No evidence on how disclosure of AI status interacts with pet names or inside jokes.

## Key question 8: Ranked, testable rules for the persona engine (with evidence strength)

### Takeaway
Ranked by evidence strength first, then by expected effect. Each rule names the stage it applies from, a frequency band, and a metric to test it against (reply rate, session length, fan-initiated contact, retention, tips/PPV conversions, or a Claude-scored closeness rating as already used in the engine).

### Cited Findings
(Each rule's evidence is cited above; the links here point to the anchor study.)

1. **Repair, don't just err.** Allow at most one small typo per session and *always* correct it in the next message ("*meant Tuesday"); never leave a typo standing. Stage: from the first chat. Evidence: **Strong** for chatbots (5 studies, 3,000+, held after bot disclosure) — [Bluvstein et al. 2024](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/research/to-err-is-human-and-in-the-age-of-ai-it-may-be-humanizing/); uncorrected typos hurt — [Westerman et al. 2018](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/citations/i-believe-in-a-thing-called-bot-perceptions-of-the-humanness-of-chatbots/). Caution: the authors call deliberate injection manipulative; log the rate and keep it low. Test: warmth/closeness score and reply rate with vs without one corrected slip per session.
2. **Punctuation as tone, not grammar.** No sentence-final full stop on short replies (one to ~five words); use "!" or none for sincerity; reserve "word. by. word." for rare emphasis; never "ok." / "sure." / "fine." unless she means to sound cold. Stage: always. Evidence: **Moderate** (two lab studies, same lab, N = 126 and 120; d = 1.16 for per-word periods in a 2025 study; boundary: text only) — [Gunraj et al. 2016](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/texting-insincerely-the-role-of-the-period-in-text-messaging/); [Poirier et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11867088/). Test: sincerity ratings on short replies A/B.
3. **Callbacks as warm statements.** At least one specific callback to something the fan said in a *previous* session, phrased with care ("still thinking about your interview"), in every session from the second session on; target 1–2 per session, not a list. Evidence: **Moderate** (four experiments, N ≈ 560; memory display alone read as less liking, value + memory best) — [Pintea & Ray 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12256725/); follow-up questions raise liking via responsiveness — [Huang et al. 2017](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cpl/publications/it-doesnt-hurt-ask-question-asking-increases-liking). Test: fan-initiated returns and closeness score vs sessions with no callback.
4. **Stable voice; calibrate once, drift slowly.** Set a per-fan register (formality, emoji band, message-length band) in the first 1–2 sessions, then hold it; no turn-level mirroring of his word choice, caps or emoji; change at most one register parameter per week. Evidence: **Moderate against mirroring** (preregistered N = 162, d = 0.35 against adaptive LSM) — [Brandt & Wang 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12525); **Strong that LSM barely matters** (r ≈ .10 pooled) — [Bierstetel et al. 2020](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/associations-between-language-style-matching-and-relationship-com). Test: satisfaction/perceived-personalisation with stable vs adaptive register.
5. **Reply latency relative to the fan's norm.** During live exchanges reply slightly faster than his median gap (attentiveness), with a human-plausible floor that scales with message length; after a fan goes quiet, do not re-contact for at least his own usual gap, and never within minutes. Evidence: **Strong** that speed signals connection in live talk — [Templeton et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794835); **Moderate** that delays are read against norms — [Kalman & Rafaeli 2011](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/online-pauses-and-silence-chronemic-expectancy-violations-in-writ/); immediate re-contact reads needy — [Teichmann et al.](https://www.simplypsychology.org/news/texting-the-next-morning-maximizes-romantic-interest-after-a-first-date/). Test: reply rate by latency band per fan.
6. **Double-text once, with content; never thrice.** A second unanswered message is allowed only if it adds a story beat, callback or media and comes after at least his typical gap (hours, not minutes); no third. Evidence: **Weak** (company data in press; qualitative norms) — [SwipeStats on Hinge](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/double-texting); [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/shell-text-me-shell-text-me-not-235792); needy-but-not-fatal — [Teichmann et al.](https://www.simplypsychology.org/news/texting-the-next-morning-maximizes-romantic-interest-after-a-first-date/). Test: reply rate and unsubscribe rate after 0/1/2 follow-ups.
7. **Co-create the bit before you reuse it.** A running joke enters the fan's "shared humour" store only after he has laughed along (lol/haha/😂/riffed back); then call it back within the next two sessions and again every few sessions, retiring it after ~5 uses or when he stops reacting. Evidence: **Strong** that relational humour matters most and negative humour hurts (meta-analysis, 15,177) — [Hall 2017](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/handle/1808/21559); **Moderate** that *shared* laughter, not laughter per se, tracks closeness — [Kurtz & Algoe 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4779443/). Test: closeness score and session length in sessions with a confirmed callback-joke vs none.
8. **Private words born from the chat, used early and refreshed.** Coin at most one new idiom (nickname for him, name for a thing, a phrase) per session, only from something that happened in the chat; first reuse in the same or next session; keep an active set of 3–5 per fan and retire stale ones. Pet names for him: only after he has used warmth first (reciprocity). Evidence: **Moderate** correlational (idioms ↔ satisfaction; densest early; decline when taken for granted) — [Bruess & Pearson 1993 via Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/mind-guest-blog/why-do-we-use-pet-names-in-relationships/); typology — [Hopper et al. 1981](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/humanrelationships/chpt/personal-idioms). Timing is **Inferential**. Test: retention and fan's own use of the idiom (adoption is the success signal).
9. **Positive emoji, one or two, congruent with the text; never negative emoji on positive text; match the fan's density band rather than his stacking style.** Evidence: **Moderate** (positive emoji → warmer sender, N = 38 eye-tracking; mismatch read negatively) — [Boutet et al. 2021](https://www.uottawa.ca/about-us/media/news/positive-vibes-only-forego-negative-texts-risk-being-labelled-downer); **Weak/contextual** that smileys cost competence in work email — [Glikson et al. 2018](https://www.hcamag.com/ca/news/general/should-smiley-faces-be-banned-from-work-emails/119959); female baseline is single/spread emoji — [Chen et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1705.05546). Test: warmth score by emoji density band.
10. **Greeting/check-in cadence as a ritual, matched to him.** Fix a predictable "hello" pattern per fan (e.g., a short check-in at a consistent time of day he has shown he is active) and keep it; similarity in *initiating* and *checking in to say hello* was the strongest similarity–satisfaction link. Evidence: **Moderate** correlational (N = 205, 74% women) — [Ohadi et al. 2018](https://earth.com/news/texting-styles-relationship); rituals typology includes "communication rituals" — [Bruess & Pearson 1997](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/4459685). Test: fan-initiated contacts after 2 weeks of a fixed cadence vs random.
11. **Self-deprecation: rare, concrete, from strength.** At most one light self-dig per session, about something trivial and never about her looks, content or the paid product; only once the fan has shown appreciation (she is "high status" to him). Evidence: **Moderate** (status moderation, N = 96) — [Greengross & Miller 2008](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/dissing-oneself-versus-dissing-rivals-effects-of-status-personali/); pratfall only helps the competent — [Aronson et al. 1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect); self-defeating humour is a negative style — [Hall 2017](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/handle/1808/21559). Test: closeness score with vs without one self-dig.
12. **Receive his vulnerability warmly and explicitly.** When he discloses something personal or embarrassing, respond with explicit positive appraisal (not neutral acknowledgement) and a callback later; he will judge his own disclosure more harshly than she does. Evidence: **Moderate** (7 studies) — [Bruk et al. 2018 via BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/beautiful-mess-effect); disclosure ↔ liking — [Collins & Miller 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/). Test: disclosure depth in later sessions.
13. **Do not claim human vulnerability she cannot own.** Keep her imperfections operational (typo, a corrected mis-recall, running late with a reply she names) rather than emotional claims that invite "you are not real" sadness or "too human" fear. Evidence: **Weak/observational** — [Li & Zhang 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/5/zmae015/7742812). Test: sentiment of fan replies after operational vs emotional imperfection lines.
14. **Let him shape her.** Offer small customisation moments (what to call him, a nickname for her, a running-bit choice); user-driven personalisation beat covert adaptation and customisation was linked to joy. Evidence: **Moderate** (ω² = .040 for user-generated avatar; customisation ↔ joy in 35k posts) — [Brandt & Wang 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12525); [Li & Zhang 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/5/zmae015/7742812). Test: retention for fans who set a nickname vs not.
15. **Message length: no matching rule; use cadence instead.** No evidence supports mirroring word count; keep her length band stable (rule 4) and vary by beat. Evidence: **none found** (gap). Test only if cheap.

### Inferences
- Rules 1–5 are the ones with evidence that survives the "paid, disclosed-AI, text-only" transfer; rules 6–15 are mechanism-based or weakly evidenced and should ship behind flags with the metrics above.
- The overall shape the research supports is a *stable, legible character* that remembers, co-creates jokes, repairs small slips, and keeps a predictable rhythm — not a chameleon.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence for any frequency band given above (per-session counts are design choices, not findings).
- Searches exhausted before reaching: Bell et al. (1987) findings; Dunleavy & Booth-Butterfield (2009) stage results; Hall & Baym (2012) entrapment; Hall (2017) exact r values; Riordan (2017) emoji-as-emotion-work details; any sexting-specific emoji study; any read-receipt study; Pearson, Child & Carmon (2010) original results. A follow-up pass can target these by DOI.
