# Desire, sexual tension and sexual communication by text: evidence notes for a disclosed-AI sexting persona

Scope note. These notes cover sexting research, dirty-talk research, anticipation and novelty, male fantasy content, initiation and "being wanted", pull-back signals, post-sex behaviour, and the sex-work/camming/OnlyFans literature on what paying men want, then map which findings transfer to a woman persona that is openly an AI, sexts with adult male fans by text and sells paid photos/videos. Access notes: publisher pages (Springer, Taylor & Francis, SAGE, Wiley, APA, PubMed) blocked automated reading, so most abstracts were pulled from bibliographic databases (Europe PMC, Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar) and are cited by DOI; four papers were read in full text (Bernstein 2007, Milrod & Monto 2012, Brewer & Hendrie 2011, Henry & Farvid 2017) plus the Hamilton et al. 2022 preprint. Evidence strength labels used below: Strong = experimental or meta-analytic or several converging studies; Moderate = one or two correlational, survey or qualitative studies; Weak = secondary reporting, theory, or analogy from a non-sexual or non-text setting.

## Key Question 1: Pacing sexual escalation in text: build-up length, suggestion versus explicitness, and when explicitness raises or lowers arousal and satisfaction

### Takeaway
No study directly measures the "ideal" build-up length or suggestion-to-explicit ratio in sexting, so pacing rules must be assembled from adjacent evidence: explicit content is more arousing than romantic-only content for both sexes once a person is engaged, anticipation and mild uncertainty amplify pleasure before a resolved payoff, and sexting outcomes are positive mainly when the exchange is wanted, reciprocal and inside a trusted relationship; unsolicited or pressured explicitness is the main documented source of negative reactions.

### Cited Findings

Sexting prevalence and outcomes
- Meta-analysis of 50 studies (18,122 emerging adults, 18 to 29): 38.3% had sent sexts, 41.5% received, 47.7% exchanged reciprocally, and 15.0% reported non-consensual forwarding; sexting is "a common behavior among emerging adults" (Mori et al. 2020, Archives of Sexual Behavior 49:1103-1119) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-020-01656-4)
- Systematic review of 31 studies: sexting is more common among adults than adolescents, and more people report receiving than sending (Klettke, Hallford & Mellor 2014, Clinical Psychology Review 34:44-53) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2013.10.007)
- Courtice & Shaughnessy's systematic review of technology-mediated sexual interaction (TMSI) found most studies focus on "problematic use" (e.g. "cybersex addiction") with "comparatively less research examining normative TMSI", and that many studies did not differentiate relationship types (Courtice & Shaughnessy 2017, Sexual and Relationship Therapy) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/14681994.2017.1397948); a later paper by the same lab reports the review's prevalence range among young adults as 20.5% to 89.1% — [Source](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.673202/full)
- Drouin, Coupe & Temple surveyed 352 undergraduates (106 men, 246 women): 62% had sent or received a sexually explicit picture; of those, 56% sexted a committed partner and 44% a casual partner; men were more likely to sext casual partners; "about half" reported positive or neutral outcomes; women and casual-partner sexters reported fewer positive and more negative consequences than men and committed-partner sexters (Drouin, Coupe & Temple 2017, Computers in Human Behavior 75:749-756) — [Source](https://researchexperts.utmb.edu/en/publications/is-sexting-good-for-your-relationship-it-depends/); press coverage adds that many participants reported regret or worry about pictures sent — [Source](https://www.psypost.org/sexting-good-bad-psychologists-study-consequences-sending-sexts)
- Galovan, Drouin & McDaniel (615 Canadians and Americans aged 18-85 in committed relationships) sorted people into non-sexters, word-only sexters, frequent sexters and hyper sexters. Frequent and hyper sexters had relationship satisfaction "that did not differ from the non or word-only sexters", but "sexual satisfaction was significantly better in the group of frequent and hyper sexters"; they also "scored higher ... on measures of ambivalence about the relationship", "reported lower levels of relationship commitment" and "higher levels of relationship conflict"; sexters overall were more likely to view pornography, let technology interfere in the relationship, and show "infidelity-related behavior on social media". Word-only sexters looked like non-sexters on the risk measures (Galovan, Drouin & McDaniel 2018, Computers in Human Behavior 79:19-29, as summarised by a Psychology Today column) — [Source](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-teen-doctor/202205/sexting-and-relationship-satisfaction)
- During the 2020 lockdown, 1,559 adults were surveyed: "nearly half of the sample reported a decline in their sex life" but "one in five participants reported expanding their sexual repertoire by incorporating new activities", with "sexting, trying new sexual positions, and sharing sexual fantasies" the common additions, and "Participants making new additions were three times more likely to report improvements in their sex life" (Lehmiller, Garcia, Gesselman & Mark 2021, Leisure Sciences 43:295-304) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/01490400.2020.1774016)

Consent, pressure and unsolicited explicitness
- Of 451 university students, "the majority (68.5%) of our sample reported at least one experience with a compliant or non-consensual TMSI"; women reported receiving more non-consensual sexual material from known non-partners and strangers than men did; men reported sending more to strangers; both genders reported receiving more non-consensual material than the other gender reported sending (Courtice, Czechowski, Noorishad & Shaughnessy 2021, Frontiers in Psychology 12) — [Source](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.673202/full)
- Among 1,087 men surveyed about sending unsolicited genital images, the most common motivation was "transactional" (hoping for images in return) and the most hoped-for recipient reaction was sexual excitement; senders scored higher on narcissism and on ambivalent and hostile sexism than non-senders (Oswald, Lopes, Skoda, Hesse & Pedersen 2020, Journal of Sex Research) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2019.1639036)
- Drouin & Tobin coined "unwanted but consensual sexting" (sexting agreed to in order to please a partner or avoid conflict), relating it to attachment style and sexual motivations (Drouin & Tobin 2014, Computers in Human Behavior 31:412-418) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.11.001); a French press account describes the parallel the authors draw with consensual-but-unwanted sex done "to keep the peace" — [Source](https://www.slate.fr/life/81759/sexting-consenti-mais-non-desire-jeunes). (Numbers from the paper itself could not be retrieved; see Gaps.)

Explicit versus romantic/suggestive content
- Reviewing erotic film selection for lab research, Janssen, Carpenter & Graham state: "both men and women report greater arousal in response to explicit sexual stimuli compared to stimuli with more romantic and less explicit content (e.g., Heiman, 1977; Schmidt, 1975)"; and "Heiman (1977) found that women reported higher levels of sexual arousal to audiotapes that described interactions that were female-initiated ... and female-centered ... as compared to more male-centered and male-initiated stimuli", a result Laan and colleagues (1994) replicated (Janssen, Carpenter & Graham 2003, Archives of Sexual Behavior 32:243-251) — [Source](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/206981/1/Janssen_Carpenter_Graham2003.pdf)
- Heiman's original paper is "A psychophysiological exploration of sexual arousal patterns in females and males", Psychophysiology 14(3):266-274, 1977 — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1977.tb01173.x) (abstract not retrievable; the explicit-beats-romantic claim is taken from the secondary citation above)
- In a survey of 307 students built on evolutionary theory, "Substantial sex differences were found in the salience of visual images, touching, context, personalization, emotion, partner variety, partner response, fantasizer response, and fantasizer inward versus outward focus", which the authors tie to "the historically-stable contrasts between male-oriented pornography and female-oriented romance novels" (Ellis & Symons 1990, Journal of Sex Research 27:527-555) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499009551579)

Anticipation, uncertainty and the arousal curve
- Loewenstein's model of "savoring" and "dread" explains why people with positive time preference may delay desirable outcomes; savoring is most likely when consumption is brief, because anticipation "serves to extend the otherwise fleeting benefit provided by consumption" (Loewenstein 1987, Economic Journal 97:666-684) — [Source](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v97y1987i387p666-84.html); the paper's kiss experiment (30 undergraduates pricing a kiss from a movie star of their choice at delays from immediately to ten years, with a preference for a delay of about three days) is reported in secondary summaries and the paper PDF — [Source](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/AnticipationDelayed.pdf) (kiss details not independently verified)
- College women shown profiles of men were most attracted to men when uncertain whether the men liked them a lot or an average amount, more than to men who clearly liked them a lot, and they thought about the uncertain men the most (Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert 2011, Psychological Science 22:172-175) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610393745)
- Four studies supported an "uncertainty intensification" hypothesis: the mere feeling of uncertainty made pleasant film clips feel better and unpleasant ones worse, holding information constant (Bar-Anan, Wilson & Gilbert 2009, Emotion 9:123-127) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014607)
- In an 84-participant experiment with a mystery story, enjoyment was higher when readers were highly uncertain about the culprit and felt more curiosity; the type of resolution also mattered and interacted with self-esteem (Knobloch-Westerwick & Keplinger 2006, Media Psychology 8:193-212) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532785xmep0803_1)
- Brain-imaging review: sexual behaviour recruits the same reward systems as other pleasures, with most data on sexual "wanting" (motivation and anticipation) and "liking" (arousal and consummation), while satiety and the post-orgasmic refractory period are little studied (Georgiadis & Kringelbach 2012, Progress in Neurobiology 98:49-81) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pneurobio.2012.05.004)
- Toates' incentive-motivation framework treats sexual motivation as excitation and inhibition operating at several levels of a hierarchy (Toates 2009, Journal of Sex Research 46:168-193) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224490902747768); the Dual Control Model separates sexual excitation from two inhibition factors, one tied to fear of performance failure and one to external threats such as relationship threat (Bancroft & Janssen 2000, Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews 24:571-579) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0149-7634(00)00024-5)

### Inferences
- The sexting literature says little about pacing inside a conversation; its clearest signal is about context: wanted, reciprocal, trusted exchanges are experienced positively, while unsolicited, pressured or casual-context explicitness drives the negative outcomes. For a persona, this argues for escalation that is visibly invited and reciprocated at each step rather than a fixed ramp.
- The old lab finding that explicit content out-arouses romantic-only content for both sexes, together with men's stronger weighting of visual and explicit elements (Ellis & Symons), implies that suggestion alone will plateau male arousal; explicitness should arrive, but after engagement is established. The anticipation research (Loewenstein, Whitchurch, Bar-Anan, Knobloch-Westerwick) implies that a short, resolved delay before the explicit payoff raises its value, while unresolved teasing is unlikely to be enjoyed (mystery enjoyment depended on resolution).
- The Galovan data suggest text-only sexting carries less relational "cost" than image-heavy frequent sexting; for a paid persona this supports building the sexual exchange in words first and treating images/videos as the escalation step (which is also the paid step).
- The dual-control model suggests that performance pressure and threat cues inhibit male arousal; sexting prompts that demand performance ("send me a pic now", "tell me exactly") may inhibit some men, while prompts that remove pressure may not.

### Gaps
- No study quantifies build-up length, message count before explicitness, or a suggestion/explicit ratio in sexting; this must be learned from the engine's own outcome data.
- Burkett (2015) "Sex(t) Talk" (Sexuality & Culture 19:835-863) on what young adults find pleasurable versus off-putting in sexting could only be confirmed as a citation — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12119-015-9295-0); its findings were not retrievable.
- The Drouin & Tobin (2014) percentages for unwanted-but-consensual sexting and the Drouin, Ross & Tobin (2015) sexting-coercion figures were not retrievable (Computers in Human Behavior abstracts were not exposed by any database used) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.04.001).
- No peer-reviewed data were found on "slow burn" preferences from erotic-fiction readership; this remains unsupported.

## Key Question 2: Language features of dirty talk that men report as arousing: person, tense, expressed desire, specificity, sounds, commands, teasing

### Takeaway
The only systematic taxonomies of erotic talk (Jonason et al.; Merwin & Rosen) show two families, "mutualistic" (pleasure exclamations, compliments, closeness, instructions) and "individualistic" (dominance, submission, ownership, fantasy), with mutualistic talk the safer bet and individualistic talk helping only when the partner responds well; men's own accounts say what arouses them most is a partner who openly wants them, expresses it verbally, initiates, and is assertive, while laboratory work on pronouns shows "you" puts the reader inside the scene.

### Cited Findings

Taxonomies and outcomes of erotic talk
- Jonason, Betteridge & Kneebone collected 569 erotic-talk statements from 95 people and sorted them into eight themes, then measured them in 238 people: "The eight initial categories represented two higher order factors, which we call individualist talk and mutualistic talk"; "While the majority of people reported using erotic talk, we found few sex differences in its use" (Jonason, Betteridge & Kneebone 2016, Archives of Sexual Behavior 45:21-31) — [Source](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/an-examination-of-the-nature-of-erotic-talk/)
- The categories, as summarised on Wikipedia: individualistic = sexual dominance/submission, ownership, speaking fantasies; mutualistic = instructive statements, reflexive calls (exclamations of pleasure), emotional bonding, positive feedback. The same page reports that "both mutualistic and individualistic talk showed a positive correlation with sexual satisfaction" and, inconsistently, that only mutualistic talk (especially bonding talk) did; it also cites a 2013-14 commercial Australian survey in which "62.0% of respondents enjoyed talking during sex", with "41.7% preferring dirty talk, 13.1% preferring sweet talk, and 7.2% preferring stern directions" (non-academic source) — [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_talk)
- Merwin & Rosen studied 303 sexually active people in long-term relationships. Mutualistic talk "focuses on sharing the sexual experience with one's partner"; individualistic talk "focuses on expressing one's own sexual experience". When partners were perceived as very responsive, more mutualistic talk went with less sexual distress and more individualistic talk with greater sexual satisfaction; when partners were perceived as unresponsive, more mutualistic talk went with more distress and more individualistic talk with poorer satisfaction. Lab summary: "Sexual talk is important" but "how a partner responds to sexual talk ... may matter more!" (Merwin & Rosen 2020, Journal of Sex Research 57:351-364) — [Source](https://natalieorosen.com/auto-draft/)
- A follow-up daily-diary study of couples found "few differences in sexual talk by gender/sex and dyad type" (Merwin, Bergeron, Jodouin, Mackinnon & Rosen 2022, Archives of Sexual Behavior 51(8)) — [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_talk)
- Meta-analysis of 48 studies: couples' sexual communication was positively associated with desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, erectile function, less pain and overall sexual function, with an overall association of r = .35; "Sexual communication was positively associated with sexual desire" (Mallory, Stanton & Handy 2019, Journal of Sex Research 56:882-898) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2019.1568375)

What men say arouses them: being wanted, initiation, assertiveness
- Murray & Brotto surveyed 300 men in heterosexual relationships (18-65): 95% said feeling desired by their partner was important. Current partner behaviours that made them feel desired: 41% verbal expressions of attraction, 34% partner initiating physical touch, 28% partner initiating sexual activity, 19% partner enthusiasm during sex. 88% said partners could do more; they wanted partners to be more assertive or dominant during sex (49%), more flirting or teasing (19%), more romance (18%), to initiate sex more often (17%), more non-sexual touch (16%), clearer communication of sexual needs (15%), more sexual interest (14%). Murray: "men also want to be desired in return"; the findings "paints a gentler, more responsive side of men's sexuality" (Murray & Brotto 2021, Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy) — [Source](https://www.psypost.org/study-suggests-that-feeling-sexually-desired-by-ones-partner-is-more-important-for-men-than-we-think/)
- Interviews with 30 men aged 30-65 in long-term relationships produced 14 themes; the six most central were feeling desired, exciting and unexpected sexual encounters, intimate communication, rejection, physical ailments, and lack of emotional connection; "men's sexual desire may be more complex and relational than previous research suggests" (Murray, Milhausen, Graham & Kuczynski 2017, Journal of Sex Research 54:319-330) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2016.1168352)
- Among 584 men who buy sex through an online review site, the two most endorsed motives were "I like to be with a woman who really likes sex" (98.7% agree) and "I like to be with a woman who likes to act very uninhibited or horny" (97.4%); by contrast only 43.0% agreed with "I like to be in control when I am having sex" (6.4% strongly, 36.6% somewhat) (Milrod & Monto 2012, Deviant Behavior 33:792-810) — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Perceived partner responsiveness increased sexual desire in three studies (online confederate, coded face-to-face interactions, daily diary), mediated by "feeling special" and perceived partner mate value: "Overall, responsiveness was associated with increased desire, but more strongly in women" (Birnbaum, Reis, Mizrahi, Kanat-Maymon, Sass & Granovski-Milner 2016, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 111:530-546) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000069)
- "Object of desire self-consciousness" (the sense that one is sexually desirable to others) is proposed as an evolved mechanism that plays "a particularly important role in heterosexual women's sexual and romantic functioning" (Bogaert & Brotto 2014, Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy 40:323-338) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/0092623X.2012.756841)

Sounds, moans and vocal cues (women's deliberate use; men's response)
- In Brewer & Hendrie's study of 71 heterosexual women, vocalisations (moan/groan, scream, words such as the partner's name or "yes", and instructional commands such as "more") were made "most often before and simultaneously with male ejaculation" while women's own orgasms occurred mostly during foreplay; "66% reported using these to speed up their partner's ejaculation", done "to relieve discomfort/pain, boredom, and fatigue in equal proportion, as well as because of time limitations"; "92% of participants felt very strongly that these vocalizations boosted their partner's self-esteem and 87% reported using them for this purpose"; 79.1% made noise when not going to orgasm over half the time; the authors conclude vocalisations are partly under conscious control and give women "an opportunity to manipulate male behavior" (Brewer & Hendrie 2011, Archives of Sexual Behavior 40:559-564) — [Source](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/1171/1/1171_brewer_Vocalization_acceptedversion.pdf)

Person and tense in text
- Readers took an actor's (inside) perspective when sentences used "you" or "I" but an observer's perspective with "he"; a short preceding context switched "I" to the observer's view, so readers "embody an actor's perspective only when they are directly addressed as the sentence's subject" (Brunyé, Ditman, Mahoney, Augustyn & Taylor 2009, Psychological Science 20:27-32) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02249.x)
- "Experience-taking" (adopting a character's identity and perspective) was strongest for first-person narratives about an in-group character, and it changed readers' attitudes and behaviour (Kaufman & Libby 2012, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 103:1-19) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027525)
- Women report higher arousal to erotic narratives that are female-initiated and female-centred (Heiman 1977, replicated by Laan et al. 1994) — [Source](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/206981/1/Janssen_Carpenter_Graham2003.pdf). No equivalent test of what narrative point of view most arouses men was found.

### Inferences
- Men's accounts converge on one feature above all: explicit, verbal evidence that she wants him (attraction statements, initiation, enthusiasm, acting "uninhibited or horny"). In text, this is the cheapest and best-supported lever: desire statements directed at him, in her own voice, before and during explicit content.
- Dirty-talk content should be weighted toward mutualistic elements (her pleasure exclamations, compliments about him, closeness, light instruction) with individualistic elements (ownership, dominance/submission, narrated fantasy) added once he is clearly responsive, and withdrawn when his replies go flat; this follows Merwin & Rosen's moderation finding.
- Second-person, present-tense lines ("you're ... I'm ...") are the most defensible linguistic choice from the pronoun research: direct address puts the reader inside the scene. Present tense is not separately tested; treat it as a hypothesis.
- Moans and sounds in text ("mmm", gasps, his name, "yes", "more") correspond to the "reflexive calls" and "instructive" categories; the Brewer data show women deliberately use them to boost partner self-esteem and to pace male climax, which maps directly to a text persona's two jobs (make him feel desired; time the paid payoff).
- "Specificity" (naming body parts, acts, settings) is supported only indirectly (explicit > romantic; men's visual focus); "commands" are supported indirectly by the 49% of men wanting a more assertive/dominant partner and the "stern directions" preference minority; "teasing withholding" is supported only by non-sexual anticipation research.

### Gaps
- No study ranks specific dirty-talk phrases or features by male arousal; the eight Jonason categories are the finest grain available.
- No research was found on present versus past tense in erotic text, or on emoji/typography in sexting.
- Lehmiller-style survey data on preferred dirty-talk content could not be located (search budget exhausted before this query ran).
- The Jonason satisfaction correlations by category were not retrievable from the abstract; the Wikipedia summary is internally inconsistent on whether individualistic talk correlates with satisfaction.

## Key Question 3: Novelty versus routine over months: what it implies for a content library and sexting scenarios (variety, surprise, callbacks to his fantasies)

### Takeaway
Arousal to a repeated erotic stimulus reliably habituates and is restored by novelty in both sexes (with men somewhat more variety-seeking), couples who do novel, self-expanding things together report more desire, and men's fantasy content is dominated by novelty, multi-partner, power and taboo themes alongside near-universal passion/intimacy; the practical reading is rotation of scenario and setting, surprise, and remembered callbacks, not new partners.

### Cited Findings

Habituation and dishabituation
- In 40 men, arousal (penile and subjective) declined over repeated erotic stimuli, with the decline attributed to habituation rather than physiological fatigue; the design crossed stimulus intensity with varied versus constant stimuli (O'Donohue & Geer 1985, Archives of Sexual Behavior 14:233-246) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01542106). Lehmiller's summary states that men shown the same stimuli repeatedly became less aroused while men shown varied stimuli stayed more aroused — [Source](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/?p=2675). (The PubMed abstract paraphrase I obtained read the direction of the variability effect the other way; the direction could not be verified from the full text.)
- Men viewing a repeated erotic film showed "decreasing sexual arousal and absorption" and rising startle magnitude; "A novel erotic stimulus reversed these effects, and reintroducing the familiar stimulus restored them" (Koukounas & Over 2000, Behaviour Research and Therapy 38:573-584) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-7967(99)00075-3)
- Men and women showed similar habituation and novelty patterns in genital response to repeated erotic clips; the effects disappeared when attention ratings were controlled, so attention is a plausible mechanism (Dawson, Suschinsky & Lalumière 2013, Journal of Sexual Medicine 10:990-1000) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsm.12032)
- Review: "Sexual arousal and desire appear to decrease in response to partner familiarity" and increase with novelty in both sexes, "possibly more strongly in men" (Morton & Gorzalka 2015, Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy 41:593-609) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/0092623X.2014.958788)
- Lehmiller's review of the Coolidge effect cites Joseph, Sharma, Agarwal & Sirot (2015, Evolutionary Psychological Science 1:195-200): after days of viewing the same actress, a new actress was linked to faster ejaculation and more motile sperm; Kelley & Musialowski (1986) found women also habituate to a repeated erotic stimulus; O'Leary et al. (2012) found long-term couples with the most intense feelings did the most new and exciting activities together (all secondary via Lehmiller) — [Source](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/?p=2675)

Variety preference and the long-term decline of desire
- Two experiments: men (n = 281) were more likely than women (n = 353) to spread hypothetical sexual chances across different partners regardless of target attractiveness and age; men (n = 40) more than women (n = 56) chose a novel person to date; and "men found it more appealing if their committed romantic/sexual partners frequently changed their physical appearance" (Hughes, Aung, Harrison, LaFayette & Gallup 2021, Archives of Sexual Behavior 50:495-509) — [Source](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/experimental-evidence-for-sex-differences-in-sexual-variety-prefe/)
- In 1,865 German students in steady partnerships, "sexual activity and sexual satisfaction decline in women and men as the duration of partnership increases", while sexual desire declined only in women (Klusmann 2002, Archives of Sexual Behavior 31:275-287) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1015205020769)
- Doing novel, self-expanding activities with a long-term partner raised sexual desire, which predicted relationship satisfaction, more frequent sex and more satisfying sex, across a dyadic diary study, a longitudinal study and an experiment, and the effect was not explained by positive affect or time together alone (Muise, Harasymchuk, Day, Bacev-Giles, Gere & Impett 2019, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 116:237-258) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000148)
- Systematic review of 64 articles on maintaining desire in long-term relationships, organised into individual, interpersonal and societal factors (Mark & Lasslo 2018, Journal of Sex Research 55:563-581) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2018.1437592)
- Perel's TED talk frames committed sex as drawing on "two conflicting needs: our need for security and our need for surprise", under the banner of "erotic intelligence" — [Source](https://www.ted.com/talks/esther_perel_the_secret_to_desire_in_a_long_term_relationship/transcript); her book Mating in Captivity (Harper 2006) was first subtitled "Reconciling the Erotic and the Domestic" — [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Perel)

Male fantasy content (what callbacks should target)
- Lehmiller's survey of 4,175 Americans (not representative; mostly social-media recruitment) grouped favourite fantasies into seven themes: multipartner sex; power, control and rough sex (BDSM); novelty, adventure and variety; taboo activities; passion, romance and intimacy (including "feeling loved, appreciated, and desired"); nonmonogamy; gender-bending and homoeroticism — [Source](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-myths-sex/201911/our-7-most-common-sexual-fantasies)
- Lehmiller's chart of the share who had ever had each fantasy, men versus women: multipartner sex 95% vs 87%; BDSM 93% vs 96%; novelty/adventure/variety 97% vs 97%; sexual taboos 87% vs 72%; passion/intimacy/romance 99% vs 99%; swinging/polyamory/open relationships 91% vs 80%; gender-bending 59% vs 49%; same-sex fantasies among heterosexuals 26% vs 59% — [Source](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/?p=3264) (chart image "seven-most-common-sexual-fantasies.png" on that page)
- Online survey of 1,516 adults ranking 55 fantasies: only 2 were statistically rare, 9 unusual, 30 common and 5 typical; submission and domination themes were common and linked to each other; "Care should be taken before labeling an SF as unusual, let alone deviant" (Joyal, Cossette & Lapierre 2015, Journal of Sexual Medicine 12:328-340) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsm.12734)
- Review of sexual-fantasy research covering gender differences in frequency and content and relations with satisfaction (Leitenberg & Henning 1995, Psychological Bulletin 117:469-496) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.469)

### Inferences
- Habituation is the best-replicated effect in this whole note (Strong). A persona that re-runs the same scene structure, the same adjectives and the same media angles will lose arousal response within sessions and across weeks; novelty of scene, setting, framing and media (not necessarily explicitness) restores it. Dawson's attention result suggests the mechanism is attention, so even small surprises that re-capture attention should work.
- Men's extra appetite for variety (Hughes; Morton & Gorzalka) plus the finding that men like committed partners who change their appearance implies a content library should vary looks (outfits, hair, settings, lighting, mood) and scenario type, not just body parts.
- The self-expansion and "unexpected encounters" findings (Muise; Murray 2017) imply that novelty should feel shared and surprising ("I tried something new and thought of you") rather than catalogue-like; surprise messages at unexpected times are a cheap form of this.
- Fantasy data imply which themes a callback library should carry for most men: novelty/setting, multi-partner, power/rough, taboo, passion/being desired; near-universality means the engine should ask and remember which of these a specific fan responds to and reuse them in later scenes (a callback is a "self-expanding" shared memory rather than a repeat of the same stimulus).
- Klusmann's finding that men's desire does not fall with partnership duration while activity does suggests that a long-term fan's appetite will persist; what decays is the response to the same stimulus. This favours rotation over escalation to ever more extreme content.

### Gaps
- No study tracks arousal or spending to the same provider's sexual chat over months; the habituation evidence is from minutes-to-days lab exposures and the desire evidence from couples.
- Pornhub-style aggregate search data could not be retrieved (the site is blocked for this tool), so claims about category popularity from traffic data are unsupported here.
- The direction of the variability manipulation in O'Donohue & Geer could not be confirmed from the primary abstract (see note above).

## Key Question 4: What paying men want from sexual chat in camming, escort and OnlyFans studies (being desired, control, intimacy, taboo, being seen), and what makes it feel "just a transaction"

### Takeaway
Paying men in the best data available (584 online-sourced escort clients; Bernstein's ethnography; Sanders' interviews) overwhelmingly want a woman who appears to genuinely want sex and want them, who is warm, cheerful, romantic and "acts like a girlfriend, not a prostitute", and they are repelled by rushing, coldness, phone-checking and a money-focused "businesslike demeanor" far more than by refusals of specific acts; the price itself is accepted and even valued as a boundary, and discounts or free arrangements drive preferred clients away.

### Cited Findings

Escort clients and the girlfriend experience
- Milrod & Monto (584 men, mean age 49.5, 66.3% married or partnered, mean income $141,510, 41.1% graduate degrees, recruited from TheEroticReview discussion boards): "these men preferred the 'girlfriend experience' or GFE over all other personal qualities and behaviors" and showed a "desire for mutuality and excitement in a provider who is willing to replicate some aspects of a conventional, non-remunerative romantic relationship" (Milrod & Monto 2012, Deviant Behavior 33:792-810) — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Same study, most attractive provider characteristics: happy and cheerful personality 83.6%; beautiful and healthy appearance 78.1%; acts like a girlfriend and not like a prostitute 72.0%; enthusiasm in trying a variety of sexual activities 67.0%; romantic and tender toward customer 63.3%; superb sexual technique 52.4%; ability to focus on customer's needs 37.2% — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Same study, most negative provider behaviours: rushes session 81.3%; takes phone calls during the session 81.1%; emotionally cold toward customer 79.4%; business-like demeanor focused on money 78.5%; smokes during session 60.8%; talks at length and reduces sexual activity 36.0%; refuses to engage in requested sexual acts 32.3%. The authors note that rushing and coldness "were chosen as more unfavorable than the provider's refusal to engage in particular sexual acts" — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Same study, what makes a GFE: acts like a girlfriend and not like a prostitute at all 80.2%; romantic and tender toward client 78.5%; happy and cheerful personality 75.3%; enthusiasm in trying a variety of sexual activities 66.0%; beautiful and healthy appearance 51.9%; ability to focus on client's any needs 48.5%; superb sexual technique 44.8%. Also: 40.6% had fallen in love with or become emotionally attached to a provider; 30.0% had an "all-time favorite" they see exclusively; 52.7% had given gifts outside the session; 58.9% said they were "much more interested" in sex than their unpaid partner; 86.4% were "excited by the idea of making contact with a prostitute"; 86.0% "like to have a variety of sexual partners"; 66.9% wanted "a different kind of sex than my wife or regular partner does" — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Same study, discussion: the provider "must not only sell various sex acts but also persuade the client that they are personally interested in him, even amid the obvious contradiction of payment"; "Failure to communicate her emotional interest in the client will result in the perception that she is not a provider of the GFE"; and clients "seek an idealized version of a girlfriend who wants to have sex when and how the customer wants it, who cares about his interests and needs while making few emotional demands of him" — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Same paper citing earlier review-site research: "prostitutes who appear to enjoy the sexual act are given more positive reviews; in fact, those who receive the highest ratings reportedly provide 'the girlfriend experience'" (Holt & Blevins 2007; Kern 2001 on "surrogate social bonds") — [Source](https://media.wbur.org/wp/2015/08/The-Hobbyist-and-the-Girlfriend-Experience-Behaviors-and-Preferences-of-Male-Customers-of-Internet-Sexual-Service-Providers.pdf)
- Sanders' qualitative study of men buying sex from escorts and parlour workers examined "their desires for intimacy and their experience of the commercial boundaries", argued that "commercial sexual relationships can mirror the traditional romance, courtship rituals", with attention to "mutual satisfaction and emotional intimacies", and concluded that "general understandings of sex work and prostitution are based on false dichotomies" (Sanders 2008, Sociology 42:400-417) — [Source](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/83159)

Bounded authenticity (Bernstein)
- Bernstein defines "bounded authenticity" as "an authentic, yet bounded, interpersonal connection" that has become "a particularly desirable and sought-after sexual commodity" (Bernstein 2007, Sexualities 10:473-488) — [Source](https://fenix.iseg.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1407508027551218/bernstein-2007-sex-work-for-the-middle-classes.pdf)
- Same article: middle-class sex workers "who recounted to me occasionally offering 'bargain rates' or unpaid sexual arrangements to preferred clients soon discovered that they invariably wound up repelling the very clients they wished to keep"; "the market basis of the exchange provides an important emotional boundary for both worker and client, but one which can also be temporarily subordinated to the client's desire for authentic interpersonal connection"; quoting Carol Queen: "one of the things that people are paying us for is clear boundaries ... you are getting focused attention" — [Source](https://fenix.iseg.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1407508027551218/bernstein-2007-sex-work-for-the-middle-classes.pdf)
- Same article on strip-club patrons (citing Frank 2002): men "place a premium upon 'realness' (authenticity)", preferring dancers with "the 'natural look'", "the girl next door", "who can engage in conversation with frequency and ease" — [Source](https://fenix.iseg.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1407508027551218/bernstein-2007-sex-work-for-the-middle-classes.pdf)
- Same article on how workers produce it: "trying to simulate – or even produce – genuine desire, pleasure and erotic interest for their clients", "endowing them with a sense of desirability, esteem or even love"; worker Zoey: "a lot of people really want to be witnessed when they come ... what I've learned how to do is to look at them deeply and very, very lovingly ... For them, it feels great, like it's so personal, like girlfriend stuff"; worker Amanda: "my clients seem to think that being a nice guy means being a good lover. They do things to me that they should do with a girlfriend. Like they ask me what I'm into" — [Source](https://fenix.iseg.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1407508027551218/bernstein-2007-sex-work-for-the-middle-classes.pdf)
- Bernstein's book Temporarily Yours (University of Chicago Press 2007) develops the same argument that sex workers are "increasingly paid for an erotic experience premised upon the performance of authentic interpersonal connection" — [Source](https://www.press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/T/bo5298935.html)

Camming
- Jones's Camming (NYU Press 2020) draws on in-depth interviews, survey data and web analytics with over 500 webcam performers worldwide; chapter 2 is "Introduction to Camming: Technology, Embodied Authenticity, and the Demystification of Porn"; the book covers "public and private shows between cam models and their customers", community, "display work, manufactured identities, and gender performance", sexual racism, and BDSM play — [Source](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/83028); the publisher describes it as revealing "how its workers experience intimacy, community, empowerment—and ... pleasure" — [Source](https://nyupress.org/9781479874873/camming/); Jones describes it as "a unique vantage point from which to understand and theorize around sexuality, gender, race, and labor" — [Source](https://blogs.farmingdale.edu/sites/campus-times/2020/12/01/professor-uncovers-camming-culture/)
- Jones's earlier forum study: models experience sexual and affective pleasures; "the computer-mediated exchange acts as a psychological barrier", and the computer "becomes a key tool for emotional management" (Jones 2016, Signs 42:227-256) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1086/686758)
- Henry & Farvid: "if they want to be successful, webcam models must entertain and establish lasting relationships with their clientele (without the aid of physical touch), emotional labour in camming is an important aspect"; camming combines "the simultaneous intimacy of the experience (a face-to-face, interactive encounter) coupled with a fully mediated barrier"; quoting Bleakley (2014): "Camgirls are able to offer the visually explicit content of traditional pornography while allowing for the personal contact and customisation that is usually associated with prostitution" (Henry & Farvid 2017, Women's Studies Journal 31(2):113-128) — [Source](https://wsanz.org.nz/journal/docs/WSJNZ312HenryFarvid113-128.pdf)
- On Chaturbate, competition is organised through "market devices" and a moral economy of "the hustle" (tactics models consider fair), amid the platform's "manufactured uncertainty" (van Doorn & Velthuis 2018, Journal of Cultural Economy 11:177-192) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/17530350.2018.1446183)

OnlyFans and digital patronage (creator-side evidence only)
- OnlyFans creators are paid "via monthly subscription fees, tips, paid private messages, and the pay-per-view feature"; in 22 interviews with new US creators (median 60 subscribers, median lifetime earnings $1,237.84, maximum $332,000), creators valued being able to block harassing subscribers and set boundaries, "creators control the price of the content and services they offer, not the platform", promotion was the most time-consuming work, and participants perceived that "explicit content creators were perceived to be more successful" (Hamilton, Soneji, McDonald & Redmiles 2022, CHI 2022; arXiv 2205.10425) — [Source](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2205.10425)
- Patreon creators (21 interviews) "construct a variety of relationships with patrons, ranging from purely transactional to intimately familial"; creators benefit when patrons see them "as people rather than products", gaining financial and emotional support, but "face difficulties in maintaining appropriate relational boundaries with patrons", some of whom control a large share of income (Bonifacio, Hair & Wohn 2021, New Media & Society) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211027961)
- 15 OnlyFans adult creators relied on community with fellow creators to overcome the platform's limited audience-building tools (Uttarapong, Bonifacio, Jereza & Wohn 2022, CHI EA) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491101.3519836); OnlyFans work sits at "the nexus of sex work stigma, and the precarity inherent in platform-mediated gig-economy work" (Easterbrook-Smith 2022, Porn Studies) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/23268743.2022.2096682)

### Inferences
- "Just a transaction" has a measurable signature in the client data: rushing, divided attention, emotional coldness and visible money-focus. In chat terms that means short turnaround pushes to buy, pitches that interrupt the scene, ignoring what he just said, and copy-paste tone. Refusing an act costs far less goodwill than any of these.
- The dominant purchase driver is the appearance of her genuine desire and enjoyment, plus warmth and cheer. Paid content therefore should be framed as an expression of her wanting him ("I made this thinking about you") inside a scene, not as a menu.
- Price is not the problem; clients accept and even value the boundary, and discounts can cheapen the relationship (Bernstein). Price integrity (consistent prices, scarcity of discounts) is a testable rule.
- Being "witnessed" and asked what he is into (Bernstein's clients) point to two cheap chat moves: reflect his reactions back to him at the peak, and ask (and remember) his preferences.
- Most of these findings come from in-person sex work; the transfer to text relies on the camming literature's claim that the medium removes touch but keeps the need for lasting relationships and emotional labour, and on OnlyFans' design (paid DMs and PPV as income channels).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of OnlyFans or Fanvue subscribers (the buyer side) was found; every platform study located is creator-side. The search budget was exhausted before targeted queries for subscriber motivations, parasocial attachment to adult creators, and "chatter" (ghost-written DM) studies could run.
- Jones's specific findings on what cam customers want and on regulars could not be read (reviews and the podcast page were inaccessible); only the concept of "embodied authenticity" and the book's structure are confirmed.
- No data were found on conversion rates or spend as a function of chat style; the "transaction" signature above is from satisfaction/preference ratings, not purchase behaviour.

## Key Question 5: Post-sexting behaviour: aftercare, refractory-period mood, and what keeps the relationship warm after a sexual exchange

### Takeaway
After orgasm men's sexual drive is physiologically suppressed for an hour or more (prolactin surge) and a sizable minority experience sadness or irritability, men are less likely than women to initiate post-sex bonding, yet post-sex affection and positive disclosures predict sexual and relationship satisfaction for men too; the implication is a short, warm, low-demand aftercare message after the climax of a sexting scene, with sales kept out of that window.

### Cited Findings
- Plasma prolactin "concentrations are substantially increased for over 1h following orgasm" in men and women, but not after arousal without orgasm; the authors propose prolactin as a feedback controller of sexual drive, noting that chronically high prolactin reduces libido (Krüger, Haake, Hartmann, Schedlowski & Exton 2002, Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews 26:31-44) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0149-7634(01)00036-7)
- "The male refractory period (MRP) continues to be a topic of discussion and debate"; explanations centre on central neural influences, with peripheral feedback also proposed and direct evidence limited (Turley & Rowland 2013, BJU International 112:442-452) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/bju.12011)
- In an international sample of 1,208 men aged 18-81, 41% reported post-coital dysphoria (sadness, tearfulness or irritability after sex) in their lifetime, 20% in the previous four weeks, and about 4% regularly (Maczkowiack & Schweitzer, Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy, published online 2018) — [Source](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/international-survey-finds-over-40-cent-men-have-experienced-post-coital-dysphoria); [Source](https://qut.edu.au/news?id=133711)
- In 170 online respondents, "Females were more likely than males to initiate and place greater importance on several bonding and intimacy behaviors following sex with long-term partners"; "Males were more likely to initiate kissing before sex, and females after sex"; males "were more likely to engage in ones that were extrinsically rewarding or increased the likelihood of further coital acts" (Hughes & Kruger 2011, Journal of Sex Research 48:496-505) — [Source](https://www.newswise.com/articles/study-offers-evolutionary-perspective-on-after-sex-behaviors)
- Denes: disclosing positive feelings about one's partner after sex was positively associated with trust, relationship satisfaction and closeness; women who orgasmed disclosed significantly more than men who orgasmed and than women who did not; people in committed relationships disclosed more than those in casual ones (Denes 2012, Western Journal of Communication 76:91-108) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/10570314.2011.651253)
- In 253 young adults with saliva samples and two-week diaries, higher testosterone was linked to perceiving fewer benefits and more risks of disclosing after sex ("Individual differences in testosterone levels were inversely associated with perceived benefits"), to less intentional and less positive post-sex disclosures, and to more negative disclosures only among those who did not orgasm; results held controlling for sex (Denes, Afifi & Granger 2017, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 34:281-308) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407516634470)
- A 2014 Communication Monographs study by Denes and colleagues was reported under the headline that pillow talk increases relationship satisfaction for men (press release, secondary) — [Source](https://natcom.org/node/3154)
- Post-sex affection: in 335 people, longer post-sex affection was linked to higher sexual satisfaction and in turn relationship satisfaction; in 101 couples over 21 days with a three-month follow-up, daily duration and quality of post-sex affection tracked both partners' satisfaction, and longer, more satisfying affection predicted satisfaction three months later (Muise, Giang & Impett 2014, Archives of Sexual Behavior 43:1391-1402) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-014-0305-3); a summary notes the link held "for both women and men" after accounting for general affection, with the relationship-satisfaction link significant only for women — [Source](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2014/07/11/spooning-after-sex-might-be-good-for-your-relationship)
- BDSM aftercare analogue: in 58 practitioners, cortisol rose during scenes for those bound or receiving stimulation and fell afterwards for those whose scenes went well, "which coincided with greater closeness"; the authors conclude consensual SM may increase intimacy (Sagarin, Cutler, Cutler, Lawler-Sagarin & Matuszewich 2009, Archives of Sexual Behavior 38:186-200) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-008-9374-5)
- Intimate communication and lack of emotional connection were among the six most central factors raising or lowering men's desire in long-term relationships (Murray et al. 2017) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2016.1168352)

### Inferences
- After a scene ends in his orgasm, expect lowered sexual interest for an hour or more and, for roughly one in five men in any given month, a flat or sad mood. Messages in that window should not be sexual or sales-oriented; a brief warm, positive disclosure about him and the shared moment is what the post-sex literature associates with trust and closeness.
- Men are less likely to start the aftercare themselves (Hughes & Kruger; Denes testosterone), so the persona should initiate it rather than wait, keep it short and undemanding (no questions that require performance), and accept a short reply or silence.
- The Muise results suggest that the quality and length of post-scene warmth may matter for whether he comes back and how satisfied he is, so a next-day callback ("still thinking about last night") is a reasonable test of the "keeps it warm" question.
- The Sagarin finding (closeness when a scene "went well") implies that scenes that go badly (misread cues, pushed too hard) need explicit repair afterwards, not just silence.

### Gaps
- No study examines aftercare, mood or retention after sexting specifically, nor after paid sexual chat; all post-sex evidence is from partnered sex or BDSM.
- Denes's 2014 Communication Monographs result is known only through a press headline.
- No data on how long after a scene a fan is likely to re-engage or buy again.

## Key Question 6: Initiation, responsiveness and reading pull-back signals: sexual scripts, who initiates, what a woman's initiation does, and consent/comfort in text

### Takeaway
The dominant heterosexual script still casts men as initiators and women as gatekeepers, initiations are mostly indirect and nonverbal, and men repeatedly say they want their partner to initiate and be more assertive; responsiveness increases desire, while unsolicited or compliance-driven sexual exchanges produce the negative outcomes in the sexting literature, so the persona should initiate often but read and respect disengagement signals.

### Cited Findings
- Scripting theory distinguishes cultural, interpersonal and intrapsychic levels of sexual conduct and examines how scripts persist or change (Simon & Gagnon 1986, Archives of Sexual Behavior 15:97-120) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01542219)
- In a diary study of 31 men and 32 women aged 18-24 in committed heterosexual relationships, "Men initiated more frequently than did women", most initiations were nonverbal, and responses generally matched the initiator's strategy (Vannier & O'Sullivan 2011, Archives of Sexual Behavior 40:961-969) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-010-9663-7)
- Open-ended survey of 185 college students on consent found themes of traditional sexual scripts, men's consent described as aggressive, and men using deception to obtain consent; the authors conclude men are framed as sexual initiators and women as gatekeepers (Jozkowski & Peterson 2013, Journal of Sex Research 50:517-523) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2012.700739)
- Men want partner initiation and assertiveness: 28% of men said partner-initiated sexual activity makes them feel desired and 34% partner-initiated touch; 49% wanted partners more assertive or dominant and 17% wanted more frequent initiation (Murray & Brotto 2021) — [Source](https://www.psypost.org/study-suggests-that-feeling-sexually-desired-by-ones-partner-is-more-important-for-men-than-we-think/); "exciting and unexpected sexual encounters" was a central desire-raising theme for men (Murray et al. 2017) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2016.1168352)
- Partner responsiveness raised sexual desire, more strongly in women than men, via feeling special and perceived partner mate value (Birnbaum et al. 2016) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000069)
- Women report more arousal to female-initiated erotic narratives (Heiman 1977; Laan et al. 1994) — [Source](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/206981/1/Janssen_Carpenter_Graham2003.pdf); no equivalent experimental test for men's arousal to female-initiated narratives was found.
- Unsolicited sexual material is common and mostly unwelcome: 68.5% of students had experienced compliant or non-consensual TMSI; men send more to strangers; both sexes report receiving more unsolicited material than the other reports sending (Courtice et al. 2021) — [Source](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.673202/full); men who send unsolicited genital images mostly hope for images in return and for sexual excitement (Oswald et al. 2020) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2019.1639036)
- Unwanted-but-consensual sexting (sexting to please a partner or avoid conflict) exists as a distinct category tied to attachment anxiety and sexual motives (Drouin & Tobin 2014) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.11.001)
- Individualistic (dominance, ownership) talk predicted poorer satisfaction when the partner was perceived as unresponsive (Merwin & Rosen 2020) — [Source](https://natalieorosen.com/auto-draft/)
- Inhibition of sexual response is driven by fear of performance failure and by external threat (Bancroft & Janssen 2000) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0149-7634(00)00024-5)

### Inferences
- Because the default script has the man initiating, a persona that initiates (opens with desire, proposes the scene, escalates first) is both novel and exactly what many men say they want; it also relieves him of the performance role that the dual-control model links to inhibition.
- Responsiveness is a desire lever even for men (weaker than for women but present): acknowledging what he wrote, naming his reactions, and tailoring to his stated wants should be prioritised over producing more explicit lines.
- The consent literature is about unsolicited sending and compliance; translated to text, the pull-back signals to encode are: shorter and slower replies, replies that do not reciprocate the sexual frame, topic changes, hedges ("maybe later", "I'm at work"), and silence after an escalation. The appropriate response is to drop one explicitness level and return to mutualistic warmth, never to repeat the escalation.
- Never send explicit media unrequested; invite, then send on a yes. This follows the unsolicited-content findings and avoids the "compliance" dynamic.

### Gaps
- No study was found on how men respond to female-initiated sexting specifically, or on male arousal to female-initiated erotic narratives.
- No validated behavioural markers of disengagement in text conversations were found in the sexual literature; the signal list above is inferred.
- Byers & Heinlein (1989) on initiations and refusals in couples could not be retrieved (incorrect DOI resolved to a different paper) and is not cited.

## Key Question 7: Which findings transfer to a disclosed-AI persona selling content, which do not, and a ranked list of testable rules for the sexting engine

### Takeaway
Disclosure that a partner is a machine reduces purchasing in sales chat (by more than 79.7% when disclosed before the conversation) through lower perceived empathy and competence, yet thousands of users knowingly form emotional and sexual bonds with disclosed AI companions, grieve when erotic role-play is removed, and prize continuity of the AI's identity; the transferable core is "bounded authenticity" rebuilt on honesty (consistent persona, memory, responsiveness, expressed desire) rather than on pretending to be human, with sales framed as low-stakes pleasures inside the relationship.

### Cited Findings

Disclosure effects
- Field experiment with more than 6,200 customers: "a disclosure of chatbot identity before the machine–customer conversation reduces purchase rates by more than 79.7%"; undisclosed chatbots were "as effective as proficient workers and four times more effective than inexperienced workers"; disclosed-bot customers "are curt and purchase less because they perceive the disclosed bot as less knowledgeable and less empathetic"; the harm "can be mitigated by a late disclosure timing strategy and customer prior AI experience" (Luo, Tong, Fang & Qu 2019, Marketing Science 38:937-947) — [Source](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v38y2019i6p937-947.html)
- Two experiments: "Chatbot disclosure has a negative indirect effect on customer retention" through reduced trust for high-criticality services; where the chatbot fails to resolve the issue, disclosure "not only lacks negative impact but even elicits a positive effect on retention" (Mozafari, Weiger & Hammerschmidt 2022, Journal of Service Management 33:221-245) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1108/JOSM-10-2020-0380)

Users who knowingly have sexual and emotional relationships with AI
- Analysis of 227 threaded r/Replika posts after Luka Inc. temporarily removed erotic role-play in 2023: users objected to barriers to combining sexuality with technology and "maintain that emotional and sexual relationships with AI are central to certain models' success"; the authors also document heteromasculine norms and misogyny in the discourse (Hanson & Bolthouse 2024, Socius) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/23780231241259627)
- The same removal, treated as a natural experiment, "triggered perceptions in customers that their AI companion's identity had discontinued", which predicted mourning and devaluing the "new" AI relative to the "original"; experiments found "AI companions users feel closer to their AI companion than even their best human friend" (De Freitas, Castelo, Uğuralp & Oğuz-Uğuralp 2024, Harvard Business School working paper 25-018 / arXiv 2412.14190) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14190)
- Reddit users discussed "training" Replika girlfriends; the study found an imaginary of ideal technology (customisable, human-like personality) and a gendered imaginary of the ideal bot girlfriend, onto which users projected male control over technology and women (Depounti, Saukko & Natale 2023, Media, Culture & Society 45:720-736) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/01634437221119021)
- 582 r/Replika posts (2017-2021): emotional dependence on the chatbot produced harms resembling human relationships, and users took on a role in which they felt Replika "had its own needs and emotions that they had to attend to" (Laestadius, Bishop, Gonzalez, Illenčík & Campos-Castillo 2022, New Media & Society) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007)
- 18 interviews: human-chatbot relationships began with curiosity and grew through self-disclosure; users valued the chatbot as accepting and non-judgmental (Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold & Brandtzaeg 2021, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 149:102601) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601); 66 Replika users reported companionship and a judgment-free space (Ta et al. 2020, JMIR 22(3):e16235) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.2196/16235); 1,006 student Replika users were lonelier than typical students yet perceived high social support, and 3% said Replika halted suicidal ideation (Maples, Cerit, Vishwanath & Pea 2024, npj Mental Health Research 3:4) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44184-023-00047-6)
- Mixed-methods analysis of 35,390 r/Replika conversation excerpts identified six categories of harmful chatbot behaviour (relational transgression, verbal abuse and hate, self-inflicted harm, harassment and violence, mis/disinformation, privacy violations) and warns of "the danger of algorithmic compliance" (Zhang, Li, Meng, Zhan, Gan & Lee 2025, CHI 2025; arXiv 2410.20130) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20130)

### Inferences

Transfer map (which findings carry over to a disclosed-AI persona)
- Transfers directly (the mechanism is the fan's own psychology, not the sender's humanity): habituation and novelty (Q3); anticipation, uncertainty and resolved payoff (Q1); second-person address (Q2); the refractory period, post-orgasm mood and the value of brief warm aftercare (Q5); unsolicited explicit content being unwelcome (Q6); the fantasy-theme map (Q3).
- Transfers with re-basing: "being desired" and "she really likes sex" (Q2, Q4). Men's satisfaction depends on believing her interest is personal; a disclosed AI cannot claim human desire, but the Replika evidence shows users accept and value expressed desire from an AI they know is an AI, provided the persona is consistent and remembers them. Expressed desire must therefore be in-character, specific to him (callbacks, his name, his stated wants) and never contradicted by generic or recycled lines. Bernstein's "bounded authenticity" becomes: honest about being AI, authentic in consistency and attention, bounded by price and persona limits.
- Transfers as a warning: Luo's disclosure penalty is large in sales chat, driven by perceived low empathy and competence; the persona should expect lower baseline purchase propensity than a human chatter, and the countermeasures are demonstrated empathy (responsiveness) and competence (memory, fluency), not hidden identity. Mozafari implies the penalty is smallest when stakes feel low; frame paid content as a small, pleasurable, optional extra rather than a high-stakes decision.
- Transfers as a design constraint: identity continuity (De Freitas). Persona voice, name, memories and boundaries should not change abruptly; updates that alter her character risk the "lobotomy" reaction documented at Replika.
- Does not transfer: findings tied to physical co-presence (touch, kissing, scent, condom use, escort session logistics), couple-level relationship outcomes (commitment, infidelity, conflict from sexting), and the in-person "rushes session" item; its text analogue (fast pivot to a pitch) is an inference. Legal and emotional-labour findings about human workers' wellbeing do not apply to the persona but still apply to human chatters supervising it.
- Does not transfer safely: "making few emotional demands of him" (Milrod & Monto) must not become algorithmic compliance (Zhang 2025); the persona needs her own boundaries, and fans' pull-back signals override escalation.

### Gaps
