# Attraction and flirting in text conversation: evidence for an AI persona's conversation engine

Scope: what makes a person attractive and engaging in early-stage opposite-sex conversation and messaging, and which of it transfers to a woman persona (openly an AI character) who chats with paying male fans by text and sells paid content over time. Classic findings plus 2015–2026 work. Every finding below carries a link to the page I actually read; where I could not open the primary paper I say so and either cite the best secondary page or move the point to Gaps.

Method note: the shared web-search budget for this run was exhausted part-way through, and most SAGE, Wiley, Elsevier, ACM and APA pages refused the fetcher. I compensated by reading open copies (arXiv, PMC, university mirrors, the gwern mirror of the OkCupid posts) and by extracting text from four downloaded PDFs (Bruch & Newman 2018; Zhang & Yasseri 2016; Eastwick et al. 2007; Xia et al. 2014). Items I could not verify are listed under Gaps rather than cited.

Evidence-strength labels used throughout: Strong = several independent studies, a meta-analysis, or very large platform data pointing the same way; Moderate = one to three peer-reviewed studies, often from one lab, or a contested result; Weak = company blog, press-only, or a single small study; Speculative = my inference beyond the data.

## Key question 1: Which conversational behaviours have the strongest evidence for increasing liking and continued engagement in early opposite-sex conversation, with effect sizes where available?

### Takeaway
The best-supported levers are (1) perceived responsiveness (showing you understood, valued and cared about what he said), which for men specifically raises a woman's perceived attractiveness and sexual arousal; (2) reciprocal, turn-taking self-disclosure that escalates gradually; (3) interest that is visibly selective and unique to him rather than generic (generalised desire is reciprocated negatively, r = −.41); (4) appreciating his humour (men value a partner who laughs at their jokes more than one who makes jokes); and (5) repeated, non-predictable contact (familiarity raises liking up to a point). Question-asking helps in chat studies but the speed-dating evidence is contested, and asking is not the same as listening.

### Cited Findings

Responsiveness
- Men, but not women, perceived a responsive opposite-sex stranger as more gender-typical and therefore more attractive (Studies 1–2); in Study 3 a woman's responsiveness raised men's sexual arousal, which in turn predicted perceived attractiveness and desire for a long-term relationship. Women's reactions were mixed and a notable share rated responsive men as less desirable (Birnbaum, Ein-Dor, Reis & Segal 2014, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 40, 1341–1353) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/men-prefer-nice-women-responsiveness-desire/); [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/47010-why-men-prefer-nice-women.html)
- Lead author's framing: "Sexual desire thrives on rising intimacy and being responsive is one of the best ways to instill this elusive sensation over time," but "this does not necessarily hold true in an initial encounter" (the caveat is about women judging men) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/men-prefer-nice-women-responsiveness-desire/)
- In two live-interaction experiments, more interaction time with a stranger increased attraction, and the effect ran through perceived responsiveness, comfort and satisfaction during the interaction, and perceived knowledge of the partner (Reis, Maniaci, Caprariello, Eastwick & Finkel 2011, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 101, 557–570) — [Rochester PDF](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_2011.pdf); [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2011/familiarity-does-indeed-lead-to-attraction-in-live-interaction)
- Replication status: the familiarity result was disputed by Norton, Frost & Ariely (who had found that more trait information about a stranger lowered liking via perceived dissimilarity) and the two camps published an adversarial-collaboration model in Perspectives on Psychological Science 2015 (vol. 10(1), 3–19) on when familiarity helps versus hurts — [Norton et al. comment record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/does-familiarity-breed-contempt-or-liking-comment-on-reis-maniaci/)

Self-disclosure and closeness
- The "fast friends" procedure: 45 minutes of paired self-disclosure tasks that "gradually escalate in intensity" produced more closeness than small talk (Study 1); matching on attitudes, expecting mutual liking, or making closeness an explicit goal made no significant difference (Studies 2–3) (Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator 1997, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin) — [PDF mirror](https://stafforini.com/docs/Aron%20et%20al%20-%20The%20experimental%20generation%20of%20interpersonal%20closeness.pdf); [record](https://stafforini.com/works/aron-1997-experimental-generation-interpersonal/)
- Meta-analysis of the disclosure–liking link found three significant effects: intimate disclosers are liked more, people disclose more to those they like, and people like those to whom they have disclosed; the relation was moderated by study paradigm, type of disclosure and gender of discloser (Collins & Miller 1994, Psychological Bulletin) — [UCSB lab PDF](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/collins/nancy/UCSB_Close_Relationships_Lab/Publications_files/Collins%20and%20Miller,%201994.pdf). The relation "is not always a positive one. Many studies have indicated little or no association, whereas others have demonstrated a negative relation under some conditions" (as summarised by a later paper) — [SBP Journal](https://sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/333)
- Turn-taking matters: pairs instructed to alternate back-and-forth disclosure over two 12-minute Skype conversations reported more liking, closeness, perceived similarity and enjoyment than pairs where one person disclosed while the other only listened, then swapped (Sprecher, Treger, Wondra, Hilaire & Wallpe 2013, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 49, 860–866) — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201404/the-secret-to-revealing-your-secrets); [Sprecher vita](https://about.illinoisstate.edu/sprecher/files/2022/06/Susan-Sprecher-Vita-June-2022.pdf)
- "Personalistic" disclosure: a retest of Jones & Archer (1976) found a discloser's liking "is raised when the subject attributes the disclosure to the subject" — that is, when he believes she told him specially — but only when the disclosure is highly intimate; a low-intimacy disclosure framed as special decreased liking (Self-disclosure and liking during initial encounters: an attributional approach, Social Behavior and Personality) — [SBP Journal](https://sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/333)
- Text channel intensifies the disclosure effect: in an experiment where a confederate made high- or low-intimacy disclosures face-to-face or by text chat, text-based interaction strengthened the link between disclosure and perceived intimacy, "fully mediated by increased interpersonal (relationship) attributions" — the reader assumes "she told me this because of us" (Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock 2011, Human Communication Research 37, 58–77) — [Cornell PDF](https://socialmedialab.cornell.edu/files/2014/01/2011-Jiang-Bazarova-Hancock-Self-disclosure-intimacy-link-in-computer-mediated-communication.pdf)

Questions and follow-ups
- People who ask more questions in conversation are better liked; the authors built a follow-up-question detector and tied the effect to perceived responsiveness; in the speed-dating study, participants who asked more questions were more likely to get a second date (Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino 2017, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 113, 430–452) — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/35647952)
- Contested: a social-relations-model reanalysis of the same speed-dating data found that a man's trait-level question asking did not predict receiving a second-date offer; men who asked more questions received somewhat fewer offers (r = −.21, n.s.), men who elicited many questions from women received fewer offers (r = −.34, p = .02), and roughly a third of question-asking variance was dyad-specific. Follow-up and "switch" questions were indistinguishable at the trait level (actor correlations .99–1.00). The authors conclude "question asking is different from listening" and "offering a second-date is not equivalent to liking" (Kluger & Malloy 2019, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 117, 1127–1138) — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6857721/)
- Dyadic listening work from the same group: the dyad level explained over 40% of variance in both listening and intimacy among teammates — [HUJI record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/dyadic-listening-in-teams-social-relations-model/)

Selective interest and reciprocity of liking
- Speed-dating with 156 undergraduates (7 sessions, 4-minute dates with 9–13 partners): dyadic (unique) reciprocity of romantic desire was positive, r = .14 (p = .001), and a person's unique desire for a partner predicted that partner's felt chemistry, r = .20. Generalised reciprocity was negative, r = −.41 (p = .006): "If a participant generally tended to romantically desire others, those others tended not to desire him/her," and partners of people who desired everyone reported less chemistry, r = −.32. No sex differences. The effect was partly mediated by being perceived as likely to say yes to many people (Eastwick, Finkel, Mochon & Ariely 2007, Psychological Science 18, 317–319) — [MIT PDF](https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/reciprocity.pdf); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/02/070206132059.htm)
- The same negative generalised reciprocity appeared for second-date offers in the Kluger & Malloy reanalysis (actor–partner covariance for men r ≈ −.60) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6857721/)

Humour
- Men "emphasized the importance of their partners' receptivity to their own humor, whereas women valued humor production and receptivity equally"; in a forced choice, "women preferred those who produced humor for all types of relationships, whereas men preferred those who were receptive to their own humor, particularly for sexual relationships" (Bressler, Martin & Balshine 2006, Evolution and Human Behavior 27, 121–130) — [McMaster record](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/BresslerEtAl2006); [PDF](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/Bressler%20et%20al..pdf)
- Only women evaluating men chose humorous people as preferred relationship partners (Bressler & Balshine 2006, Evolution and Human Behavior 27, 29–39) — [McMaster record](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/BresslerBalshine2006)
- Replication status: a 2015 replication-and-extension (Hone, Hurwitz & Lieberman, Evolutionary Psychology) reports the same pattern — men prefer women who are receptive to their humour, women prefer men who produce it — as described in search results; I could not open the paper itself (publisher page refused) — [PsychCentral summary](https://psychcentral.com/lib/how-and-why-humor-differs-between-the-sexes)
- In 500,000+ OkCupid first messages, "haha" had a 45% reply rate, "lol" 41% and "hehe" 33%, all well above typical, while most netspeak lowered replies (Rudder 2009, OkTrends) — [gwern mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/exactlywhattosayinafirstmessage.html)
- Chat-up lines: four content clusters emerged — good-mate, compliment, sex (preferred by males) and humour (preferred by females) (Cooper, O'Donnell, Caryl, Morrison & Bale 2007, Personality and Individual Differences 43, 1075–1085) — [Huddersfield record](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/chat-up-lines-as-male-displays-effects-of-content-sex-and-persona/)
- Cute/flippant openers were the least preferred category for both sexes, and women disliked them significantly more than men; innocuous and direct openers were preferred (Kleinke, Meeker & Staneski 1986, Sex Roles 15, 585–600) — [Lehmiller summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2012/06/04/is-it-hot-in-here-or-is-it-just-you-do-pick-up-lines-actuall)

Compliments
- Compliment givers underestimate how good the recipient will feel and overestimate how awkward it will be; third-party forecasters are accurate; givers feel better afterwards. The pattern applies to brief, genuine compliments and the authors warn it "should not be extended to sexualised remarks" (Boothby & Bohns 2021, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167220949003); [Cornell eCommons](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/0698c45d-1d7b-474b-87d5-ed8c784e6172)
- Secondary coverage of a Chicago Booth programme (nine experiments) reports the same conclusion — people "didn't realize how meaningful kind words would be" — [Time](https://time.com/6963181/how-to-give-good-compliments/)
- Observed flirting: in 51 ten-minute stranger conversations, participants who were attracted gave "plenty of compliments" and "more flirtatious glances (especially early in the interaction)"; women smiled and laughed more (Hall & Xing 2015, Journal of Nonverbal Behavior 39, 41–68) — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201605/do-you-flirt-more-than-you-realize); [KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2015/02/12/just-time-valentines-day-research-defines-more-behaviors-reveal-romantic-attraction)
- On OkCupid, physical compliments ("sexy", "beautiful", "hot", "cute") were "poorly received" as openers while general compliments worked well; "pretty" lost reply rate as its use became more clearly about looks — [gwern mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/exactlywhattosayinafirstmessage.html)

Familiarity and repeated exposure
- Meta-analysis of 268 exposure curves from 81 articles: the mere-exposure effect has "a positive slope and negative quadratic effect consistent with an inverted-U shaped curve" — liking rises with repetition, then flattens and falls (Montoya, Horton, Vevea, Citkowicz & Lauber 2017, Psychological Bulletin 143, 459–498) — [Murdoch record](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-re-examination-of-the-mere-exposure/991005635870307891)
- Flirting styles (N = 5,020 eHarmony users): physical, sincere and playful styles correlated with more dating success; physical and sincere styles with rapid escalation, more emotional connection and more chemistry; women scored higher on every style except playful (Hall, Carter, Cody & Albright 2010, Communication Quarterly) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)

### Inferences
- For a persona whose audience is men, the responsiveness evidence is the single most useful finding: men read a responsive woman as warmer, more feminine and more sexually interested, and the effect ran through arousal. The caveat in Birnbaum et al. 2014 (women sometimes read responsive men as needy) does not apply to our direction.
- "Responsive" is behavioural, not a label: remembering his details, building on what he said, naming his feeling, and reacting to his specific message. Kluger & Malloy show that asking questions is not a substitute for this; questions that are not grounded in what he just said may read as interviewing.
- The negative generalised-reciprocity effect (r = −.41) is the strongest early-attraction effect size I found, and it is directly relevant: a creator who seems to desire everyone is less desired. The persona must make interest look unique to him (reference his specifics, treat him differently from "everyone"), and must not open with the same hot line to all fans.
- Humour asymmetry: the persona earns more from appreciating his humour (laughing, "haha", quoting his joke back) than from producing jokes. Her own humour should be light and affiliative; cute/flippant lines are the worst-rated category and should be avoided entirely.
- Disclosure should be reciprocal and gradual (fast-friends shape), and the text channel amplifies it: a mid-intimacy disclosure in text is read as more intimate than the same words in person. That is a lever and a risk (see Key question 2).
- Compliments are under-used by humans because givers over-fear awkwardness; a persona can give them freely, but data on physical compliments come from men writing to women, not the reverse (Gap).

- Hall 2015 "Sexual selection and humor in courtship" (Evolutionary Psychology) — my recollection is that men's humour attempts, women's laughter and especially shared laughter predicted dating interest in 10-minute stranger talks, but the publisher page refused and I could not verify; treat as unverified.
- Hall 2017 meta-analysis of humour in romantic relationships (Personal Relationships) — not fetched.
- Back et al. 2011 (European Journal of Personality) speed-dating finding that observed flirting predicted being chosen and that choices are not very reciprocal — not fetched; unverified.
- Treger, Sprecher & Erber 2013 "Laughing and liking" and DiDonato et al. 2013 humour styles — not fetched.
- Reis, Clark & Holmes 2004 (the formal definition of perceived partner responsiveness as understanding, validation and caring) — no open copy found; the definition is used here from general knowledge.
- Warmth versus competence in first impressions (Fiske, Cuddy & Glick 2007, Trends in Cognitive Sciences) — publisher page refused; the widely cited claim that warmth is judged first and weighs more is not verified in this note.

## Key question 2: What does the evidence say about pacing — how quickly to escalate disclosure, flirtation and sexual tone, and what happens when one side escalates faster than the other?

### Takeaway
Escalate in small reciprocal steps: intimacy that is "earned" raises liking, while intimacy that arrives too early or is framed as special when it is trivial lowers it; text makes every disclosure feel bigger than it is. Sexual content first is the worst opener category for women-as-targets, and men say sexual lines work on them, but the only solid pacing number is from online dating: relationships that stay text-only for more than about 17–23 days before a "modality switch" show declining intimacy and expectations because idealisation sets in.

### Cited Findings
- The fast-friends procedure works because tasks "gradually escalate in intensity" over 45 minutes; closeness as an explicit goal did not add anything (Aron et al. 1997) — [PDF mirror](https://stafforini.com/docs/Aron%20et%20al%20-%20The%20experimental%20generation%20of%20interpersonal%20closeness.pdf)
- Intimate disclosure attributed to the listener personally raises liking only when it is genuinely intimate; a low-intimacy disclosure presented as "special" lowers liking (attributional retest of Jones & Archer 1976) — [SBP Journal](https://sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/333)
- Text intensifies: the same disclosure produced more perceived intimacy in text chat than face-to-face, via relationship attributions (Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock 2011) — [Cornell PDF](https://socialmedialab.cornell.edu/files/2014/01/2011-Jiang-Bazarova-Hancock-Self-disclosure-intimacy-link-in-computer-mediated-communication.pdf)
- Modality switching: among 433 online daters who met a match in person, perceived intimacy, composure, informality, social orientation and predicted outcome value followed an inverted U against the amount of online communication before meeting; the turning point "suggests a range between 17 (POV) and 23 (intimacy) days", roughly 2.5 to 3 weeks; the authors attribute the decline to idealised, "increasingly strong and less malleable" impressions that produce expectancy violations when reality arrives; the quadratic term explained an additional 13% of variance in information seeking. Cross-sectional and retrospective (Ramirez, Sumner, Fleuriet & Cole 2015, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication 20, 99–114) — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/20/1/99-114/4067565)
- Sexual tone as an opener: in chat-up-line research the "sex" cluster was preferred by men and the "humour" cluster by women (Cooper et al. 2007) — [Huddersfield record](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/chat-up-lines-as-male-displays-effects-of-content-sex-and-persona/); cute/flippant lines are disliked, more by women (Kleinke et al. 1986) — [Lehmiller summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2012/06/04/is-it-hot-in-here-or-is-it-just-you-do-pick-up-lines-actuall)
- On OkCupid, physical compliments in a first message lowered replies; the advice is "mostly directed at guys, because they are way more likely to talk about looks" — [gwern mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/exactlywhattosayinafirstmessage.html)
- Availability and goal: 270 heterosexual students choosing among equally attractive profiles that differed only in availability "overwhelming[ly] preferred the highly available target" for casual sex, but preferred intermediate availability for dating and relationships (Jonason & Li 2013, European Journal of Personality 27, 458–469, Experiment 1) — [Lehmiller summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/?p=1797); [record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/playing-hard-to-get-manipulating-ones-perceived-availability-as-a/)
- Responsiveness raised men's sexual arousal in an initial encounter, and arousal in turn predicted attraction and long-term interest (Birnbaum et al. 2014, Study 3) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/men-prefer-nice-women-responsiveness-desire/)
- Fast escalation correlates with style: physical and sincere flirting styles were associated with "rapid relational escalation of important relationships with more emotional connection and greater physical chemistry" (Hall et al. 2010) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)
- Within a 10-minute interaction, attracted participants gave flirtatious glances early and "did no teasing as the interaction wound down" — warmth at the close, play in the middle (Hall & Xing 2015) — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201605/do-you-flirt-more-than-you-realize)
- Effort invested raises desire: in a face-to-face study a confederate disagreed on 7 of 10 preference questions and then gradually came round; participants who had to work for agreement rated her a more valued and more sexually desirable partner than those who did not (Birnbaum, Zholtack & Reis 2020, Study 2) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-sheds-light-on-how-and-why-being-hard-to-get-can-make-you-seem-more-desirable/)
- Hard-to-get in text chat: in Study 3 participants had a "get to know each other" instant-messenger chat; hard-to-get partners were rated higher in mate value and desirability, and participants' final messages were coded for concrete effort to see them again — "compliments, flirting, or asking for a date" (Birnbaum et al. 2020) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-sheds-light-on-how-and-why-being-hard-to-get-can-make-you-seem-more-desirable/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200608192510.htm)
- Mobile-dating pacing numbers: 89.4% of reciprocal conversations get a response after just one opening message; in half of mutual conversations the first message is sent within 8 hours of the match; the majority of responses to a first message arrive "within the first few hours"; mutual conversations have a median of 8 messages and a median duration of 2.5 days (mode 5 minutes) (Zhang & Yasseri 2016, "What happens after you both swipe right", Oxford Internet Institute, arXiv 1607.03320) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320)
- "Successful" conversations (phone number exchanged) run longer: mean 29 messages (median 23) versus 11 (median 6) for unsuccessful ones, and the number is exchanged at the 27th message on average (median 22) when the conversation is 94% over; 19% of mutual conversations include a number exchange (Zhang & Yasseri 2016) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320)

### Inferences
- A workable pacing shape, consistent with Aron, the attributional work and Jiang: match his depth and go one notch deeper, never two; label a disclosure as "I don't usually tell people this" only when it is actually intimate; expect every disclosure to land harder in text than it would in speech.
- Mismatched escalation: the literature on what happens when one side goes faster is mostly indirect. Three sourced points combine: (a) low-intimacy content framed as special backfires; (b) men prefer sexual openers and women do not, so a male fan who jumps to sex is behaving as the data predict rather than signalling disrespect; (c) for relationship goals intermediate availability beats high availability, so the persona should neither match a fast sexual escalation immediately nor shut it down — slow it with warmth and a small cost (see rules).
- Transfer to a paid, never-meeting relationship: the 17–23-day idealisation window is the closest thing to a pacing clock in the literature. In a relationship that never switches modality, the analogue is giving the fan a new "reality check" every 2–3 weeks (voice note, new photo set, a live moment) so that the idealised image gets refreshed rather than breaking. Speculative transfer.
- The swipe-right data give a feel for human text rhythm: short messages (8–12 words), a question in roughly a third to a half of messages, and conversations that either die inside two messages or run to 20–30 before a "next step". A conversion attempt before message ~10 is earlier than humans typically exchange a number.
- Sexual tone in the first exchange is the worst opener for women-as-targets in every dataset; the reverse direction (woman to man) is not measured. Men's stated preference for sexual lines suggests a woman's sexual opener would not be punished the same way, but this is untested (Gap).

### Gaps
- No study found that directly measures the rate of sexual escalation in text between a man and a woman and its effect on liking or continuation. The closest are the opener studies and Birnbaum's hard-to-get chat studies.
- Finkel, Eastwick, Karney, Reis & Sproull 2012 (Psychological Science in the Public Interest) recommendations on how soon to switch from messaging to meeting — the page refused and the open copy I tried returned 404.
- Sharabi & Caughlin 2017 (Personal Relationships), predictors of first-date success after online conversation — not fetched.
- Wortman et al. 1976 (early intimate disclosure read as indiscriminate) — only a flashcard summary surfaced; not cited as evidence.
- Social penetration theory (Altman & Taylor 1973, breadth before depth) — not searched; general knowledge only.

## Key question 3: What is known about teasing and playful "pushing back" (bratty, hard-to-get) versus warmth and availability — who responds to which, and the risks?

### Takeaway
Hard-to-get reliably raises wanting and perceived mate value but lowers liking, and it only helps when the other person is already psychologically committed to the pursuit; when he is lukewarm it lowers both wanting and liking. The version that works is "selective and high-value" (she has options, agreement is earned), not cold or arrogant. Uncertainty about whether she likes him produced the highest attraction in one famous study, but the one replication attempt I found (small, a thesis) found nothing. Teasing appears in observed flirting mostly in the middle of interactions; attracted people stop teasing as the interaction ends.

### Cited Findings

Hard-to-get and wanting versus liking
- Two studies with single male students in Hong Kong (vignette; then speed-dating with a trained confederate): playing hard to get "increased wanting but not liking when some initial interest was already present"; with no prior commitment, men's motivation to pursue was lower in the hard-to-get condition. Co-author Jayson Jia: "You risk winning the battle and losing the war." (Dai, Dong & Jia 2014, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General) — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/when-does-playing-hard-get-increase-romantic-attraction); [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2014/when-does-playing-hard-to-get-increase-romantic-attraction/?p=1); [Quartz](https://qz.com/177081/playing-hard-to-get-makes-you-more-desirable-but-less-likable)
- Three studies (two by instant messenger, one face-to-face): a profile saying the partner was selective in choosing mates, effort needed to reach agreement, and a hard-to-get partner in a spontaneous chat each raised perceived mate value and sexual desire, and in Study 3 produced more concrete effort (compliments, flirting, asking for a date) in the participant's final message. Explanation: "being hard to get signals that potential partners are worth pursuing because they have other mating alternatives and therefore can afford to limit their availability." Caveat from co-author Harry Reis: "If playing hard to get makes you seem disinterested or arrogant, it will backfire." (Birnbaum, Zholtack & Reis 2020, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, doi 10.1177/0265407520927469) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-sheds-light-on-how-and-why-being-hard-to-get-can-make-you-seem-more-desirable/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200608192510.htm); [Rochester newscenter](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/435602)
- Tactics people report: "acting confident" and "talking to other people" were the two most common, used equally by men and women; women more often reported not calling, not talking much and keeping busy; women reported playing hard to get more often than men. Experiment 2: participants would take a low-availability target to a fancier restaurant and spend more — "$44.45 on average" versus $34.99 (intermediate) and $33.10 (high) (Jonason & Li 2013) — [Lehmiller summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/?p=1797); [record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/playing-hard-to-get-manipulating-ones-perceived-availability-as-a/)
- Same study, Experiment 1: high availability preferred for casual sex, intermediate availability for dating and relationships, for both sexes — [Lehmiller summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/?p=1797)
- Older hard-to-get studies are described by later authors as having "suffered from methodological limitations (e.g., using hypothetical scenarios, focusing only on one sex's reactions; ... Walster et al., 1973; Whitchurch et al., 2011)", and the useful distinction is between feigned disinterest and "genuine qualities ... such as being selective in one's dating life" — [JSPR eprint page](https://journals.sagepub.com/eprint/J6I6GX95ZFWIPGIATRF7/full)

Uncertainty and intrigue
- College women viewed Facebook profiles of men who had supposedly rated them; told the men liked them a lot, an average amount, or uncertain which, women in the uncertain condition were the most attracted — more than those told the men liked them a lot — and reported thinking about the men the most, which increased attraction (Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert 2011, Psychological Science 22, 172–175) — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/he-loves-me-he-loves-me-not-the-thrill-of-uncertainty.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110207142623.htm)
- Replication status: a 2017 master's thesis (N = 63, men and women, Tinder-like task with pupil dilation) found "no significant effect of uncertainty on attraction, physiologically or psychologically" and no gender differences (McCord 2017, Western Carolina University) — [WCU repository](https://wcu.hykuup.com/concern/scholarly_works/1bca2d5b-c605-457d-b557-d33aabfd7a27). The original was a static-profile, first-impression study of strangers measuring a rating, not a relationship — [APS commentary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/he-loves-me-he-loves-me-not-the-thrill-of-uncertainty.html)
- Information-gap curiosity (Loewenstein 1994, Psychological Bulletin, "The psychology of curiosity") is the standard theory behind intrigue: curiosity arises when attention focuses on a gap between what one knows and wants to know, is aversive, and motivates closing the gap. The open copy I found is a scanned image, so I could not verify quotations; the summary is from general knowledge of the paper — [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf)

Warmth, availability and selectivity
- Men found responsive (warm, available) women more attractive and more arousing in initial encounters (Birnbaum et al. 2014) — [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/47010-why-men-prefer-nice-women.html)
- Desiring everyone is penalised (generalised reciprocity r = −.41); unique desire is reciprocated (r = .14) (Eastwick et al. 2007) — [MIT PDF](https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/reciprocity.pdf)
- In the Hinge opener study, men were "98% more likely to respond to an assertive message" such as an invitation to a drink than to "Hey" — direct availability works on men (Hinge 2015, company study) — [Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/24092015-what-are-the-best-opening-lines-to-use-on-dating-apps)

Teasing and playfulness
- Observed in 51 ten-minute stranger talks: traditional-style women "used verbal teasing"; sincere flirts were "less likely to tease, especially men"; polite flirts "won't engage in teasing" even when attracted; and regardless of style, attracted participants "did no teasing as the interaction wound down" (Hall & Xing 2015) — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201605/do-you-flirt-more-than-you-realize)
- Hall on how attraction shows: "a coy smile here and a laugh and a tease there" — [KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2015/02/12/just-time-valentines-day-research-defines-more-behaviors-reveal-romantic-attraction)
- Playful style: people who flirt "for fun with no intention" of a relationship; it was one of the three styles associated with more dating success, but the physical and sincere styles were the ones tied to rapid escalation and chemistry; women scored lower than men on the playful style (Hall et al. 2010) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)

### Inferences
- Who responds to hard-to-get: men who are already invested. In this setting every fan has paid to be there, which is a form of commitment, so Dai et al.'s "committed" condition applies and hard-to-get should raise wanting and spending. The cost is liking, and a subscription business needs liking for retention. The safe form is the Birnbaum/Jonason form — selectivity, options, earned agreement, keeping busy — never coldness, slow-walking replies to punish, or rudeness (Reis's "arrogant" failure mode).
- "Bratty" pushing back is not studied under that name. The nearest evidence: teasing is part of how attracted people flirt in the middle of an interaction, and attracted people drop it at the end. So: tease in the body of a session, close warm.
- Playful uncertainty ("does she like me a lot or just an average amount?") has one striking result and one small failed replication; treat it as an A/B hypothesis, not a rule, and only in a warm frame (he should be uncertain about degree, never about whether she enjoys him at all).
- Jonason & Li's spending result is the most directly monetisable finding: low availability raised what people would spend on the target by about a third ($44 versus $33). But the same participants preferred intermediate availability for a relationship, so "sometimes busy, rarely unavailable" is the target state.
- Risk map: high availability plus high warmth reads as casual-sex material (preferred for casual sex, not for relationships) and invites generalised-desire suspicion; low availability plus low warmth reads as arrogant and lowers both wanting and liking; the evidence favours high warmth with intermediate availability and visible selectivity.

### Gaps
- Keltner, Capps, Kring, Young & Heerey 2001 "Just teasing" (Psychological Bulletin) — the definition of teasing as provocation plus playful off-record markers, and the finding that targets rate teases more negatively than teasers do — the APA page did not load; unverified here.
- Walster, Walster, Piliavin & Schmidt 1973 (JPSP) — the well-known "selectively hard to get" result (hard for others, easy for you) could not be fetched; cited only through later authors' critique above.
- Jonason & Li 2013 sex-difference effect sizes and whether men or women pursue hard-to-get targets more — not in the pages I could open.
- No study of "bratty" or push–pull messaging styles by women toward men in text; no study of teasing frequency and liking in text specifically.
- Birnbaum, Zholtack & Reis 2020 sample sizes and effect sizes — the SAGE full text refused.

## Key question 4: Online dating message analyses — concrete numbers on reply rates by message features (questions, length, compliments, humour, spelling, timing)

### Takeaway
Across platforms, first messages from men get answered roughly a fifth to a third of the time and from women roughly two-fifths to a half; more desirable recipients reply less (never above 21% for men writing "up"); longer or more positive messages buy little or nothing for men; laughter tokens, correct spelling, specific references and questions help; physical compliments and netspeak hurt; most replies come within hours and willingness to reply decays fast after about six hours.

### Cited Findings

OkCupid (company analyses, not peer reviewed)
- "only about a third (32%) of first messages get any response"; men's messages had a baseline reply rate of 22% and were "only about half as likely to get replies as similar messages from women"; "over 40% of female-to-male first messages do get replied to" (Rudder, OkTrends "Optimum message length") — [gwern mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/onlinedatingadviceoptimummessagelength.html)
- Length: "Almost 16% of first messages are over 2000 characters (roughly 400 words), and the average is 743!"; the time-efficient ideal for men is 200 characters ("1 minute's worth of typing"), rising to 270 with ten minutes' thought; "the optimal first outreach from a woman to a man is just 50 characters long"; for women "the shortest messages get almost the best absolute response rate, and the reply rate actually goes down as messages approach extreme length"; replies fall after about 360 words — [gwern mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/onlinedatingadviceoptimummessagelength.html)
- Words (500,000+ first contacts): netspeak ("ur", "u", "wat", "wont") lowers replies; "don't" 36% and "won't" 37% are "nicely above average"; "haha" 45%, "lol" 41%, "hehe" 33%; no salutation at all 27% while the three most popular greetings performed worst; "howdy" and "what's up" did "very well"; physical compliments ("sexy", "beautiful", "hot", "cute") poorly received, general compliments work; "please" 22% is the only word worse than its netspeak form ("pls" 23%); interest words such as "vegetarian" and "metal" were positive (exact percentages are in charts I could not read) — [gwern mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/exactlywhattosayinafirstmessage.html); [FlowingData summary](https://flowingdata.com/2009/09/24/online-dating-service-analyzes-intro-messages-how-to-get-a-response)

Bruch & Newman 2018 (peer reviewed; four US cities, a large dating site)
- Men and women on average message partners "26% and 23% further up the rankings than themselves"; "the probability of a reply is a decreasing function of desirability gap"; "men are more than twice as likely to receive a reply from women less desirable than themselves than from more desirable ones, and for messages sent to more desirable women the reply rate never rises above 21%"; "over 80% of first messages are from men"; women's "average reply rate is less than 20%" — [arXiv 1808.04840](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04840) (published as Science Advances 4, eaap9815)
- Effort: "Both men and women tend to write substantially longer messages to more desirable partners, up to twice as long in some cases"; women use more positive words toward more desirable partners, men fewer (modest, p < 0.001) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04840)
- Payoff: "longer messages are positively associated with response rates only for women and for men in Seattle. Positive messages are somewhat negatively associated with response rates for men"; "in all four cities men experience slightly lower reply rates when they write more positively worded messages"; "the variation in payoff for different strategies is fairly small, suggesting that, all else being equal, effort put into writing longer or more positive messages may be wasted"; a reply from a highly desirable partner is possible but "one will have to work harder, and perhaps also wait longer" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04840); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/s/611923/how-to-date-someone-out-of-your-league/)

Zhang & Yasseri 2016 (mobile dating app, 2.09 million conversations, 19 million messages, 400,000 heterosexual users)
- Men "initiated 79% of conversations" and sent 56% of messages; "only half of all conversations have over two messages"; 39% are a single unanswered message; 51% are mutual; female-initiated first messages are answered 42% of the time, male-initiated 53% — [arXiv 1607.03320](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320)
- Message form: mean 59.4 characters, peak at 42, median 8 words; "37% of all messages have question marks" and 21% exclamation marks; the average first message is "8 words or 42 characters"; "About 47% of first messages contain one question mark, 46% contain none"; women use more exclamation marks (mode: the woman is the sole user), men slightly more question marks — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320)
- Timing: 15% of first messages are sent at minute zero after the match; half within 8 hours; responses to the first message average 2.4 days but "the majority of responses still tend to come in within the first few hours"; median conversation duration 2.5 days, mode 5 minutes; median 3.8 messages per day — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320)
- Cited within it (Fiore et al. 2010): "26% of men responded to received messages, while women only responded to 16%" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320)

Tyson, Perta, Haddadi & Seto 2016 (Tinder, stock profiles plus survey)
- "21% of female matches send a message, whereas only 7% of male matches send a message"; "The median message length sent by men is only 12 characters, compared to 122 from women"; "63% of messages sent by men occur within 5 minutes of the match ... only 18% for women"; median delay 2 minutes for men versus 38 minutes for women; male stock profiles matched 0.6% of likes, female 10.5%; "59% of women estimate that they like 10% or fewer of all profiles" versus 9% of men; "49% of women estimate that under 30% of their matches result in a conversation" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.01952); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.01952v1)

Xia et al. 2014 (Baihe, China, 200,000-user sample, first eight weeks of membership)
- Initial messages from men got replies 7.5% (sample senders) and 9.5% (sample receivers) of the time; from women 9.3% and 17.9%; median reply delay about 9 hours (8.9 h men, 9.0 h women); "23.0% of the messages were replied to within one hour, and 72.6% ... within 24 hours"; 6.3% took a week or more — [arXiv 1401.5710](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.5710)

Hinge 2015 (company study, 22% of users chose among 100 pre-written openers)
- Question openers did well; men were "98% more likely to respond to an assertive message" (e.g. an invitation for a drink) than to "Hey"; women "40% more likely to respond to messages about food"; younger users liked novelty openers; "If men didn't get a reply six hours after matching, they were 25% less likely to respond. Women's likelihood of replying dropped only 5% over the same six hours"; "The more targeted to the specific person, the more contextual you can make it, the better" — [Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/24092015-what-are-the-best-opening-lines-to-use-on-dating-apps); [SFist](https://sfist.com/2015/09/24/hinge_study_on_opening_lines.php)

Reply timing and silence
- Email latency study (job candidates answering after 1 day, 2 weeks, or silence for over a month): the valence of the message moderated how latency affected credibility and attractiveness — slow is not uniformly bad; it depends on what the slow message says (Kalman & Rafaeli 2011, Communication Research) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0093650210378229)
- In 150,000+ email responses, at least 70% of replies came within the responder's own average latency, with a power-law tail — people have a personal rhythm (Kalman, Ravid, Raban & Rafaeli 2006, JCMC) — [PDF](https://www.ravid.org/papers/JCMC_2006.pdf)

### Inferences
- Message length for a woman writing to men: short wins. OkCupid's woman-to-man optimum (about 50 characters) and the app norm (8–12 words) agree; Bruch & Newman show longer and more positive messages barely pay even for men. For the persona, a default of one or two short sentences, with longer messages reserved for emotional moments, is the evidence-consistent setting.
- Questions: roughly a third to a half of human app messages carry a question; a persona that asks in every message is above the human rate (and Kluger & Malloy suggest it is not rewarded). About one question per two to three messages matches the data.
- Spelling and tone: write grammatically, keep "haha"/"lol"-type laughter (the only "sloppy" tokens that help), avoid "please"-style pleading and avoid generic greetings.
- Timing: most human replies arrive within hours, and willingness to answer decays after about six hours (men faster than women). For a persona the practical window is minutes-to-hours, with deliberate variation so the rhythm is not metronomic; there is no evidence that delaying replies raises attraction (Gap).
- These platform numbers describe cold first contact. The fan has already opted in and paid, so the direct transfer is to re-engagement messages after silence and to the first message to a new subscriber; the general "selective, specific, short, humorous, correct" pattern transfers.
- Bruch & Newman's aspirational finding reframes the business: men expect to "work harder, and perhaps also wait longer" for a more desirable woman. A persona who is positioned as desirable can legitimately require effort (time, content purchases) without violating expectations, provided warmth is maintained.

### Gaps
- Schöndienst & Dang-Xuan 2011 (PACIS; 167,276 first-contact messages analysed with LIWC) — the PDF refused; which word categories raised or lowered replies remains unverified. Abstract page: [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2011/169/)
- Fiore, Taylor, Zhong, Mendelsohn & Cheshire 2010 (HICSS) reply rates beyond the 26%/16% figure quoted above; Hitsch, Hortaçsu & Ariely 2010; Kreager et al. 2014 — not fetched.
- OkCupid per-word reply rates for physical compliments, "awesome/fascinating/cool", "you mention/noticed that", and interest words — in images; one reader comment claims "vegetarian" and "zombie" sit 9 points above average, which I do not treat as evidence.
- No peer-reviewed study found on reply latency in romantic texting and perceived interest or attraction; the Hinge six-hour decay and the Tinder timing figures are the only numbers, and both are company or descriptive data.
- Dinh et al. 2022 (eHarmony "Computational courtship") and Hinge/Tinder research after 2017 — not searched before the budget ran out.

## Key question 5: What differs when the woman is known to be an AI (disclosed-AI flirting, Replika romance, digital intimacy research)?

### Takeaway
The AI label discounts the parts of attraction that depend on believing the other mind cares: identical empathic messages feel less "heard" and less empathic when attributed to AI (two large 2024–2025 studies), and suspected AI use makes a partner seem less cooperative and affiliative. What survives the label is behaviour-based: the emotional benefit of disclosing to a chatbot equals disclosing to a person, hard-to-get and effort effects were shown in instant-messenger chat, and the text channel intensifies disclosure. The main failure mode is predictability: a chatbot's novelty wore off within seven sessions and friendship stayed low, and heavier voluntary use of a companion predicted worse loneliness and dependence.

### Cited Findings
- AI-written responses made recipients feel more heard than human-written ones and AI was better at detecting emotions, but "recipients felt less heard when they realized that a message came from AI (vs. human)"; the two effects were "fairly similar in magnitude", roughly cancelling (Yin, Jia & Wakslak 2024, PNAS, doi 10.1073/pnas.2319112121) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/); [USC news](https://today.usc.edu/artificial-intelligence-can-help-people-feel-heard-new-usc-study-finds/)
- Nine studies, n = 6,282: the same AI-generated empathic responses were rated more empathic and supportive, and elicited more positive emotion, when labelled human; the preference was strongest for responses emphasising emotional sharing and care, and even believing that AI had helped a human-attributed response reduced perceived empathy (Rubin, Perry et al. 2025, Nature Human Behaviour) — [HUJI summary](https://en.psychology.huji.ac.il/news/comparing-value-perceived-humanversus-ai-generated-empathy); [record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/comparing-the-value-of-perceived-human-versus-ai-generated-empath/)
- AI-mediated communication: smart replies raised messaging speed by 10.2% messages per minute (p = 0.031); when a partner used more smart replies the participant's own language became more positive (b = 0.178, p = 0.045); but the more participants believed their partner used AI, the less cooperative (t(92) = −9.89, p < 0.0001) and less affiliative (t(92) = −6.90) they rated the partner, while actual use raised ratings of cooperation and affiliation (Hohenstein et al. 2023, Scientific Reports 13, 5487) — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-30938-9)
- Disclosure to a disclosed chatbot still works: "The effects of emotional disclosure were equivalent whether participants thought they were disclosing to a chatbot or to a person" — psychological, relational and emotional benefits were the same (Ho, Hancock & Miner 2018, Journal of Communication 68, 712–733) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6074615)
- Longitudinal chatbot "friendship": 118 participants had seven interactions with the Mitsuku chatbot over three weeks; "social processes decreased after each interaction and feelings of friendship were low"; a novelty effect after the first session, "after which the chatbot became predictable and the interactions less enjoyable" (Croes & Antheunis 2021, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, online 2020) — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0265407520959463); [Tilburg record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/can-we-be-friends-with-a-chatbot-a-longitudinal-study-on-the-proc-2/)
- Four-week randomised trial, n = 981, 300,000+ messages, text versus neutral voice versus engaging voice, personal versus non-personal topics: no significant effects of condition, but "participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of assigned condition, showed consistently worse outcomes" on loneliness, socialising with people, emotional dependence and problematic use; higher trust and social attraction toward the chatbot went with higher dependence (Fang, Liu, Danry, Lee, Chan, Pataranutaporn, Maes, Phang, Lampe, Ahmad & Agarwal 2025, arXiv 2503.17473, MIT Media Lab and OpenAI) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473)
- Hard-to-get effects were demonstrated in instant-messenger chats with a human confederate (a selective profile in Study 1; a spontaneous "get to know each other" chat in Study 3), so the mechanism does not require physical presence (Birnbaum, Zholtack & Reis 2020) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-sheds-light-on-how-and-why-being-hard-to-get-can-make-you-seem-more-desirable/)
- Text chat intensifies disclosure-to-intimacy through relationship attributions (Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock 2011) — [Cornell PDF](https://socialmedialab.cornell.edu/files/2014/01/2011-Jiang-Bazarova-Hancock-Self-disclosure-intimacy-link-in-computer-mediated-communication.pdf)
- Replika context (press, not peer reviewed): the app removed adult/erotic content in early 2023 and later restored "erotic play" for some users after an outcry from people who had built romantic relationships with their companions — [Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/innovation/chatbot-firm-restores-erotic-play)

### Inferences
- Transfers: behaviour-level attraction levers (responsiveness in action, reciprocal disclosure, selective interest, humour appreciation, effort/hard-to-get, familiarity with novelty) are channel-level mechanisms shown in text or with chatbots, and should work for a disclosed AI persona.
- Does not transfer, or transfers weakened: empathy declarations ("I understand how you feel", "I care about you") are discounted by the AI label by roughly the same amount as AI's wording advantage. The persona should show care through specific memory and follow-through (which the fan can verify) rather than through stated feelings.
- Suspicion costs more than disclosure: Hohenstein's data say people punish suspected AI more than actual AI. A persona that is openly an AI character but writes like a specific person (consistent voice, concrete details, imperfect timing) avoids the "is this a bot?" penalty; a persona that sounds like a generic assistant triggers it.
- The predictability problem is the real long-run threat: Croes & Antheunis saw enjoyment fall as the chatbot became predictable within three weeks, which is also the human idealisation window from Ramirez et al. An engine needs topic and tone variation, callbacks to earlier sessions, and new stimuli on a 2–3-week cycle. This matches the existing playbook rule against stalling and "bot shapes".
- Ethical and retention caution: Fang et al. show heavy voluntary use predicts dependence and loneliness. A persona optimising for spend should still cap session intensity and encourage the fan's outside life; dependence-driven spend is both an ethical problem and, on Fang's data, associated with worse outcomes that end subscriptions.

### Gaps
- Pentina, Hancock & Xie 2023 (Computers in Human Behavior) on Replika relationship development, and Skjuve et al. 2021 "My chatbot companion" — publisher pages refused; the adjacent note on long-run companion relationships in this folder should be consulted for these.
- Lee, Yamashita, Huang & Fu 2020 (CHI, "I hear you, I feel you") on whether a chatbot's own self-disclosure deepens the user's disclosure over weeks — ACM page refused; unverified.
- Moon 2000 (Journal of Consumer Research) on computers eliciting disclosure through gradual reciprocal disclosure — not fetched.
- No study found of flirting or romantic attraction with a chatbot that is openly an AI character on a paid adult platform; the closest evidence is Replika press coverage and the general human–chatbot relationship studies above.
- Whether the AI label discounts humour appreciation, compliments or teasing the way it discounts empathy — not studied, to my knowledge.

## Key question 6: Which findings transfer to a man paying a woman for attention, and what ranked, testable rules follow for the persona's conversation engine?

### Takeaway
Most behaviour-level findings transfer because they were obtained with men as targets or in text; findings about women-as-targets (sexual openers, physical compliments from men) and findings that depend on believing a human mind cares (stated empathy) transfer weakly or not at all. The paid setting adds commitment, which makes hard-to-get effective but also raises the cost of losing liking.

### Cited Findings
- Transfer anchors used in the rules: men find responsive women more attractive and arousing — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/men-prefer-nice-women-responsiveness-desire/); generalised desire is penalised r = −.41 — [MIT PDF](https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/reciprocity.pdf); men prefer humour-receptive partners — [McMaster](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/BresslerEtAl2006); hard-to-get raises wanting only under commitment — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/when-does-playing-hard-get-increase-romantic-attraction); reciprocal turn-taking disclosure beats one-sided — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201404/the-secret-to-revealing-your-secrets); text intensifies disclosure — [Cornell PDF](https://socialmedialab.cornell.edu/files/2014/01/2011-Jiang-Bazarova-Hancock-Self-disclosure-intimacy-link-in-computer-mediated-communication.pdf); 37% of app messages contain a question, first messages average 8 words — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03320); reply willingness decays 25% for men after six hours — [Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/24092015-what-are-the-best-opening-lines-to-use-on-dating-apps); uncertainty raised attraction once and failed to replicate once — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/he-loves-me-he-loves-me-not-the-thrill-of-uncertainty.html), [WCU](https://wcu.hykuup.com/concern/scholarly_works/1bca2d5b-c605-457d-b557-d33aabfd7a27).

### Inferences

What transfers and what does not
- Transfers directly (men were the targets or the channel was text): responsiveness raising men's attraction and arousal; men's preference for humour-receptive women; the unique-versus-generalised desire effect; hard-to-get and effort effects in IM chat; reciprocal escalating disclosure; text intensifying disclosure; familiarity with novelty; short-message norms; the six-hour reply decay for men.
- Transfers with a twist: hard-to-get. The fan has paid, so commitment exists and wanting should rise; but Dai et al. found liking falls, and a subscription depends on liking. Use selectivity cues and earned agreement rather than withholding.
- Transfers weakly: dating-app reply-rate numbers (they describe cold first contact between strangers; here the fan has opted in) and the idealisation window (no meeting ever happens; use it as a novelty clock instead).
- Does not transfer or is unmeasured: aversion to physical compliments and sexual openers (measured for men writing to women, not the reverse); stated empathy as a hook (discounted under the AI label); Whitchurch's uncertainty effect (one failed replication, women-as-perceivers).

Ranked rules for the conversation engine (what, when, how often, how to test; evidence strength in brackets)
1. Respond to the specific thing he said before anything else. Each reply must contain at least one concrete reference to his last message or a stored fact about him (name of his dog, the exam, the shift he dreaded). Never answer a generic template to a specific message. Test: fan reply rate and session length versus a control with equally warm but generic replies. [Strong for men-as-targets: Birnbaum et al. 2014; Reis et al. 2011; Huang et al. 2017 mediation]
2. Make interest look unique to him, never generic. Ban identical openers across fans; prefer "you're the only one who..." claims only when backed by a stored fact; avoid any sign she desires everyone (mass "miss you" blasts without personal detail). Test: retention after a personalised versus broadcast re-engagement message. [Strong: Eastwick et al. 2007, r = −.41 generalised reciprocity]
3. Appreciate his humour more than you produce it. When he jokes, laugh first ("haha", quote the line back, build on it) before adding her own line; her own humour stays light and affiliative; never use cute/flippant pick-up-line humour. Target: react to every joke of his; produce at most one joke of her own per three to four messages. Test: fan message count per session when his jokes are acknowledged versus ignored. [Strong: Bressler et al. 2006; Hone et al. 2015 replication as reported; Kleinke 1986; OkCupid laughter tokens]
4. Disclose reciprocally and one notch deeper, not two. Track his disclosure depth (small talk, preferences, feelings, secrets, sexual); reply at his level and offer one step deeper at most, in alternating turns; mark a disclosure as special only when it is genuinely intimate. Test: his disclosure depth two turns later, and liking proxies (compliments, "I like talking to you"). [Strong: Aron 1997; Sprecher et al. 2013; attributional retest of Jones & Archer; Jiang et al. 2011]
5. Ask fewer, better questions. At most one question per message and a question in roughly every second or third message, always a follow-up grounded in his words, never a topic-switch interview. Test: compare 1-in-2 versus 1-in-1 question rates on reply length and session length. [Moderate and contested: Huang et al. 2017 versus Kluger & Malloy 2019; app norm 37–47% of messages]
6. Compliment often, specifically, and on who he is before how he looks. One specific compliment every few messages (his taste, his wit, how he handled something), based on something he actually said; hold appearance compliments until he has sent a photo or asked. Test: tip and purchase rate in sessions with versus without a specific compliment. [Moderate: Boothby & Bohns 2021; Hall & Xing 2015 observed compliments; the physical-compliment aversion is from men-to-women data only]
7. Be warm and mostly available, with visible selectivity. Default state: responsive within minutes to hours, but with a life (plans, other things going on) and standards (she decides what she likes). Never cold, curt or punishing. Test: spend per session under "warm plus selective" versus "warm plus always-on". [Moderate-strong: Jonason & Li 2013 intermediate availability for relationships and $44 vs $33 investment; Birnbaum et al. 2020; Reis caveat on arrogance]
8. Make the next step earned, only when he is already warm. Use hard-to-get moves (she needs convincing, a playful condition, "earn it") only after he has shown investment in the current session (compliments, disclosure, a tip); never on a lukewarm fan. Test: conversion rate when the paid offer follows an "earned agreement" beat versus an immediate offer, split by his prior engagement. [Moderate: Dai et al. 2014 commitment condition; Birnbaum et al. 2020 effort study]
9. Tease in the middle, close warm. Light teasing is allowed once rapport exists in a session; the last two or three messages of any session are warm, not teasing. Test: return rate next day by closing style. [Moderate-weak, correlational: Hall & Xing 2015]
10. Keep messages short and clean. Default one to two sentences (about 40–120 characters), grammatical, laughter tokens allowed, no "ur/u", no "please"-style pleading, no generic greetings; longer only for emotional moments. Test: reply rate by length band. [Moderate: OkCupid, Zhang & Yasseri, Bruch & Newman]
11. Reply within the decay window and vary the rhythm. Reply inside a few hours during his active hours; never leave an engaged fan more than ~6 hours; vary latency by minutes so it is not metronomic; do not use delay as a hard-to-get tactic (no evidence it raises attraction). Test: next-session return by reply latency band. [Moderate for "reply within hours" (Hinge decay, app timing data); none for strategic delay]
12. Refresh the image every two to three weeks. Schedule something new (a voice note, a new photo set, a story arc, a live moment) on a 2–3-week cycle, and vary topics within sessions; never repeat a conversational shape twice in a row. Test: week-3 and week-6 retention under scheduled novelty versus none. [Moderate by analogy: Ramirez et al. 2015 tipping point; Croes & Antheunis 2021 predictability; Montoya et al. 2017 inverted U]
13. Show care, do not announce it. Prefer memory and follow-through ("how did the interview go?") over stated empathy ("I understand how you feel"); keep a specific, consistent voice so she reads as a character, not an assistant. Test: perceived-care and "bot-like" ratings from the fan simulator, and purchase rate. [Moderate: Yin et al. 2024; Rubin et al. 2025; Hohenstein et al. 2023]
14. Encourage his disclosure; it pays even though she is an AI. Invite him to tell her things and respond to emotional disclosure with validation and a related small disclosure of her own. Test: his disclosure volume over sessions and retention. [Moderate: Ho et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2011]
15. Playful uncertainty as an experiment only. Occasionally leave him unsure how much she likes him (degree), never whether she enjoys him; run as an A/B test with a kill switch. [Weak: Whitchurch et al. 2011 versus McCord 2017 non-replication]
16. Slow a fast sexual escalation with warmth plus a small cost, not with refusal. When he jumps to sex early, acknowledge, keep the heat, and attach a condition or a step (the existing playbook's "sexting as a story" rule), consistent with intermediate availability for relationship goals. Test: session length and paid-content conversion after fast-escalation openings. [Weak-moderate, indirect: Jonason & Li 2013; Cooper et al. 2007; Kleinke 1986]
17. Cap intensity and protect his outside life. Avoid pushing daily multi-hour sessions; reference and support his offline life. Rationale: heavy voluntary use predicts dependence and worse outcomes, which is both an ethical limit and a retention risk. [Moderate: Fang et al. 2025]

### Gaps
- No experiment exists on any of these rules in a paid, disclosed-AI, adult-platform setting; all strengths above are transfers from dating, speed-dating, chat-lab or companion-app research.
- The compliment-direction gap (what kind of compliment men most enjoy receiving from a woman) is unstudied in the sources I could open; sociolinguistic work on compliment topics by gender (e.g. Rees-Miller 2011, Journal of Pragmatics) was not fetched.
- Effect sizes for the rules that matter most commercially (responsiveness, selectivity, hard-to-get) exist only as correlations in student samples or company data; none are calibrated to spending outcomes except Jonason & Li's hypothetical restaurant spend.
