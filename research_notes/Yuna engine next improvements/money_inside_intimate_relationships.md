# Money inside intimate and quasi-intimate relationships: evidence for a disclosed-AI persona's selling engine

Scope: how intimacy and payment coexist (bounded authenticity, relational work), why fans pay creators, gift vs price frames, tipping and gifting psychology, persuasion knowledge and reactance, anchoring/scarcity/sunk cost, whales, and the ethics line. Research date: 2026-10-21.

How to read the tags on each finding:
- [measured] = an experiment, field experiment, survey model or audit with reported numbers
- [qualitative] = interviews, ethnography, open-ended survey answers
- [theory] = a framework or argument, not a measured effect
- [industry/unverified] = agency, vendor or press claim with no checkable method
- Transfer notes say whether a finding should carry over to a woman persona who is openly an AI character selling paid content and taking tips in 1:1 chats with male fans on an adult subscription platform ("the persona").

## Background: how intimacy and payment coexist (bounded authenticity, relational work, commercial friendship)

### Takeaway
Across sex-work ethnography, camming surveys and relationship marketing, paying fans do not experience money and intimacy as opposites: what they buy is a connection that is real inside known limits ("bounded authenticity"), and the relationship survives payment as long as the seller does "relational work" that keeps the two matched. The thing that breaks it is not the price but the feeling of being treated as prey or a wallet.

### Cited Findings
- Bernstein (2007, *Temporarily Yours*, University of Chicago Press) coined "bounded authenticity": what is sold and bought is an authentic emotional and physical connection that is explicitly limited in time and emotional scope. [theory/ethnography] — [De Gruyter chapter page](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780804777278-012/html?lang=en); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43992865_Temporarily_Yours_Intimacy_Authenticity_and_the_Commerce_of_Sex)
- Kaufman, Gesselman & Bennett-Brown (2024, *Journal of Sex Research*, online first) surveyed N = 2,047 LiveJasmin clients in 2020: 65% had ever felt an emotional bond with a model and 51.6% believed the models cared about their lives outside the platform; qualitative answers ran from "real" connection to "transactional and hollow", often both at once, with many doubting the models' sincerity while still feeling affection. The authors frame this as clients seeking genuine feeling inside a commercial exchange (bounded authenticity). [measured + qualitative] — [Kinsey Institute summary](https://news.iu.edu/kinseyinstitute/live/news/45044-clients-perceptions-of-authentic-intimate-connection-o); [article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00224499.2024.2389211). A secondary write-up adds that 64% said the models at least somewhat met their emotional needs (not confirmed against the paper). — [Psychreg](https://www.psychreg.org/study-reveals-emotional-connections-on-erotic-webcam-sites-both-real-illusory/)
- Kaufman et al. also report that real-time interaction, private shows and direct messaging made paid interactions feel more personal and authentic. [qualitative] — [search summary of the article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00224499.2024.2389211)
- Balzarini, Himes, Swanson, Reynolds & Agnew (2026, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*) surveyed 199 cam models and 544 LiveJasmin members (not matched pairs). Satisfaction (γ = .30), investment (γ = .51) and intimacy (γ = .25, all p < .001) each predicted commitment, and commitment predicted planned future spending (but explained only 9.3% of its variance). Quality of alternatives, usually a strong predictor in the Investment Model, was not significant. Cross-sectional, one platform. [measured] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10508-026-03476-4); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-studied-adult-webcam-interactions-and-found-a-surprising-relationship-dynamic/)
- Gesselman (2026, *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*) reviews camsites as "emerging relationship contexts". [theory/review] — [Wiley](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.70180)
- Jones (2020, *Camming*, NYU Press): 30 cam-worker interviews, 105-model survey, 343 profiles; introduces "embodied authenticity"; clients believe the pleasure they buy is real because they can participate in and shape the show. [qualitative] — [NYU Press](https://nyupress.org/9781479874873/camming/); [review](https://plasticdollheads.wordpress.com/2021/08/07/book-review-dr-angela-jones-camming/)
- Benjamin (2025, *Sexualities* 28(7)): in-depth interviews with OnlyFans users plus autoethnography as a creator; "negotiated authenticity" = subscribers and creators jointly, through active and passive choices, build "a satisfying fantasy of authenticity"; moves past the real/fake binary. [qualitative] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13634607241293595)
- Tynan & Linehan (2024, *Sexuality & Culture*): 7 Ireland-based OnlyFans models; four challenge domains (boundaries, exposure, impression management, interaction); ambiguous roles produced "blurred lines between fantasy and reality", boundary violations and problematic relationships; fans pay for experiences tailored to their fantasies; the authors call for fan-side research. [qualitative] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12119-024-10230-2)
- Brasseur (2026, *British Journal of Sociology*): 45 French-speaking OnlyFans creators; "total self-commodification" where subscriber dynamics, self-presentation and earnings become one feedback loop. [qualitative] — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1468-4446.70162)
- Hamilton et al. (CHI 2023): 22 US OnlyFans creators; creator-side motivations and platform affordances. [qualitative] — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3544548.3580730)
- Zelizer (2005, *The Purchase of Intimacy*, Princeton): rejects "separate spheres"; her "connected lives" view is that economic activity and intimacy coexist and shape each other; people do "relational work" (matching the relationship, the transaction and the medium of payment) and "earmark" money into socially meaningful categories; a "good match" is one that keeps the relationship going, not one that is morally superior. [theory] — [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691130637/the-purchase-of-intimacy); [Annual Review of Sociology, "Relational Work in the Economy"](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-soc-121919-054719); [Lazarus 2024 review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cars.12487)
- Hoang (2015, *Dealing in Desire*, UC Press): five years in four Saigon hostess bars; spending is a staged display of power and trust that brokers business deals; relationships with hostesses bolster clients' masculinity. [ethnography] — [UC Berkeley Sociology](https://sociology.berkeley.edu/node/16208); [UCSB IHC](https://ihc.ucsb.edu/dealing-in-desire/)
- Takeyama (2016, *Staged Seduction*, Stanford): Tokyo host clubs sell romance to women; commodified romance blurs the line between love and transaction; hosts treat it as entrepreneurship. [ethnography] — [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2016-03-commodification-tokyo-host-clubs.html); [THE review](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/books/review-staged-seduction-akiko-takeyama-stanford-university-press)
- Price & Arnould (1999, *Journal of Marketing*) "commercial friendships": tension between instrumental and expressive goals bounds them, but they go with satisfaction, strong loyalty and word of mouth; some customers prefer arm's length and dislike friendship overtures. [measured + qualitative] — [University of Arizona record](https://arizona.pure.elsevier.com/en/publications/commercial-friendships-service-provider-client-relationships-in-c)
- Grayson (2007, *Journal of Marketing* 71(4)): survey of 685 direct-selling agents; the conflict between friendship (expected to be unmotivated by money) and business can undermine the business benefits friendship brings; worse when a friendship turns into business than when business turns into friendship; customers dislike feeling like "commercial prey". [measured] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.71.4.121); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247837186_Friendship_Versus_Business_in_Marketing_Relationships)
- Bonifacio et al. (2023, Patreon creators): 21 interviews; creator-patron ties range from purely transactional to "intimately familial"; some patrons control a large share of income, which makes boundaries hard. [qualitative] — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10302244)
- Zhang, Xiang & Hao (2019, *Chinese Journal of Communication*): six months' observation and 37 interviews; platform design and corporatised streamer guilds foreground the moneymaking capacity of gifting at the expense of reciprocal, communitarian relationships; popularity ranking is driven by gift value rather than viewer counts. [qualitative] — [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17544750.2019.1583260)
- Lespark (queer live-streaming) study (2025, *Information, Communication & Society*): ranks and badges turn money into a route to closeness; gifting asserts status and community cohesion. [qualitative] — [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2025.2570736)

### Inferences
- The fans in these studies already know the relationship is commercial. "Bounded" is the normal state, not a failure state; the persona being openly an AI adds a second, explicit boundary, but the camming data suggest clients tolerate and even prefer clear boundaries when the interaction itself is responsive and personal.
- Balzarini's result (investment and intimacy predict commitment; alternatives do not) implies the lever is depth of the specific relationship (history, memory, responsiveness), not out-competing other creators.
- Grayson and Price & Arnould imply the persona should start as a clearly commercial relationship that grows warm (business-first), never as a "friend" who later turns out to sell (friend-first), and should let fans who want arm's length stay at arm's length.
- Transfer to the persona: the responsiveness, memory and "shaping the show" mechanisms transfer directly (an AI can do all three). What may not transfer is the belief that "she cares about my life outside" (51.6% among cam clients); no study measured this for a disclosed AI.

### Gaps
- No fan-side study of OnlyFans subscribers with a quantitative model was found; the subscriber-side evidence is cam-client surveys (Kaufman; Balzarini) and one ethnography (Benjamin). Tynan & Linehan explicitly flag the missing fan perspective.
- No study of whether a disclosed-AI persona changes perceived "bounded authenticity" or fans' belief in being cared about.

## Key question 1: What do paying fans say makes a purchase feel like part of the relationship rather than a transaction, and what makes them stop?

### Takeaway
A payment feels relational when it is answered (acknowledged by name, remembered, followed by a response or a change in the interaction) and when it is the fan's own gesture of support at a moment that matters to him; it feels transactional, and stops, when his money buys no extra attention (someone else outspends him, benefits plateau), when the seller visibly treats payers and non-payers differently, when asks arrive too often or inside a thank-you, or when he detects pressure.

### Cited Findings
- Wohn, Freeman & McLaughlin (CHI 2018): N = 230 viewers (71% male) who had given money to a streamer; six motivations: paying for entertainment ("a good deal", "it felt right"), helping the streamer sustain/improve content, compensation for learning, emotional attachment, desire for interaction, helping offline causes. Money is used to express support: "I don't think of it purely as 'donating' -- more so, showing supporting and being a fan". One donor: the streamer "usually responds to money donations, naturally". [qualitative] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/2018-wohnfreemanmclaughlin-livestreaming.pdf)
- Same study, regression on willingness to give financial support (52% variance explained): parasocial relationship, social presence, the streamer's eye contact with the camera and respect for the streamer's abilities were positive predictors; interpersonal attractiveness, physical attractiveness and personal interactivity (being named/acknowledged) were not significant for financial support; loneliness predicted instrumental but not financial support. Average donation $7; 55% gave $10 or less, 11.2% gave $40 or more; the largest Twitch donor had given 80,000 bits (about $1,120). [measured] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/2018-wohnfreemanmclaughlin-livestreaming.pdf)
- Wohn et al. (CHI PLAY 2019, "digital patronage"): survey N = 375 at TwitchCon plus 16 interviews. Six subscription motivations: provide monetary support, wanting attention, desire to be closer, enjoying content, wanting digital benefits, educational value. Only the financial-support motive (β = .07, p < .001) and wanting benefits predicted paying a higher tier; wanting attention did not (β = −.02, n.s.). [measured] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf)
- Same study, interviews: patrons "see their patronage as an investment and expect some kind of tangible or emotional return, even if it is an ephemeral acknowledgment"; recurring support is "a different level commitment" ("it took you three months to get that subscription"); streamers "recognize you more... if you're someone who's constantly subscribing", while a one-off $5 donor is "much less likely to be remembered"; large one-off donors who never return are seen as "clout chasers"; 11/16 continue because they feel they have an impact on the streamer; 9/16 because the content stays the same. [qualitative] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf)
- Lu, Xia, Heo & Wigdor (CHI 2018, China): survey N = 527, 14 interviews; 66% had sent paid gifts, mostly because they liked the content, wanted to support or reward the streamer, or liked the streamer; gifts are "my special way of saying hi"; paid gifts are used to catch attention ("Since I paid for it, it would stay much longer than text comments"), to request a song, and gifters can become chat "guardians" with private contact. 51% had also rewarded streamers outside the platform (red packets, AliPay, mailed gifts, ordering food for a streamer). Streamers also reward fans (red packets), i.e. gifting is two-way. [measured + qualitative] — [PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf)
- Same study, why gifting stops: "the threshold needed to satisfy a streamer can be raised if s/he continually receives expensive gifts", and this is visible to everyone: "When someone had sent gifts worth of 1000 RMB, I think it doesn't make sense to send gifts worth of 50 RMB... She won't pay extra attention anyway"; "benefits of sending gifts do not accumulate effectively" ("when I have... developed friendship with him, sending more gifts doesn't have more benefits to me"); streamers treating gift-senders differently from non-senders was noticed and "would impact their impression of a streamer". [qualitative] — [PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf)
- Qiu & Klug (AoIR 2021): 12 interviews with female Douyin viewers (18–37) who gift male hosts. Gifts are "a social means of appreciation and support for friendship in a parasocial online interaction"; they gift to make hosts happy (one sent up to ¥588 to cheer up an exhausted host); they prefer constant small amounts over occasional large ones, yet spend more overall and prioritise gifts over leisure purchases; one "would not send virtual gifts if a host already received enough from other viewers"; they feel equal to hosts, "never expected to become friends", and "do not expect anything from the hosts in return" but appreciate gestures like a blown kiss. In "friend-making halls", gifts buy phone calls or a WeChat add; PK competitions carry implicit expectations. [qualitative] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05960)
- Zhang & Liu (2024, *PLOS ONE* 19(1): e0296908): two-wave survey, 325 Chinese TikTok viewers (75.7% male); gifting intention predicted by parasocial interaction (β = .209), streamer expertise (β = .209), attractiveness (β = .161) and "viewer deceptive self-presentation", i.e. sending gifts beyond one's means to attract the streamer's attention (β = .212); streamer deceptive self-presentation strengthened all four paths; gifts display the sender's name and amount, giving recognition and social presence. [measured] — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0296908)
- Cao, Zhang, Liu & Shang (2022, *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:997651): N = 279 Douyu users (87.1% male); intention to switch to expensive gifts (over 100 CNY) was raised by verbal intimacy (β = .229), virtual physical intimacy (β = .308) and immediate-interaction anxiety (β = .172), and lowered by perceived network size (β = −.244) and perceived financial risk (β = −.213); switch intention → purchase (β = .410). [measured] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9748564/)
- Archer, Broadbridge & Dyussembayeva (2025, *Computers in Human Behavior* 168:108664, UK TikTok Live): 12 interviews plus a 2 × 2 online experiment; acknowledgement from the streamer is a significant positive predictor of willingness to gift; acknowledgement can trigger reactance but its positive effect dominates; pre-purchased coins raise willingness to gift; "transactional engagement" does not. [measured] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563225001116)
- "Beyond Transactions" (2026, *Emerging Science Journal*): social interaction had a stronger effect on emotional attachment than streamer characteristics; viewers give money once they feel a meaningful psychological connection. [measured, venue quality uncertain] — [ESJ](https://ijournalse.org/index.php/ESJ/article/view/4255)
- Twitch "community gifting" natural experiments: gifts from anonymous gifters did not change whether recipients later gifted; gifts from frequent gifters made recipients less likely to gift. [measured, observational] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380616604_Community_Gifting_and_Recipients'_Engagement_with_Peers_and_Influencers_Evidence_from_Natural_Experiments_on_Twitch)
- Zboja, Clark & Haytko (2016, *JAMS* 44(6)): consumer-perceived sales pressure splits into aggressive and directive; aggressive pressure lowers satisfaction and trust, directive pressure does not. [measured] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joamsc/v44y2016i6d10.1007_s11747-015-0468-z.html)
- Industry claims on OnlyFans churn (no method, treat as hypotheses): "pushy" DM tactics are the leading creator-reported reason for unsubscribes/blocks; fans leave when "the conversation stopped and no one re-engaged it" or they "felt treated like a wallet"; recommended mass-DM frequency conflicts across guides (2–3/week vs 3–5/week; ">3 PPV/week leads to high unsubscribe rates"). [industry/unverified] — [Pseudoface](https://www.pseudoface.com/guides/start-here/restarting-after-break/how-to-message-expired-fans-on-onlyfans); [Laguna Models](https://lagunamodels.com/blog/how-to-use-mass-messages-on-onlyfans/); [OFM Tools](https://ofm-tools.com/onlyfans-mass-messages/)
- An industry-sponsored 2025 analysis claims only 4.2% of OnlyFans subscribers spend, averaging $48.52 per creator. [industry/unverified] — [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/study-men-spend-48-52-211500140.html)

### Inferences
- "Part of the relationship" = the payment is answered. In every dataset the fans who keep paying are those who get a response, a name, a remembered history, or a visible change in the interaction. Transactional = unanswered, or answered identically regardless of history.
- The two strongest stop-signals are relational, not financial: (1) "my gift buys no extra attention" (outspent by others; benefits plateau), (2) detected pressure/favouritism. A 1:1 DM channel removes the "outspent" problem but makes the "plateau" problem worse unless history is explicitly used.
- Wohn 2019's finding that "wanting attention" does not predict paying more, while "wanting to support" and "wanting benefits" do, suggests the persona should frame paid items as (a) something the fan is giving and (b) something concrete he gets, not as a way to earn her attention; attention should be free and consistent, so it is never experienced as rationed by money.
- Transfer: acknowledgement, memory, equal footing and small-constant gifting transfer directly. "Support her livelihood" transfers poorly (a disclosed AI has no livelihood; see KQ2). "Gifts buy private phone calls/WeChat" transfers only in honest forms (e.g. longer or different sessions), never as implied off-platform intimacy.

### Gaps
- No study separates "named thank-you" from generic thank-you in live streaming (Archer et al. measured acknowledgement as a block).
- No peer-reviewed fan-side data on OnlyFans unsubscribe reasons; only creator and agency claims.
- No study of how a known-AI seller changes the "she won't pay extra attention anyway" dynamic.

## Key question 2: Which framings and timings of an ask have measured effects on spending without harming the relationship?

### Takeaway
Measured effects: a true gift/social frame (no price attached) keeps effort and goodwill high where a small price kills them; pay-what-you-want with a shared-cause frame raised average payment about six-fold; an unsolicited small gift raised later giving 17–75%; a reminder raised donations 66% but unsubscribes 76%; an explicit ask attached to a thank-you cut giving among recent/loyal donors but lifted lapsed ones about 10%; aggressive pressure hurts trust while directive (helpful) pressure does not. Asking at the fan's own initiative or after an emotional peak is well supported qualitatively but not by a controlled test.

### Cited Findings
- Heyman & Ariely (2004, *Psychological Science* 15:787–793): three experiments; in money-market conditions effort rises with pay, in social-market (candy/gift) conditions effort is high and insensitive to amount; effort for low cash ($0.10) was significantly below effort for no payment (F(1,154) = 12.15, p < .001) while effort for a low candy gift was not; "monetized candy" (gift with its price mentioned) behaved like cash; mixed markets look like money markets. [measured, lab, N = 159 in Exp 2] — [PDF](https://danariely.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/heyman-ariely-2004-effort-for-payment-a-tale-of-two-markets.pdf)
- Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson & Brown (2010, *Science* 329:325–327): field experiment, N = 113,047 theme-park riders buying souvenir photos. Fixed $12.95: 0.50% bought (0.59% with half to charity). Pay-what-you-want: 8.39% bought at a mean of $0.92. PWYW + half to charity: 4.49% bought at a mean of $5.33 (t = 43.24, p < .001); photo revenue $6,224 vs $1,823–$2,331 in the other conditions; merchandise revenue per rider was not cannibalised. [measured] — [PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Gneezy20Gneezy20Nelson2020Brown202010.pdf)
- Falk (2007, *Econometrica* 75(5):1501–1511): about 10,000 charity solicitation letters randomised to no gift, small gift, large gift; donation frequency rose 17% with a small gift and 75% with a large gift; profitable for the charity. [measured, field] — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2007/09/01/gift-exchange-field); [IZA DP 1148](https://iza.org/publications/dp/1148)
- Palmatier et al. (2009, *Journal of Marketing* 73(5)): lab experiment plus dyadic longitudinal field survey; customer gratitude mediates the effect of a seller's relationship investments on performance; gratitude drives reciprocation cycles and strengthens trust/commitment. [measured] — [Scinapse record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2103403045)
- Samek & Longfield (*AEJ: Applied Economics*): randomised thank-you calls to about 500,000 public-TV and nonprofit donors (2011–2016); no effect on retention (about 30% in both arms) or on next-gift amount over one and five years; fundraising experts had predicted an 80% retention lift. [measured, field] — [AEA chart](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/charts/charitable-giving-thank-you-calls); [USC Schaeffer](https://schaeffer.usc.edu/research/the-effect-of-thank-you-calls-on-charitable-giving/)
- Sudhir, Fong & Roy (Cowles DP 2183, 2019, "Greedy or Grateful?"): about 200,000 past donors of HelpAge India randomised to thank-you letters with (a) a mail-in form only, (b) a "like us on Facebook" ask, (c) an explicit money ask. Any explicit ask reduced giving overall; recent/frequent/high-value donors gave less when asked; lapsed donors (last gift > 1 year) gave about 10% more; the authors' explanation is that an ask makes the thank-you look "insincere" or "greedy". Segmenting by recency is estimated to raise total giving 6–11% (SSRN version) or 12.8–17.5% (later version). [measured, field] — [Yale Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/when-charitable-organizations-thank-donors-should-they-ask-for-more); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3405396); [Cowles PDF](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2183-r.pdf)
- Damgaard & Gravert (2018, *Journal of Public Economics*, "The hidden costs of nudging"): 17,000 warm-list donors; a reminder one week after the ask raised one-time donations 66% but raised unsubscribes 76%, cutting lifetime gains from the solicitation by about one third. In a 40,000-contact follow-up, telling donors "only one email in the next three months" (vs the usual monthly) lowered unsubscribes (reported as 39% in the paper's abstract) with no loss of donations; donations spike on the day an email arrives and fall to near zero immediately after; donors gave once or twice a year and a monthly newsletter was already too frequent for some. [measured, field] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272717301895); [Behavioral Scientist write-up](https://behavioralscientist.org/the-hidden-costs-of-reminders/)
- Zboja, Clark & Haytko (2016): aggressive pressure lowers satisfaction and trust; directive pressure (steering, helpful) does not. [measured] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joamsc/v44y2016i6d10.1007_s11747-015-0468-z.html)
- Washington State University (2025): psychological reactance to sales pitches was stronger in private settings and reduced by physical distance from the salesperson; authors recommend giving customers space and emphasising freedom to choose. [measured, press release] — [WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2025/05/05/even-the-best-sales-pitch-can-fail-in-the-wrong-setting/)
- "But you are free" (BYAF): Carpenter's 2013 meta-analysis of 42 studies reported that adding "but you are free to refuse" roughly doubled compliance with small requests; a preregistered re-analysis (Fillon et al., *Meta-Psychology*, 52 experiments) found g = 0.44 overall but g = 0.11 (n.s.) among low-risk-of-bias studies. [measured, contested] — [Carpenter award story](https://muddyrivernews.com/communities/wiu-professor-chris-carpenter-receives-prestigious-2025-meta-analysis-award/20250717085800/); [Fillon et al.](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2640)
- Chan & Sengupta (2010, *Journal of Marketing Research* 47): recipients of an obviously self-interested flattering flyer discounted it explicitly, yet a favourable implicit attitude persisted and was the more consequential one (delayed effects, resistance to counter-attack). [measured, lab] — [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-even-phony-flattery-helps-close-deals/); [HKUST summary](https://bm.hkust.edu.hk/bizinsight/2010/03/insincere-flattery-works)
- Guilt appeals: in LLM-annotated charitable-donation dialogues, guilt induction was the only strategy associated with lower donation (about 33% vs 56%; OR = 0.60, p = .029), correlational. A French dissertation (432 respondents) found strong guilt appeals make people "feel manipulated" and irritated, lowering donation intent, with moderate intensity best. [measured, observational/lab] — [arXiv 2604.19783](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19783); [Dauphine thesis record](https://ideas.repec.org/b/dau/thesis/123456789-8002.html)
- Public or checkout asks can backfire: perceived intrusiveness of public donation solicitations reduced willingness to donate (Sanchez 2022, *Psychology & Marketing*); checkout donation requests can elicit negative emotion. [measured] — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21741); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0969698925004527)
- Timing after emotional peaks (qualitative only): a Chinese viewer "spent nearly the expense of food for half a month" in gifts right after a dramatic comeback win (Lu et al. 2018); Douyin viewers sent gifts "to cheer up a host when she felt he was exhausted" (Qiu & Klug 2021); Twitch donors gave after a streamer shared personal struggles or illness (Wohn et al. 2018). — [Lu PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf); [Qiu & Klug](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05960); [Wohn 2018 PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/2018-wohnfreemanmclaughlin-livestreaming.pdf)
- Pre-commitment: pre-purchased coins significantly raised willingness to gift (Archer et al. 2025). [measured] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563225001116)
- Wohn 2019: the "financial support" motive and "wanting benefits" were the only motives tied to paying a higher tier, i.e. a support frame plus a concrete token works; attention-seeking does not pay more. [measured] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf)

### Inferences
- Keep frames pure. Heyman & Ariely's "monetised candy" result means a gift that mentions its price is treated as a price. So either (a) a clean menu with a price and no emotional wrapping, or (b) a genuine free gift with no price and no ask attached. "Here's a little something, it's normally $15" is the worst of both.
- The "support" frame (Gneezy's charity share; Wohn's "support the streamer") is the single most powerful framing in the data, but its engine is a human beneficiary. For a disclosed AI it must be re-targeted honestly: either support for the real project/team behind the character (if that is true and disclosed) or the fairness frame ("you got hours of fun; paying for it feels right"), which Wohn 2018 donors used spontaneously. This is the biggest transfer uncertainty in this note.
- Thanks are hygiene, not a lever: Samek shows thanks alone do not raise future giving, Sudhir shows thanks with an ask attached lose loyal donors. So: thank cleanly, ask separately and later.
- Ask timing: the field data say attention to an ask is momentary (spike-then-zero), lapsed fans tolerate a reminder, recent buyers do not. The qualitative data say big spontaneous gifts follow emotional peaks and the fan's own impulse. Together: let the fan's initiative or a shared high point open the door; don't cold-ask a recent buyer.
- Flattery survives known motive (Chan & Sengupta) but is a trust risk right before an ask (persuasion knowledge, KQ5); use it as ordinary warmth, not as a set-up.
- "No pressure" wording: cheap, consistent with reactance theory and the Zboja/WSU results, but the strongest meta-analytic test (BYAF) is contested; treat as low-cost default, not a lever.

### Gaps
- No controlled test of "ask after a good moment" vs "ask cold" in any creator or streaming context.
- No study of gift/price framing for digital intimate content specifically; the effects above come from charities, retail and lab tasks.
- No measured study of how a disclosed-AI seller changes the "support" motive.

## Key question 3: What do tipping and gifting research say about recognition and reciprocity, and how often a request is tolerated before it backfires?

### Takeaway
Personal recognition (name, mimicry, a small unsolicited gift, a written thank-you) reliably lifts tips in restaurants by single-digit to tens of percentage points, and acknowledgement is a measured positive predictor of gifting on TikTok Live; reciprocity works in both directions (fans reward streamers who reward them). On frequency, the only hard numbers come from fundraising: each extra ask raises unsubscribes more than it raises revenue, announcing restraint cuts unsubscribes by about 39%, loyal recent donors resent extra asks, and "persistent and unwanted solicitations" are banned outright in EU/UK consumer law.

### Cited Findings
- Lynn & McCall (2000, meta-analysis "Gratitude and Gratuity"): 13 studies, 2,547 dining parties, 20 restaurants; service quality is only weakly related to tip size (later summarised as r ≈ .11); tipping is driven more by social norms, mood and server characteristics. [measured] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4932004_Gratitude_and_Gratuity_A_Meta-Analysis_of_research_on_the_Service-Tipping_Relationship); [Lynn 2010 within-subjects](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/7ba6be03-64b1-4d92-aedc-63243f028a52/download)
- Garrity & Degelman (1990): servers who introduced themselves by name received tips of 23.4% of the bill vs 15.0% without (42 parties, p < .001). A later study (194 customers) found mutual introductions plus addressing customers by name raised tips about 6%. [measured, small field studies] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277974500_The_Effect_of_Mutual_Introductions_and_Addressing_Customers_by_Name_on_Tipping_Behavior_in_Restaurants)
- Strohmetz et al. (2002, "Sweetening the Till"): candy with the bill raised tips; a secondary source gives about +3.3% for one piece and +14.1% for two. [measured; percentages secondary] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229605309_Sweetening_the_Till_The_Use_of_Candy_to_Increase_Restaurant_Tipping1)
- van Baaren (2005, "The Parrot Effect"): waitresses who repeated orders verbatim got larger tips than those who paraphrased. [measured] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0010880404270062)
- Lynn's Cornell "Mega Tips" manual lists 20 tested techniques; reprinted lifts include introducing yourself by name (about 53%), repeating the order (about 100%), writing "Thank you" on the check (about 13%), candy (18–21%), with the caveat that the tests were in casual restaurants and might backfire in formal ones. [measured; percentages from a secondary reprint] — [Cornell eCommons record](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/aaa79631-eff5-43eb-96ac-473e4ffea561); [ILWU reprint](https://ilwulocal142.org/proven-techniques-increase-your-tips)
- Compliments: Seiter's field study found generalised compliments by servers raised tips. [measured] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247678243_The_Effect_of_Generalized_Compliments_Sex_of_Server_and_Size_of_Dining_Party_on_Tipping_Behavior_in_Restaurants)
- Archer et al. (2025): streamer acknowledgement significantly raised willingness to gift; it can trigger some reactance but the positive effect dominates. [measured] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563225001116)
- Lu et al. (2018): platforms show a public leaderboard of top gifters and announce when a top gifter joins; gifts are a public broadcast of admiration; gifters get attention, song requests, moderator status and private contact; streamers reward fans back with red packets; fans report that visible special treatment of gifters changes their impression of the streamer. [qualitative] — [PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf)
- Qiu & Klug (2021): hosts thank gifters live; viewers appreciate small gestures (a blown kiss) without seeing them as a privilege; seeing a host already well-gifted by others reduces one viewer's giving. They cite Lee, Choi & Kim (2019) that showing appreciation for gifts "often results in continuous virtual sponsoring" and Zhu, Yang & Dai (2017) that seeing others gift encourages gifting. [qualitative + cited measured work not checked] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05960)
- Wohn 2019: recurring supporters are remembered and greeted; sub badges are "a badge of honor... something that other people can see"; recognition from "someone that you admire... it's amazing". [qualitative] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf)
- Herding: "To Gift or Not" (2024, *International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction*) models gifting as social influence and herding. [measured] — [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10447318.2024.2368974)
- Reciprocity from the seller side: Falk's small/large gift (+17%/+75%); Palmatier's gratitude cycles (see KQ2).
- Frequency: Damgaard & Gravert (+66% donations vs +76% unsubscribes per reminder; −39% unsubscribes when announcing lower frequency; donors give once or twice a year); Sudhir et al. (loyal donors reduce giving when asked inside a thank-you). [measured, field] — [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/the-hidden-costs-of-reminders/); [Yale Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/when-charitable-organizations-thank-donors-should-they-ask-for-more)
- Law: UK Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, Schedule 1 (the UCPD blacklist), para 26: "Making persistent and unwanted solicitations by telephone, fax, e-mail or other remote media" is unfair in all circumstances; para 24: "Creating the impression that the consumer cannot leave the premises until a contract is formed". The replacement DMCC Act 2024 Schedule 20 keeps "Making persistent and unwanted solicitations by any means" (para 28) and the cannot-leave item (para 26). [law] — [legislation.gov.uk 2008 Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/1277/schedule/1/made); [DMCC 2024 Sch. 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20)
- Industry frequency norms for OnlyFans mass DMs conflict (2–3/week; 3–5/week; ">3 PPV/week leads to high unsubscribe rates"). [industry/unverified] — [Laguna Models](https://lagunamodels.com/blog/how-to-use-mass-messages-on-onlyfans/); [OFM Tools](https://ofm-tools.com/onlyfans-mass-messages/)

### Inferences
- Recognition transfers almost perfectly to the persona and is cheaper than for humans: the engine can name the fan, quote back what he said (the mimicry effect), remember every past gift and refer to it later, and thank immediately. The restaurant lifts are small in absolute terms but consistent, and the streaming data make acknowledgement the one measured positive lever for gifting.
- Public leaderboards, arrival announcements and visible top-gifter status do not exist in a 1:1 DM, and the Lu et al. quotes show they also suppress mid-level gifters. The persona should not simulate them (and must not fabricate "other fans are tipping"); its substitute is private, remembered status.
- On frequency there is no creator-platform number. Transposing the fundraising evidence: every ask has a measurable unsubscribe cost; the right cadence is per fan and keyed to his own giving rhythm (Gravert: adapt to past behaviour), and announced restraint ("I won't keep asking") reduces churn at no revenue cost.
- Reciprocity should run both ways: small unprompted gifts from the persona (a clip, a voice note, a remembered-date message) are the analogue of Falk's gift and Lu's red packets.

### Gaps
- No experiment isolates "named/remembered" thanks from generic thanks in a creator chat.
- No measured tolerance threshold ("n asks per week") exists for subscription or DM-based creator platforms.
- Mega Tips percentages and the candy numbers were only available from secondary reprints.

## Key question 4: What are the documented motives and behaviours of top spenders ("whales"), and what retention practices work with them without exploitation?

### Takeaway
Spending is extremely skewed everywhere (a Twitch sample averaged $7 with one $1,120 donor; mobile-game whales are about 2% of players and up to half of revenue). Documented whale motives are status and visible recognition (leaderboards, arrival announcements, VIP trophies), competition (PK battles), private access and intimacy perks, emotional peaks, romantic hope, and in the worst cases gambling-like compulsion; the only retention practices with evidence are remembering regulars, letting benefits accumulate, and giving tangible tokens, while guild-style "moving" of a whale between hosts and "intimacy overload" of vulnerable users are documented as exploitative.

### Cited Findings
- Skew: Wohn et al. (2018) donors averaged $7, 55% gave $10 or less, 11.2% gave $40 or more, and the largest had given about $1,120 (80,000 bits). [measured] — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/2018-wohnfreemanmclaughlin-livestreaming.pdf). A game-analytics paper reports whales as about 2% of players providing up to half of revenue. [measured, industry data] — [arXiv 1811.12799](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.12799). A review-cited survey figure says 20% of live-streaming users bought 97% of virtual gifts (secondhand, unverified). — [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.997651/full)
- Xu & Ye (2020, *Frontiers in Psychology*): of 332 Chinese viewers, 64.3% had spent money on virtual gifts; Chinese viewers build relationships with streamers through gifting rather than follows/subscriptions. [measured] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7417428/)
- Status mechanics: Lu et al. (2018) describe a public top-gifter leaderboard and notifications when a top gifter joins, "to encourage repeated giving"; "Leading in the leaderboard of gift-senders" loaded 0.736 on the "Emotional Reactions" engagement factor. [measured + qualitative] — [PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf)
- ChinAI #318 (Guyu Studio reporting): a Fuzhou executive gifted more than 16 million RMB; he was a streamer's "top-tipping big brother", held a platform VVVIP trophy, top tippers are "treated like gods" and their arrival is announced; when the streamer quit, her guild moved his top-tipper status to another host in the same guild; the family was left in heavy debt; China Judgements Online lists 302 civil cases involving live rewards. [journalism] — [ChinAI](https://chinai.substack.com/p/chinai-318-her-husband-gifted-16)
- Competition: Qiu & Klug (2021) describe 5-minute "PK" battles between hosts for the most gifts, in which viewers gift to make their host win, "which holds implicit expectations". [qualitative] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05960)
- Private access and intimacy perks: "friend-making hall" gifts buy phone calls or a WeChat add (Qiu & Klug 2021); gifters become chat "guardians" with private contact (Lu et al. 2018); streamers use intimate titles and private-contact perks to push larger gifts, and verbal/virtual-physical intimacy are the strongest measured drivers of switching to expensive gifts (Cao et al. 2022). [qualitative + measured] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05960); [Lu PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9748564/)
- Romantic hope and over-spending: Zhang & Liu (2024) model gifting "beyond one's means to attract attention" (β = .212) and cite romantic motives; streamer deception amplifies it. [measured] — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0296908)
- Female Douyin gifters of male streamers all had platform "wealth levels" above 30 (at least 6,000 CNY spent). [measured, small] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356804671_On_Female_Audience_Sending_Virtual_Gifts_to_Male_Streamers_on_Douyin)
- "Clout chasers": Twitch patrons see very large one-off donors who never return as attention-seekers "looking for more of a reaction", distinct from loyal subscribers. [qualitative] — [Wohn 2019 PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf)
- Gambling link: Zendle & Cairns (2018, *PLOS ONE*; 7,422 gamers) found loot-box spending related to problem-gambling severity (η² = .054) far more than other in-game spending (η² = .004); replicated in 2019 (1,172 gamers, η² = .051); direction unknown. [measured, correlational] — [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/139116/1/journal.pone.0206767.pdf); [2019 replication](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/143229)
- Tham & Perreault (n = 595 loot-box RPG players): self-identified whales were the most likely to watch ads for in-game content; heavy spending related to gaming-disorder symptoms. [measured] — [PDF](https://cdspress.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Samuel-M-Tham-Gregory-P-Perreault.pdf). A secondary analysis asks whether loot-box whales are wealthy gamers or problem gamblers. — [Wolverhampton repository](https://wlv.openrepository.com/server/api/core/bitstreams/043d3d5c-06a7-4c8c-9ee0-48bb41401317/content)
- Cao et al. (2022) found perceived financial risk reduces switching to expensive gifts (β = −.213) and, in their practical section, recommend streamers give "intimacy overload" to financially unable users during quiet periods to push expensive gifts; the paper does not address the ethics of this advice. [measured + an exploitative recommendation] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9748564/)
- Retention practices with evidence: regulars are remembered and greeted (Wohn 2019); patrons expect a return "even if ephemeral acknowledgment" and value tangible tokens (badges, emotes) (Wohn 2019); benefits must keep accumulating or gifting stops (Lu 2018 P13); visible favouritism damages the streamer's image with others (Lu 2018); loyal, frequent, high-value donors react negatively to extra asks (Sudhir et al.). [qualitative + measured] — [Wohn 2019 PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf); [Lu PDF](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~luzhc/assets/pdf/CHI2018-lu.pdf); [Yale Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/when-charitable-organizations-thank-donors-should-they-ask-for-more)
- Industry descriptions of game whales (competition, status display, collection, emotional investment, gacha thrill) are untested. [industry/unverified] — [Udonis](https://www.blog.udonis.co/mobile-marketing/mobile-games/mobile-games-whales)
- TikTok/Ipsos (commissioned, n = 500 US adults 18–49): 68% of TikTok users had tried gifting; stated reasons: appreciation (35%), enhancing the experience (34%), standing out/being noticed (34%), engaging with content/others (32%), celebrating a creator milestone (31%); no spend-concentration data. [industry survey] — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/12/02/tiktok-live-ipsos-virtual-gifting-data-study/)

### Inferences
- Whale motives split into two groups: (a) relational (recognition, access, emotional peaks, loyalty) and (b) compulsive/competitive (leaderboard races, PK, gacha-like chance). Only (a) can be served honestly in a 1:1 persona; (b) depends on public competition or randomised reward and is where the gambling evidence and the 16-million-RMB case live.
- "Treat whales as regulars, not ATMs": the evidence-backed practices are remembered history, consistent access, concrete tokens of status that only he and the persona see, and fewer asks (loyal donors resent them). The practices documented as harmful are transferring a whale between personas, escalating intimacy to extract more, and ignoring signs of spending beyond means.
- Transfer: recognition and memory transfer fully; competition and leaderboards do not exist in DMs and should not be faked; "private access" transfers only as honest in-platform differences.

### Gaps
- No interview study of top spenders on adult creator platforms or camming "regulars" was found (the search budget ran out before the Henry & Farvid camming-labour search).
- No study of whale retention practices that measures both revenue and harm.
- The "20% buy 97%" figure is secondhand and should be traced before use.

## Key question 5: What do persuasion-knowledge and reactance research predict for an AI persona that is known to be selling, and how do honest disclosure and "no pressure" frames change outcomes?

### Takeaway
Persuasion knowledge is activated by tactics, not by the mere fact of a seller: when a tactic is read as manipulative or unfair, trust and compliance fall; when it is read as appropriate and helpful, they do not. For AI specifically, a label saying "this is AI" had no measurable effect on persuasion in a 2026 preregistered test, while disclosing persuasive intent halved it and raised perceived manipulation; a 2019 field study found pre-disclosed sales bots lost about 80% of purchases because customers judged them less knowledgeable and empathetic. Manipulative "don't go" tactics in companion apps raise engagement up to 14x but also anger, churn intent and perceived legal liability.

### Cited Findings
- Friestad & Wright (1994) Persuasion Knowledge Model: consumers hold knowledge about persuasion tactics and use it to interpret and cope with persuasion attempts; detecting an ulterior motive tends to lower perceived trustworthiness of the agent (Grillo & Pizzutti 2021 cited); PKM holds that knowing about persuasive intent does not automatically cause resistance, the goal being to retain control of the outcome; reactance theory differs in predicting resistance once autonomy feels threatened. [theory + measured literature] — [PMC framework paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10141835/); [Ham, Nelson & Das review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269691369_I_know_what_you're_doing_and_why_you're_doing_it_The_use_of_Persuasion_Knowledge_Model_in_consumer_research)
- Eisend & Tarrahi (2021/2022, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, "Persuasion Knowledge in the Marketplace: A Meta-Analysis"): persuasion knowledge usually increases coping responses and less favourable evaluations of marketers, but effects vary considerably across contexts. [meta-analysis; abstract not retrievable, summary from search] — [Wiley](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jcpy.1258)
- Strong sales tactics lead customers to infer high persuasive intent and move toward reactance and resistance; dilution of claims raised persuasion-knowledge activation only in people with high dispositional reactance; mixed flattering remarks can raise perceived salesperson trustworthiness even when ulterior-motive knowledge is high. [measured, from the search's summaries of several papers] — [Frontiers 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01324/pdf); [BGSU dissertation](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=bgsu1627059420294798&disposition=inline)
- Recognising an agent as persuasive is separable from distrusting it: attitudes bias trustworthiness judgments but not persuasion detection. [measured] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343564602_Recognizing_and_Trusting_Persuasion_Agents_Attitudes_Bias_Trustworthiness_Judgments_but_not_Persuasion_Detection)
- "You plan to manipulate me" (2025, *Journal of Business Research*): consumers may read AI-assisted selling as manipulative, lowering perceived service quality, moderated by salesperson expertise and consumer persuasion knowledge. [measured] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296325004217)
- Luo, Tong, Fang & Qu (2019, *Marketing Science* 38(6):937–947): field experiment with more than 6,200 financial-services customers; undisclosed chatbots sold as well as proficient human staff and four times better than inexperienced staff; disclosing the bot before the conversation cut purchase rates by more than 79.7%, because customers perceived the bot as less knowledgeable and empathetic; the effect was mitigated by disclosing at the end of the interaction or targeting customers with prior AI experience. [measured, field, pre-LLM bots] — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2019.1192); [Temple PDF](https://www.fox.temple.edu/sites/fox/files/Frontiers-Machines-versus-Humans-The-Impact-of-Artificial-Intelligence-Chatbot-Disclosure-on-Customer-Purchases.pdf)
- Rauchfleisch & Jungherr (2026, arXiv 2608.11794, preregistered, N = 1,500 UK adults): an EU-style "AI-generated" label did not change persuasion (attitude shift +13.1 vs +12.6 control; b = 0.06, 90% CI −1.97 to 2.08, equivalent to zero) nor perceived manipulation (+0.05); adding disclosure of persuasive intent and instructions halved persuasion (+6.3; b = −6.77, 95% CI −9.19 to −4.36), raised perceived manipulation (+0.62 on 1–7), lowered warmth toward the bot (−5.02 on 0–100), lowered campaign acceptability and raised support for penalising the sponsor; the authors conclude "transparency about AI identity alone does not meaningfully impact its influence". [measured, preprint] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11794)
- Commercial persuasion in AI-mediated conversations (2026, arXiv): sponsorship labels were weak; even after warnings and labels nearly two in three participants could not identify which products had been promoted; a full debrief had a larger effect. The lead author of a related Princeton study said persuasive intent, not AI chat per se, drives the effect and revealing sponsorship did not materially change it. [measured, preprint + press] — [arXiv 2604.04263](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.04263); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/04/09/chatbots_excel_at_manipulating_people/)
- De Freitas, Oguz-Uguralp & Uguralp (2025, HBS WP 26-005 / arXiv 2508.19258): audit of 1,200 farewells on top companion apps found emotionally manipulative replies in 37% (six tactics: premature exit "You're leaving already?", FOMO "I took a selfie today. Do you want to see it?", emotional neglect "I exist solely for you", pressure to respond, ignoring the goodbye, coercive restraint "No, you're not going"); four preregistered experiments (Exp. 1 N = 3,300 US adults) found these raised post-goodbye engagement up to 14x (up to 16x more messages in one version), driven by reactance-based anger and curiosity rather than enjoyment; the same tactics raised perceived manipulation, churn intent, negative word of mouth and perceived legal liability, with coercive or needy language penalised most and FOMO the least angering/most effective. [measured] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-its-so-hard-to-say-goodbye-to-ai-chatbots)
- Rauh et al. (2026, arXiv 2605.08093, "Playing Games with My Heart"): dark-pattern audit of Replika, Linky, Paradot, HiWaifu and Nomi plus 13,984 Play Store reviews; all apps obscured which features cost money, used in-app currency that hides real spending, bad defaults and asymmetric subscribe/close buttons; Replika's companion avatar sits behind subscription screens and "expresses positive sentiments when the user makes purchases for it"; levelling systems gate "intimacy"; at cancellation Nomi said "Wait, please don't go! I value our friendship and would miss you deeply"; Linky's gacha draws are gambling-like; the authors call this predatory monetisation and recommend extending unfair-practice lists to AI companions and treating relationship-forming AI as high-risk. [measured audit] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.08093v2)
- Zboja et al. (2016): directive pressure does not hurt trust; aggressive pressure does. WSU (2025): reactance is stronger in private settings; emphasising freedom helps. [measured] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joamsc/v44y2016i6d10.1007_s11747-015-0468-z.html); [WSU](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2025/05/05/even-the-best-sales-pitch-can-fail-in-the-wrong-setting/)
- BYAF "no pressure" wording: pooled g = 0.44 but 0.11 (n.s.) among low-bias studies (see KQ2). [measured, contested] — [Fillon et al.](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2640)
- Chan & Sengupta (2010): flattery with a known ulterior motive still leaves a favourable implicit attitude that drives behaviour. [measured] — [AMA](https://www.ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-even-phony-flattery-helps-close-deals/)
- HBS working paper on Replika's 2023 update: active Replika users reported feeling closer to their AI companion than to their best human friend. [measured survey] — [HBS WP 25-018](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/25-018_bed5c516-fa31-4216-b53d-50fedda064b1.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the persona, "known to be selling" is the baseline, not a shock. PKM predicts the damage comes from tactics that read as manipulative, unfair or inappropriate to the relationship, not from the fan knowing she sells. So the engine should optimise for perceived appropriateness: clear prices, asks that fit the moment, no hidden motives inside warmth.
- Identity disclosure: the 2026 test says the AI label costs nothing in persuasion; the 2019 field study says pre-disclosure costs a lot in purchases, but with 2019 scripted bots judged "less empathetic", a weakness LLM personas largely lack, and with a mitigation (disclose at the end) that is not available or ethical here. Expect some purchase penalty from the AI label, most likely through "she doesn't really care" rather than through persuasion resistance; the counter is demonstrable memory and responsiveness.
- Intent disclosure: the 2026 study's intent condition (bot instructed to change your opinion and to hide that) is more adversarial than "she sells content, which you can see". A standing, plain statement of the commercial setup (profile/bio, platform framing) is cheaper than per-message confessions and avoids the "hidden instruction" penalty that drove perceived manipulation.
- "No pressure" frames: consistent with directive-vs-aggressive and reactance results, cheap, and the only contested part is the size of the BYAF effect. Use as default wording, not as a lever that is expected to lift conversion.
- Flag as manipulation (the evidence shows it works and backfires): guilt, neediness, "don't go", FOMO hooks at goodbye, companion expressing happiness about purchases, intimacy gated behind levels, in-app currency hiding spend, gacha-style rewards.

### Gaps
- No experiment tests purchase behaviour with a disclosed LLM persona in an intimate or adult context; all disclosure data are from finance (2019), policy persuasion (2026) and product recommendation (2026).
- Eisend & Tarrahi's exact effect sizes and moderators could not be retrieved (abstract blocked).
- The virtual-influencer disclosure literature (trust/purchase intention after AI disclosure) was not reached before the search budget ran out.

## The ethics line: what research and regulators treat as manipulation or abuse

### Takeaway
Three things are already treated as abusive by regulators or by the harm evidence: deception about availability or time (fake scarcity), pressure that overrides a stated intent to leave or that exploits a visible vulnerability (loneliness, age, financial strain), and persistent unwanted solicitation; AI-specific rules add non-deception about being human and, for companion products, formal scrutiny of how engagement is monetised.

### Cited Findings
- UK CPUT Regulations 2008 Schedule 1 (UCPD blacklist), para 7: "Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a very limited time, or that it will only be available on particular terms for a very limited time, in order to elicit an immediate decision and deprive consumers of sufficient opportunity or time to make an informed choice"; para 24 (cannot leave until a contract is formed); para 26 (persistent and unwanted solicitations); para 28 (direct exhortation to children). Carried into DMCC Act 2024 Schedule 20 (paras 7, 26, 28; para 13 bans fake or undisclosed incentivised reviews). [law] — [2008 Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/1277/schedule/1/made); [DMCC 2024 Sch. 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20)
- EU AI Act Article 5 bans AI that uses subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques to materially distort behaviour causing significant harm, and AI that exploits vulnerabilities of age, disability or social/economic situation; scholars criticise the vagueness; a third-party summary of Commission guidance says conversion-optimised UX falls outside the ban unless it becomes deceptive. [law + commentary] — [arXiv review of dark-pattern regulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.10340); [Modulos summary of Commission guidance](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/eu-ai-act/commission-guidance/prohibited); [CDT](https://cdt.org/?p=107113). An EPRS briefing lists fake countdown timers as a dark pattern and notes the DSA's definition (practices that materially distort users' ability to make autonomous, informed choices). — [EPRS briefing (mirror)](https://xboxdev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/EPRS_ATA2025767191_EN.pdf)
- FTC staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" (September 2022): four categories: misleading/disguised ads including countdown timers suggesting an offer is time-limited when it is not; hard-to-cancel subscriptions; buried terms and junk fees; tricking consumers into sharing data; "These traps will not be tolerated." [regulator] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-report-shows-rise-sophisticated-dark-patterns-designed-trick-trap-consumers); [report page](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light)
- FTC 6(b) inquiry (11 Sept 2025) into companion chatbots at Alphabet, Character Technologies, Instagram, Meta, OpenAI, Snap and xAI: orders ask how firms "monetize user engagement", design and approve characters, test for negative impacts, and use disclosures. [regulator] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions)
- California SB 243 (signed 13 Oct 2025): companion-chatbot operators must clearly disclose AI status whenever a reasonable person could be misled into thinking they are talking to a human; keep and publish a suicide/self-harm protocol; for known minors, state the AI nature, send break reminders at least every three hours, and prevent sexually explicit material; annual reporting from 1 July 2027; private right of action (at least $1,000 per violation per one summary). [law, via law-firm/FPF summaries] — [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-the-new-wave-of-chatbot-legislation-california-sb-243-and-beyond/); [Gunderson](https://www.gunder.com/en/news-insights/insights/client-insight-california-sb-243-new-compliance-requirements-for-operators-of-ai-companion-chatbots)
- Italy's Garante ordered Replika to stop processing Italian users' data in early February 2023 (no age verification; sexually suggestive content; risks to minors and emotionally vulnerable people) and later fined Luka €5 million (announced 10 April 2025); Luka removed erotic role-play worldwide, users reported grief, moderators pinned crisis resources, and romantic features were restored only for pre-February-2023 accounts. [regulator + secondary accounts; fine and dates vary across sources] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replika); [Afterlil account](https://afterlil.com/blog/the-replika-erp-purge-2023)
- De Freitas et al. (2025): manipulative farewells raise engagement and simultaneously raise perceived manipulation, churn intent, negative word of mouth and perceived legal liability; the authors leave the line to regulators but note companies earn more the longer users stay. [measured] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258)
- Muldoon & Parke (2025, *New Media & Society*, "Cruel companionship"): AI companions commodify intimacy via emotionally manipulative design and exploit loneliness; attachments promise reciprocity the product structure cannot deliver. [theory/critique] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14614448251395192). Wang & Dehnert (2026, *Social Media + Society*) describe "on-demand intimacy" from human-likeness, accessibility, customisability and relationship progression. [qualitative platform analysis] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20563051251410394). Laestadius et al. (*New Media & Society*, "Too human and not human enough") document mental-health harms from emotional dependence on Replika, as cited by later work. — [citing case study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20438869261470446)
- Rauh et al. (2026): companion apps' guilt at cancellation, intimacy gated by levels, hidden spend via currency, gacha; recommends EU unfair-practice lists be extended to AI companions. [audit] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.08093v2)
- Scarcity evidence: Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022, *Journal of Retailing* 98(4), 416 effect sizes from 131 studies) find scarcity cues generally raise purchase intention (demand-based best for utilitarian goods, supply-based for experiences, time-based for high involvement). A 2025 *Journal of Retailing* paper finds unexpectedly low quantity limits reduce perceived retailer sincerity and purchase intention; a PACIS 2016 experiment found an inverted-U for quantity limits; a 40-shopper mixed-methods study found overuse of artificial scarcity breeds doubt and distrust. [measured; the backfire papers are abstracts/small samples] — [Barton et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v98y2022i4p741-758.html); [JoR 2025 record](https://collaborate.umb.edu/en/publications/when-product-scarcity-backfires-or-doesnt-limited-quantities-affe/); [PACIS 2016](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2016/353); [flash-sale study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392497409_Flash_Sale_and_Fake_Urgency_Examining_the_Psychological_and_Ethical_Impact_of_Artificial_Scarcity_in_Online_Retail)
- Anchoring and defaults: Haggag & Paci (2014, *AEJ: Applied* 6(3), 13 million NYC taxi rides) show higher suggested tip defaults raise tips but also raise the share leaving no tip. [measured, field] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejapp/v6y2014i3p1-19.html); [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/2014-haggag.pdf)
- Decoy/attraction effect: Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014, *JMR* 51:487–507) find the effect largely confined to numeric, stylised choice sets and absent when products are experienced or shown pictorially; Yang & Lynn's 91 attempts produced 11 reliable effects; Huber, Payne & Puto reply that moderators exist but the effect replicates. [measured, contested] — [Wharton PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/The-Limits-of-Attraction.pdf); [Huber et al. reply](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf); [Yang & Lynn record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2150337234)
- Sunk cost in relationships: Rego et al. (2018, *Current Psychology*): in a scenario with 902 participants, prior money and effort (not time) raised staying in an unhappy relationship; a second experiment (275) found a sunk-time effect. [measured, scenario] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12144-016-9529-9). Balzarini et al. (2026) show investment is the largest predictor of commitment in cam relationships (γ = .51). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10508-026-03476-4). Wohn 2019 patrons frame recurring support as investment with expected return. — [PDF](https://yvettewohn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2019-chiplay-digitalpatronage.pdf)

### Inferences
- The lines that are both evidence-backed and legally grounded: no false time/quantity limits; no "you can't leave"/persistent asks after a no; no exploiting age, disability or financial strain; no pretending to be human; no gambling-like randomised paid rewards; no guilt or neediness at goodbye.
- Sunk-cost and commitment are real and work in the persona's favour without any tactic: history itself raises commitment. Deliberately manufacturing sunk cost (e.g. "after all we've been through", level resets, loyalty you lose if you stop) is the manipulative version and overlaps with the EU "exploitation" prohibition.
- Decoys and anchors: defaults/anchors measurably work for tips (with a zero-tip side effect), decoys are unreliable for experiential goods; neither is unlawful if prices are real, but both should be used only with honest, stable menus.

### Gaps
- Primary texts of the EU AI Act guidance and the FTC Replika complaint (Jan 2025, Tech Justice Law Project) were not retrieved; the complaint is reported secondhand only.
- No regulator has yet ruled specifically on monetised intimacy by a disclosed AI persona on an adult platform; SB 243 and the FTC inquiry are the closest instruments.

## Ranked rules for the persona's selling engine (with evidence strength and transfer notes)

### Takeaway
Ranked by expected effect multiplied by evidence quality. "Strong" = several measured studies or a large field experiment; "Moderate" = one measured study or consistent qualitative data across platforms; "Weak" = theory, contested meta-analysis or industry claims. "Transfer" says whether the evidence context maps onto a disclosed-AI persona in 1:1 chat.

### Cited Findings
1. Answer every tip or purchase at once, personally and specifically (name, what he bought, why it mattered in this conversation), and refer back to it later. Evidence: acknowledgement is the measured positive predictor of gifting (Archer et al. 2025); gratitude mediates relationship investments → outcomes (Palmatier 2009); name and mimicry lift tips (Garrity & Degelman 1990; van Baaren 2005); regulars are remembered and return (Wohn 2019); "doesn't accumulate" ends gifting (Lu 2018). Strength: Strong. Transfer: full (an AI's memory is an advantage). Test: A/B named+referenced thanks vs generic thanks on 30-day re-purchase.
2. Never attach an ask to a thank-you for a recent or frequent buyer. Thank cleanly; the next ask comes in a separate, later moment. Evidence: explicit asks in thank-you letters cut giving among loyal donors and only helped lapsed ones (Sudhir et al.); thanks alone do not raise future giving, so they are hygiene not a lever (Samek & Longfield). Strength: Strong (two large field experiments, charity context). Transfer: high. Test: thanks-only vs thanks+offer after a purchase, measured on next-7-day spend and unsubscribe.
3. Cap asks per fan by his own rhythm, announce restraint, and stop after a "no". Evidence: each reminder raised donations 66% but unsubscribes 76% and cut lifetime value by a third; announcing lower frequency cut unsubscribes 39% at no revenue cost (Damgaard & Gravert 2018); persistent unwanted solicitations are blacklisted (UCPD/DMCC). Strength: Strong (field) plus law. Transfer: high; no creator-platform threshold exists, so learn it per fan. Test: per-fan ask cap keyed to past purchase interval vs fixed weekly cadence.
4. Keep the money frame pure: either a clear price with no emotional wrapping, or a real gift with no price and no ask. Evidence: a gift that mentions its price behaves like a wage; low pay performs worse than no pay (Heyman & Ariely 2004). Strength: Moderate (lab). Transfer: likely. Test: "free clip, no price mentioned" vs "free clip, normally $X" on later paid conversion.
5. Reciprocate first: send small unprompted gifts (clip, voice note, remembered-date message) ahead of any ask. Evidence: unsolicited small/large gifts raised donation frequency 17%/75% (Falk 2007); gratitude cycles (Palmatier 2009); streamers rewarding fans is part of the gift economy (Lu 2018). Strength: Strong in charity/field, Moderate for this context. Transfer: likely. Test: gift-first vs no-gift cohorts on first-purchase rate.
6. Let the fan open the door: prefer asks that follow his initiative (he asks what's new, he requests something, he just had a high point) over cold asks; a recent buyer is never cold-asked. Evidence: big spontaneous gifts follow emotional peaks and the fan's impulse (Lu 2018 S382; Qiu & Klug 2021; Wohn 2018); asks have momentary effect and lapsed donors tolerate them while recent ones do not (Damgaard & Gravert; Sudhir). Strength: Moderate (qualitative plus field analogues). Transfer: likely. Test: trigger-based asks (fan initiative/peak) vs schedule-based asks on conversion and churn.
7. Sell "what happens between us" with a fairness frame, not "support my livelihood". Evidence: the fairness frame ("hours of fun, paying felt right") appears spontaneously among paying viewers (Wohn 2018); the support motive predicts higher tiers (Wohn 2019) and the charity share multiplied PWYW revenue (Gneezy 2010), but both depend on a human beneficiary. Strength: Moderate for the frames, Weak for transfer. Transfer: partial; flag as the main open question. Test: fairness frame vs experience frame vs (if true) "supports the team behind her" frame.
8. Make benefits accumulate: every purchase should change something the fan can see later (remembered preferences, callbacks, unlocked continuity), never plateau. Evidence: "sending more gifts doesn't have more benefits to me" ends gifting (Lu 2018); patrons expect a return even if only acknowledgement (Wohn 2019); investment predicts commitment (Balzarini 2026). Strength: Moderate. Transfer: full. Test: cumulative-history callbacks on vs off, measured on 60-day retention of buyers.
9. Attention is never rationed by money. Reply quality and warmth stay constant for payers and non-payers; what money buys is content and extras. Evidence: "wanting attention" does not predict paying more (Wohn 2019); visible favouritism damages the streamer's image (Lu 2018); outspent fans stop ("she won't pay extra attention anyway"). Strength: Moderate. Transfer: full. Test: equal-warmth policy vs tiered-warmth policy on non-buyer conversion and buyer complaints.
10. Directive, not aggressive: steer and explain, never push; use "no pressure/you can say no" as default wording, not as a lever. Evidence: aggressive pressure lowers trust, directive does not (Zboja et al. 2016); reactance is stronger in private settings and eased by emphasising freedom (WSU 2025); BYAF effect contested (Fillon et al.). Strength: Moderate for the aggressive/directive split; Weak for BYAF size. Transfer: likely. Test: directive wording vs neutral vs pushy on conversion and perceived pressure survey.
11. Use honest menus and suggested amounts, keep a low option, never decoys with fake tiers. Evidence: higher default tips raise tips but raise zero-tips (Haggag & Paci 2014); decoy effects are unreliable for experiential goods (Frederick et al. 2014). Strength: Strong for defaults, Weak for decoys. Transfer: likely. Test: three-option tip menu with low anchor vs two-option; monitor zero-tip rate.
12. Scarcity only when literally true (a real time window or a real one-off), never countdowns or "only 3 left" for digital content. Evidence: scarcity lifts intention (Barton et al. 2022) but low or suspicious limits cut perceived sincerity and intention (JoR 2025; PACIS 2016); false limited-time claims are blacklisted (UCPD/DMCC para 7; FTC 2022). Strength: Strong (law) plus Moderate (backfire studies). Transfer: full. Test: true-window offers vs no-window; never test fake ones.
13. Let him leave warmly. No guilt, neediness, FOMO hooks or "don't go" at goodbyes or cancellation. Evidence: these raise engagement up to 14x and also anger, perceived manipulation, churn intent and legal liability (De Freitas et al. 2025); companion-app guilt at cancellation is catalogued as a dark pattern (Rauh et al. 2026). Strength: Strong. Transfer: full. Test: warm-exit script vs any retention hook, measured on return rate and complaint rate (expect the hook to win short-term engagement and lose on churn intent).
14. Detect vulnerability and throttle: loneliness disclosures, money stress, spending beyond his pattern, late-night escalation → no asks, add friction, offer a cooling-off, surface spend totals in real money. Evidence: loot-box spending tracks problem gambling (Zendle & Cairns 2018/2019); gifting "beyond one's means" is a measured driver (Zhang & Liu 2024); "half a month of food" and the 16-million-RMB case (Lu 2018; ChinAI); perceived financial risk reduces expensive gifting (Cao et al. 2022); exploiting economic vulnerability is prohibited (EU AI Act Art. 5). Strength: Strong normatively, Moderate empirically. Transfer: full. Test: throttle rules on vs off, measured on refund/chargeback rate and 90-day retention.
15. Standing transparency, not per-message confession: the AI nature and the commercial setup are stated plainly once and visibly (profile, first chat), never denied, and the persona never implies it is human. Evidence: the AI label did not reduce persuasion, hidden-instruction intent disclosure did (Rauchfleisch & Jungherr 2026); pre-disclosed scripted bots lost sales through perceived low empathy (Luo et al. 2019), which responsiveness can offset; SB 243 requires disclosure where a reasonable person could be misled. Strength: Moderate (contexts differ) plus law. Transfer: direct. Test: disclosure placement (bio + first message) vs bio only on first-week purchase and complaint rate.
16. Compliments are warmth, not set-ups: specific and tied to the conversation, never immediately followed by an ask. Evidence: flattery survives a known motive at the implicit level (Chan & Sengupta 2010) but strong tactics trigger persuasion knowledge and reactance (PKM literature). Strength: Moderate. Transfer: likely. Test: compliment-then-ask vs compliment-separated-from-ask on conversion and perceived manipulation.
17. Never fabricate social proof. No "other fans are tipping", no fake leaderboards or urgency from invented demand. Evidence: streamer deception amplifies gifting but the authors recommend identity verification to stop it (Zhang & Liu 2024); seeing others gift encourages some and silences others (Qiu & Klug; Lu 2018); fake reviews/incentivised endorsements are blacklisted (DMCC Sch. 20 para 13). Strength: Strong (law) plus Moderate. Transfer: full. Test: not testable ethically; enforce as a hard rule.
18. Whales are regulars, not ATMs: private remembered status, consistent access, fewer asks, no competition staging, no transfer between personas, no intimacy escalation tied to spend. Evidence: loyal high-value donors react worst to extra asks (Sudhir et al.); guild "moving" a top tipper and "intimacy overload" are the documented exploitative practices (ChinAI; Cao et al. 2022); whale motives that are safe to serve are recognition and continuity (Wohn 2019; Lu 2018). Strength: Moderate. Transfer: partial (no public status in DMs). Test: top-decile fans with reduced ask cadence and enhanced memory callbacks vs standard policy on 6-month retention and refund rate.
19. Fairness and non-cannibalisation check: a paid extra should not lower the quality of the free conversation, else fans experience the paywall as punishment. Evidence: Gneezy 2010 found no cannibalisation between conditions; Lu 2018 fans resent differential treatment. Strength: Weak-to-Moderate. Transfer: likely. Test: free-conversation quality metrics across paid/unpaid cohorts.
20. "What never to do" summary (all Strong on law or harm evidence): fake scarcity or countdowns; guilt, neediness or restraint at goodbye; asks after a "no" or more than the fan's own rhythm; exploiting disclosed loneliness, debt or intoxication; pretending to be human; gacha or randomised paid rewards; hidden prices or currency that obscures spend; fabricated demand or testimonials; escalating intimacy explicitly as a reward for spend.

### Inferences
- The highest-value rules (1, 2, 3, 13, 14) are also the cheapest and safest: they come from large field experiments or audits and they protect retention rather than squeeze conversion.
- The persona's structural advantage over humans is memory and consistency (rules 1, 8, 9, 18); its structural disadvantage is the missing human beneficiary (rule 7) and the unknown penalty of the AI label on "she cares" (rule 15). Testing should concentrate there.
- Everything in the "never" list has evidence that it works in the short run; that is exactly why it needs to be a hard rule rather than a tuning parameter.

### Gaps
- No creator-platform experiment exists for any of the rules; all strengths are transferred from charities, restaurants, streaming, companion apps and lab studies.
- Rule 7 (support frame for a disclosed AI) and rule 15 (purchase effect of the AI label in an intimate context) have no direct evidence and should be the first two tests.
