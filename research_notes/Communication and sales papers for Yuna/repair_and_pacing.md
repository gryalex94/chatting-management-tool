## 1. Paper 1: Moore, An & Marrese (2024), "Understanding is a Two-Way Street"

### Takeaway
The paper argues that conversational interfaces let the agent ask the user to rephrase but rarely let the user repair the agent's turn, tests nine user-initiated repair types (from Moore and Arar's Natural Conversation Framework) in a voice interface in a small-scale study, and finds that understanding-oriented repairs work much the same in text and voice while hearing- and speaking-oriented repairs are voice-specific. I could not read the body, so the type list, N, tasks and numbers below are either absent or from secondary sources.

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic record: Robert J. Moore, Sungeun An, Olivia H. Marrese, Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. vol. 8, CSCW1, Article 187, April 2024, 26 pages, DOI 10.1145/3641026 — cited in full by a 2025 EMNLP paper's reference list — [Ngo et al. 2025, arXiv 2510.24628](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24628v1); IBM lists the publication date as 26 Apr 2024 and links the PDF at dl.acm.org — [IBM Research publication page](https://research.ibm.com/publications/understanding-is-a-two-way-street-user-initiated-repair-on-agent-responses-and-hearing-in-conversational-interfaces).
- Open-access status: hybrid, CC-BY licence; Semantic Scholar counts 11 citations, OpenAlex counts 4 (different indexes) — [Semantic Scholar API record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3641026?fields=title,abstract,tldr,openAccessPdf,year,venue,authors,citationCount,externalIds); [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3641026).
- Abstract (paraphrased): chatbots and voice assistants can ask the user to paraphrase, but users cannot easily do the same when they fail to understand the agent; Moore and Arar (2019) demonstrated nine types of user-initiated repair on agent responses that are common in natural conversation and showed users employ them correctly in text interfaces if taught; this small-scale study tests those repairs in "second position" (on the agent's response) in a voice interface; hearing- and speaking-oriented repairs emerged in the multimodal interface; the analysis is of user logs and transcripts — [IBM Research page](https://research.ibm.com/publications/understanding-is-a-two-way-street-user-initiated-repair-on-agent-responses-and-hearing-in-conversational-interfaces).
- Findings stated in the abstract: participants used understanding-oriented repairs in much the same way in text and voice; participants used hearing- and speaking-oriented repairs to manage troubles specific to the voice modality; conclusion that user-initiated repair features are valuable components of conversational interfaces — [IBM Research page](https://research.ibm.com/publications/understanding-is-a-two-way-street-user-initiated-repair-on-agent-responses-and-hearing-in-conversational-interfaces); Semantic Scholar's TLDR says the same — [Semantic Scholar API record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3641026?fields=title,abstract,tldr,openAccessPdf,year,venue,authors,citationCount,externalIds).
- Indexed keywords: chatbots, conversational interfaces, multimodal interfaces, text-based interfaces, speech interfaces, mutual understanding, voice assistants, repair strategies; 17 referenced works — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3641026).
- The nine-type list itself is not given in any page I could reach; citing papers describe it only as "nine types of user-initiated repair on agent responses" — [Ngo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24628v1).
- SECONDARY, unattributed: a chatbot-platform pattern library lists user-initiated repair types on a bot's utterance with example utterances: general ("What?", "I don't get it", "That's not what I asked"), definition request, repeat request, partial repeat request ("Size 10 what?"), paraphrase request ("What do you mean?", "Can you say that another way?"), example request, understanding check ("Delectable means delicious?"), correction (the user corrects a bot-stated price), and a voice-only hearing check; the page says the bot's response depends on content and use case, suggests meeting a general "I don't get it" with a paraphrase, and gives one fallback chain (answer a definition request, then a general repair, then transfer) — [OpenDialog, Types of repair](https://docs.opendialog.ai/opendialog-platform/conversation-designer/conversation-design/conversational-patterns/repair/types-of-repair).
- SECONDARY, student-authored deck on the Natural Conversation Framework: lists Repeat Request (full or partial repetition of the agent's utterance) and Paraphrase Request (turn-level help) among conversation-navigation patterns, plus Capability Check, Sequence Closer and Sequence Abort; content-format principles: keep agent utterances to one sentence or less, break paragraphs into parts, give the shortest complete answer and add detail on request — [SlideShare, Natural Conversation Framework](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/natural-conversation-framework/249990693).
- Related CA-informed chatbot work found only at search-snippet level (not read): a 2024 study of how Google Assistant and Siri handle "huh?" reports ten assistant-generated repair strategies and an inability to replicate human-like ones — [Galbraith 2024, Frontiers in Robotics and AI](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2024.1356847/pdf); a 2026 LREC paper reports that LLMs align weakly with human annotators on recognising repair initiation, especially trouble-signalling — [Ngo et al. 2026, LREC](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.547/).

### Inferences
- The paper's central design claim (users need a way to repair the agent's turn, and will use natural forms such as "what do you mean?" when the agent handles them) maps directly onto the Yuna bug in the brief: a fan's "What do you mean?" is a user-initiated repair on her response, not a pushback.
- The types in the secondary list (repeat, partial repeat, paraphrase, definition, example, understanding check, correction) are consistent with the vocabulary of Moore's framework as described in the deck, but I cannot confirm they are the paper's nine, nor the ideal agent response for each.
- The paper is about an agent in a task or information setting and about voice versus text modality; nothing in the abstract concerns a persona with its own life, intimacy, or an LLM-driven writer.

### Gaps
- The full text (taxonomy with example utterances, ideal agent response per type, N, tasks, system, hearing/speaking-oriented types, numbers, limitations) could not be retrieved: ACM returned 403 to fetches and a scripted download, and the built-in browser hit a Cloudflare human-verification step that I did not complete. ## 2. Paper 2: Jiang, Chen, Zhang, Li & LC (2026), "Hear You in Silence"

### Takeaway
A between-subjects study (N=50, 25 per arm, two ten-minute supportive role-play scenarios each) compared a GPT-4o agent that inserts context-chosen silences of 0.5 to 16 seconds with strategy-specific status-bar text and strategy-specific response instructions against an agent that replies at once under a generic prompt; the paced agent scored higher on human-likeness, smoothness and interactivity in both scenarios and on perceived listening and affective trust only in the career scenario (rank-biserial r about 0.30 to 0.48), with more emotion words, first-person pronouns and turns, but timing was bundled with prompt content and interface cues, delays were seconds not minutes, and a notable minority read the slowness as malfunction or low effort.

### Cited Findings
- Record: arXiv 2602.06134, submitted 5 Feb 2026, 29 pages, 10 figures, conditionally accepted to CHI '26, related DOI 10.1145/3772318.3791238; authors Zhihan Jiang, Qianhui Chen, Chu Zhang, Yanheng Li, Ray LC — [arXiv abstract page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06134).
- Formative study: ten YouTube "Active Listening Counseling" videos (20 to 60 minutes, English, counselling or simulated counselling, visible listener behaviour), coded by three researchers for silence segments (strategy, trigger, duration, timing, surrounding talk), 289 pacing instances; counts across the ten cases: Resolve 84, Reconfirm 79, Recognize 62, Reconsider 17, Resonate 17, Re-engage 12, Reposition 12, Holding 6 — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Five pacing types and eight strategies (Table 1, paraphrased): Reflective Silence = Recognize (user wants feelings validated or hints at wanting advice; reflect feelings back; 1 to 2 s, placed after transition words such as "but" or "maybe"); Facilitative Silence = Reconfirm (vague or contradictory statement; restate key points gently and invite elaboration; 2 to 3 s) and Re-engage (story fades or stops; short unfinished phrase ending in an ellipsis; 2 to 3 s); Empathic Silence = Reposition (stuck in blame or hopelessness; acknowledge emotion then offer an exploratory reframe; 5 to 6 s), Reconsider (rigid belief or automatic thought; weigh whether a reply could hurt, then a reflective question; 2 to 3 s), Resonate (absorbed in an emotional story; let it settle, then respond emotionally; 3 to 15 s); Holding Space = Holding (repeated intense or vulnerable material; guide emotional release such as breathing; 3 to 16 s, opened with a breathing instruction); Immediate Response = Resolve (asks for information or a direct answer; answer concisely with no deliberate silence; 0 s) — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Appendix B silence ranges in milliseconds: Resolve 0; Recognize 500 to 1000; Reconfirm, Re-engage, Reconsider 2500 to 3000; Reposition 5500 to 6000; Resonate 3500 to 15000; Holding 3500 to 16000; some example outputs in the appendix (80, 150, 7000, 8000 ms) fall outside these ranges and the authors do not address this — [arXiv full text, appendix](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Punctuation pauses, sampled uniformly: commas, dashes, parentheses, colons, semicolons, quotes 100 to 150 ms; periods, question and exclamation marks 100 to 150 ms; line breaks 150 to 300 ms; ellipses 1000 to 2000 ms — [arXiv full text, Table 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Interface: a browser chat window with Flask, LangChain and GPT-4o; memory keeps the last 8 turns verbatim and summarises older ones; both conditions share a streaming transport; the control shows only "Thinking…" and "Answering…"; the experimental arm adds strategy-specific status text (reflecting quietly; waiting; in holding space; reflecting and answering), deliberately not naming the strategy so as not to break immersion; after 60 seconds of user inactivity the paced agent sends a strategy-labelled check-in; whether the control also had the check-in or the punctuation pauses is not stated — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Prompts differ in content, not only timing: the experimental response module receives per-strategy instructions (paraphrased: for Recognize, reflect back what you hear with openers such as "I see" or "It sounds like"; for Reconfirm, repeat the user's key phrase back gently as a question; for Resolve, answer concisely without emotional framing; for Resonate, acknowledge the weight of the emotion; for Holding, offer a calming prompt that turns attention inward), while the control prompt is only an expert-persona line with the goal of a supportive, reflective conversation plus a plain-HTML formatting rule — [arXiv full text, Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Participants: N=50 (P1 to P25, N1 to N25), recruited by word of mouth and social media, English proficiency required; 30% male, 66% female, rest non-binary or undisclosed; 62% aged 18 to 24; 64% students; 58% daily CA users; all East Asian (stated in limitations); no demographic differences between arms (age p=0.56, gender p=0.55, employment p=0.14, CA experience p=0.25) — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Task: each participant did a career-support and a relationship-support scenario (counterbalanced), each at least 10 minutes, after reading a second-person vignette, with a list of common questions available; the "relationship" scenario is support for the participant's own romantic difficulties (communication breakdowns, trust, conflict), not a romantic conversation with the agent — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Measures (7-point Likert): perceived listening (6 items, alpha 0.85; one item asks whether the response pace made the chatbot seem sensitive to what the user said), human-likeness (5 semantic pairs, alpha 0.855), cognitive trust (4 items, 0.873), affective trust (3 items, 0.803), smoothness (4 items, 0.879), interactivity (1 item on whether the pace felt interactive), a 2-item manipulation check (pauses intentionally; slows down appropriately), open questions, semi-structured interviews, logs; behaviour: NRC emotion-word counts, first-person pronouns, input length, total turns, strategy distribution; Mann-Whitney U with rank-biserial r. There is no frustration scale; frustration appears only in interviews — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Manipulation check: perceived pace change, experimental M=5.68 (SD 1.25) career and 5.40 (1.00) relationship versus control 2.92 (1.50) and 3.24 (1.39); U=56, p<0.001, r=0.71 (career) and U=64, p<0.001, r=0.70 (relationship); appropriateness 5.12 (1.05) and 5.20 (1.08) versus 2.96 (1.46) and 3.04 (1.46), U=78.5, p<0.001, r=0.65 and 0.68 — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Strategy use in the study (Table 4, 426 pacing instances, career/relationship): Reconfirm 103 (47/56), Resolve 94 (49/45), Recognize 70 (33/37), Reposition 60 (38/22), Re-engage 41 (23/18), Reconsider 35 (16/19), Resonate 15 (5/10), Holding 8 (5/3); classifier accuracy 86.2% overall (career 90.7%, relationship 81.4%), lowest for Resonate (73.3%) and Reconsider (77.1%); strategy mix did not differ by scenario (chi-square 8.40, df 7, p=0.30) — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- RQ1, career scenario (experimental vs control): perceived listening 5.74 (0.89) vs 5.05 (1.06), U=188, p=0.016, r=0.34; affective trust 5.45 vs 4.73, U=197, p=0.024, r=0.32; human-likeness 4.26 vs 3.42, U=182, p=0.011, r=0.36; smoothness 4.80 vs 4.14, U=205, p=0.024, r=0.30; interactivity 5.56 vs 4.08, U=141, p=0.001, r=0.48; cognitive trust not significant — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- RQ1, relationship scenario: human-likeness 4.16 vs 3.36, U=206, p=0.039, r=0.30; smoothness 4.73 vs 3.99, U=180, p=0.010, r=0.36; interactivity 5.64 vs 4.56, U=162, p=0.002, r=0.43; perceived listening, affective trust and cognitive trust not significant (no means given in the text) — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- RQ2 behaviour: total emotion words higher in the paced arm (U=359.0, p=0.04, r=0.301); per-category results are reported inconsistently between the figure caption (p<0.05 for trust, negative, disgust; p<0.1 for sadness, fear, surprise) and the body text; first-person pronouns higher (U=382.0, p=0.001, r=0.384); input length 19.91 vs 18.15 words (p=0.06, r=0.268, marginal); total turns higher (U=520.0, p<0.01, r=0.884) — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Qualitative themes (paraphrased): pauses were read as careful thought, special attention, or a breath or sigh, giving a sense that someone was present; the instant control agent was described as picking up a few words and copying information, and its bullet-point replies to personal sharing felt mechanical; participants said pacing let them read line by line and start typing before the reply finished and reduced scrolling back; one participant forgot what they wanted to say during a reflective pause; one said the exchange felt like counselling and drew out more disclosure — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- The double-edged sword: for a notable minority (N11, P18, P11, P23, P13, P8 are cited) slowness violated a machine-efficiency expectation; it was read as the AI not putting in effort, as something an intelligent system should not do, as irritation and suspicion of malfunction (despite the status bar), and in one case as poor model capability leading to disengagement; the section does not count how many felt this — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Discussion and design implications (paraphrased): learn individual pacing personas rather than one setting; pacing is a promissory note, so a long pause followed by a weak reply hurts trust more than an immediate weak reply, hence reserve long reflective pauses for high-confidence turns and use fast transactional pacing when confidence is low, with a visible "Reflecting…" cue for high-effort pauses and a plain acknowledgement instead when confidence is low; negative reactions may be a temporary adaptation cost, so start new users faster and introduce nuanced pacing gradually; informational strategies (Resolve, Reconfirm) are frequent hygiene factors while affective ones (Holding, Resonate) are rare but high-stakes; model pacing as a state machine so that a holding state after distress persists through a minor factual question instead of oscillating fast-slow; for relationship users the content of validation may matter more than a visible thought process, which the authors offer as a possible reason the listening and trust effects appeared only in the career scenario (root cause not investigated) — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Limitations stated by the authors: free-form conversation meant turn-by-turn content could not be controlled, and semantic analysis cannot rule out subtle differences; individual differences and interaction inertia; in text-only interaction with a minimal identity, silence can be misread as system lag, and a stronger persona might help (persona not controlled); all participants East Asian, so pause meanings may not generalise; strategies were tested as a bundle, so no single strategy's effect is isolated; formative data from ten videos by one creator; predefined role-play scenarios limit ecological validity — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).

### Inferences
- The manipulation worked as a perception of pace (r about 0.70), but every outcome effect is a bundle of (a) silence, (b) strategy-specific content instructions, (c) strategy-specific status text, and possibly (d) the inactivity check-in and punctuation pauses; the authors' own description of the control contradicts the claim that only the high-level prompt differed. The safest reading: "deliberate pauses plus listening-shaped content, signposted on screen" beat "instant generic support", not "delay alone helps".
- The attentiveness and trust gains were scenario-specific (career only) and modest (r 0.32 to 0.34), while the robust gains were human-likeness, smoothness and interactivity (both scenarios). The turn-count statistic (r=0.884) is out of line with the other effects and may reflect how turns were counted under the inactivity check-in; do not lean on it.
- Immediate Response was chosen for roughly 22% of turns (94 of 426) and was the paper's only zero-delay strategy, reserved for information requests; the affective silences (Resonate, Holding) covered about 5% of turns. The paper's own counsel is that these rare moments deserve disproportionate care.

### Gaps
- No condition tested delays longer than 16 seconds, asynchronous messaging, or a persona with its own life; nothing on sexting, flirting or sales; no frustration scale; no long-term or repeat-user data; non-significant relationship-scenario means are not reported in the text I could read.

## 3. ADJACENT (CA): what repair types exist in talk and how people resolve each in one turn

### Takeaway
Across languages and in English, a recipient signals trouble with one of three formats (open "huh?/what?", a restricted request that names the part at issue, or a restricted offer of a candidate understanding), speakers pick the most specific format they can, and the fix is almost always done in one turn and kept about as long as the trouble source: a repeat for open requests, a repeat or clarification for restricted requests, a confirmation for candidate offers.

### Cited Findings
- Other-initiated repair occurs in all 12 languages sampled (8 families), 2,053 cases in 48.5 hours, about once every 1.4 minutes; three formats account for a mean 92% of cases: open request (e.g. "Huh?", mean 3.7 characters), restricted request (e.g. "Who?", 10.4 characters), restricted offer (a candidate for confirmation, 13.0 characters) — [Dingemanse et al. 2015, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).
- Three universal principles: specificity (people choose the most specific initiator they can; open initiators are commoner in noise or overlap and rarer when the trouble source is an answer, 9%; after a failed attempt initiators get more specific), conservation (the two-turn repair costs about 1.2 times the trouble-source turn, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.47), division of labour (the trouble-source speaker's share of the work falls as the initiator gets more specific) — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).
- Repair solutions shrink as initiators get more specific (chi-square 50.7, p<0.00001); the canonical solution is repetition for open requests, repetition or clarification for restricted requests, and confirmation for restricted offers — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).
- English corpus (227 cases, 15 video recordings, 411 minutes, UK and US): 81.5% of initiations locate a specific trouble source and only 18.5% are open; the most frequent format is a candidate understanding (28.2%), then open (18.5%: "what?" 17, "huh?" 17, "what's that?" 4, "sorry?/pardon?" 3), category-specific question words (18.1%), repeats (17.6%), other-corrections (5.7%), copular questions such as "who's X" (4.0%); "what do you mean?" occurs 4 times and "I don't understand" once — [Kendrick 2015, Open Linguistics](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- "What do you mean?" specifies the trouble type (understanding) but not its location; in the corpus case the speaker of a muddled question answered it by re-asking the question in plainer words and the sequence moved on; "I don't understand" appeared only as a last resort after several failed repairs — [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- "Why?" is a request for an account, not a repair initiation — [Kendrick 2015, citing Bolden and Robinson 2010](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Cascading troubles: when each repair solution introduces new unexplained material (a playful example where "ready for what?" is answered with a new opaque phrase, and so on), the sequence expands until a last-resort "I don't understand" — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Candidate understandings (the recipient offers "you mean X?"-style guesses by replacement, continuation or insertion) are the strongest and preferred format; "you mean" itself prefixes only 3% of them, while a turn-initial "what" prefixes 23.4% — [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Other-correction (the recipient asserts a replacement, e.g. correcting a mispronounced name) is rare, consistent with the preference for self-correction; the corrected speaker simply repeats the corrected item and moves on — [Kendrick 2015, Section 4.7](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- A full repeat of someone's question can treat the question as a whole as problematic, even "ridiculous" — [Kendrick 2015, citing Robinson and Kevoe-Feldman 2010](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Open-class initiators ("pardon?", "sorry?", "what?") in telephone data cluster after an abrupt topic shift or an inapposite, even disaffiliative, response, so that the trouble shades into alignment and conflict — [Drew 1997, abstract](https://emcawiki.net/Drew1997); the apology-format "sorry?" places responsibility for the trouble on the person asking — [Robinson 2006, via search summary](https://emcawiki.net/Robinson2006a).

### Inferences
- In text chat there are no hearing troubles (the fan can re-read), so the "repeat" solution that is canonical for "huh?" in speech almost never fits; an open "huh?/wait what" in text is best treated as an understanding trouble or, per Drew, as a signal that her last message did not fit what came before.
- The conservation principle gives a length rule: the fix should be about the size of the muddled line, not a paragraph.
- When her own reading of his message is unsure, CA says the preferred move is a specific candidate understanding, not an open "what do you mean?"; this is relevant to the Claude reader's low-confidence readings.

### Gaps
- No corpus study of repair frequencies in text or messaging chat was found in this pass; all frequencies above are from spoken, mostly face-to-face data.

## 4. ADJACENT (CA): telling a confusion request from a challenge

### Takeaway
The same forms ("what do you mean?", "what?", a repeat) do repair in one place and challenge, tease, pre-disagreement or disbelief in another; the usable cues are whether the added words quote her turn or supply counter-grounds, whether her turn was plainly understandable, whether her turn was an assessment or a tease that invites a stance, and what his next turn does after she fixes it.

### Cited Findings
- Repair initiations can serve as vehicles for displays of doubt, non-alignment, disagreement, challenge and rejection; a recipient may answer both the repair and the incipient disagreement, for example by backing down — [Schegloff 1997, Discourse Processes](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf).
- "What do you mean" with or without an added X does repair when the X is taken from the other person's earlier turn (a guest quoting the host's remark about a weaving, which the host then explains in plainer terms), whereas in the challenge use the X is "the grounds of a challenge to her recipient's prior turn" (one sister questioning the other's claim by citing what doctors do); challenge turns need no X at all, as in a protest shouted at a tease — [Schegloff 1997](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf).
- Two repair uses in the same paper: a hotline worker's vague question about the caller's relationship gets "what do you mean?" and is answered with a plainer, more explicit version of the question; a caller's vague "what happened today" gets the same initiator and is answered by adding the missing specifics (where, when) — [Schegloff 1997, Extracts 26 and 27](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf).
- Schegloff asks whether the challenge use of "what do you mean" contaminates its repair use, making it more likely to be heard as disagreement, and notes that Garfinkel's breaching experiments, built almost entirely on "what do you mean?", were heard as challenges — [Schegloff 1997, footnote 3](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf).
- Three classes of non-repair use in English: non-serious actions (jokes, teases), preliminaries to dispreferred responses (challenges, rejections, disagreements), and displays of surprise or disbelief; the decisive evidence is the response: when the recipient confirms or corrects, the turn was treated as repair; when the recipient defends or reaffirms instead (answering "the concrete jungle?" with "I think it's quite pretty"; answering "what, on Saturday?" with "I think you should"), it was a pseudo-repair doing disagreement or pre-rejection — [Kendrick 2015, Section 6](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Structural basis: a repair initiation after a first action (assessment, proposal, invitation) displaces the response and breaks contiguity, which itself signals that a dispreferred response may be coming; in one case the trouble-source speaker, sensing this, backed down (adding a tag question and softening "a week" to "ten days") before the other then disagreed outright — [Kendrick 2015, Extracts 25 and 26](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- A high, level-pitched, drawn-out "what" feigns incomprehension to do objection or protest (after a complaint or accusation) or surprise and disbelief (after an extreme report); it takes for granted that the speaker heard and understood — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 29](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- The usefulness of repair forms for disagreement rests on their being hearable as not about disagreement; if challenge were their first-order use, their softening value would be lost — [Kendrick 2015, footnote 9](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Jokes and teases "inhabit" repair forms; a tease dressed as a candidate understanding (offering an exaggerated replacement for what the other said) is met with laughter, not confirmation — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 24](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).

### Inferences
- Signals that a "what do you mean?" is confusion, not offence (derived from the above): no added words, or added words that quote her line; her line was in fact ambiguous, novel or elliptical; her line was a share or a question rather than a verdict on him; no counter-ground follows. Signals of a challenge: added words that state grounds against her ("I just subscribed", "doctors use it"); her line was plain and he still asks; her line was an assessment, a proposal, a tease or a compliment deflection; emphasis markers (caps, exclamation, doubled question marks) that in text may play the role of the high-pitch "what".
- "What's that supposed to mean?" is not covered by any source I read; treating it as offence is an inference by analogy with the grounds-bearing challenge form, not a finding.
- The brief's examples line up with the CA categories: "why do you like painting?" is an account request, not repair; "wow, that's beautiful" is an assessment, not repair; "why finally? I just subscribed" is a grounds-bearing challenge; "what do you mean?" after "I could use some of that right now" is an understanding-type repair on an ambiguous line.
- Because people give the benefit of the doubt and answer the repair first (Kendrick Extract 25: the speaker simply restated, then the disagreement came), a safe default when the reader is unsure is to fix the understanding problem in one short line and let his next turn reveal whether a challenge follows.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how often "what do you mean?" is a challenge versus a repair in text chat; the CA material is qualitative on this point.

## 5. What a good repair turn looks like, what a bad one looks like, and whether to add a question back

### Takeaway
The human pattern is one short turn that says the same thing more plainly or adds the missing specific, costs about as much as the muddled line, carries no apology or defence, and then lets the original thread resume; a question back is part of the fix only when the muddled line was itself a question, and the recipient's "you mean X?" guess is the preferred way to ask when it is she who is unsure.

### Cited Findings
- Fixes are one turn and about 1.2 times the trouble source in length; open requests get a repeat, restricted requests a repeat or clarification, candidate offers a confirmation — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).
- Repair solutions to "what do you mean?" in the data are a plainer restatement (the hotline worker rephrasing his question explicitly) or a restatement with specifics added (where and when something happened) — [Schegloff 1997, Extracts 26 and 27](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf); a host explaining her cryptic remark opens with "I mean" and gives a simple, concrete account, after which the guest shows understanding ("oh, so it's square") — [Schegloff 1997, Extract 28](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf).
- After a successful fix the original sequence moves forward at once (a repeated question gets its answer in the next turn) — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 1](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf); fixes that introduce new unexplained material cascade into further repairs — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- When the recipient corrects a fact, the corrected speaker repeats the correction and continues; such corrections are rare because self-correction is preferred — [Kendrick 2015, Section 4.7](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Apology-format initiators ("sorry?") assign responsibility to the asker, which is why they differ from "what?" — [Robinson 2006, via search summary](https://emcawiki.net/Robinson2006a).
- The paced-agent study instructed its Reconfirm strategy to repeat the user's key phrase back as a gentle question when the user's statement was vague, and its Resolve strategy to answer a direct question concisely without emotional framing — [Jiang et al. 2026, Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- A chatbot pattern library suggests meeting a general "I don't get it" with a paraphrase of the previous bot turn — [OpenDialog, Types of repair](https://docs.opendialog.ai/opendialog-platform/conversation-designer/conversation-design/conversational-patterns/repair/types-of-repair).

### Inferences
- Good repair turn (derived): one line, same meaning in plainer words or with the missing specific added, optionally opened with "I mean"; no sorry, no defence, no new metaphor; then back to the thread (answer his pending question or continue her share). In the brief's case, "I could use some of that right now" should be followed by a line saying what "that" was and why she wants it now, not by owning a fault.
- Bad repair turn (derived): repeating the identical line (that is the hearing fix, useless in text); answering a question he did not ask; apologising or explaining why she wrote it that way (turns a two-second side sequence into a topic); introducing a second unexplained idea (cascade risk); treating the repair as a pushback and going defensive, which per Kendrick is exactly the response that marks the exchange as a disagreement.
- A question back: when her muddled line was a question, the fix is a clearer question (the sources show exactly this); when her muddled line was a share, the fix is a plainer statement and no question is needed; adding a fresh question is a style choice the sources do not support and the Yuna "few questions" rule argues against. When she is the one unsure about his message, a single candidate understanding ("you mean the trip?") is the preferred form over an open "what do you mean?".
- For a fact correction from him, the human pattern is to accept and repeat the corrected item in passing and continue; no apology beyond a light acknowledgement is modelled in the data.

### Gaps
- No experimental study compares repair-turn designs (plain restatement vs apology vs repeat) for user satisfaction in text chat; Paper 1 may contain relevant guidance per type, but I could not read it.

## 6. Pacing: what the study varied, what helped, and what it says about Yuna's delays and typing time

### Takeaway
The pacing study varied seconds of silence before a streamed reply, chosen per turn by a classifier, together with status text and listening-shaped content; it helped human-likeness, smoothness and interactivity, helped attentiveness and affective trust only in the advice-plus-feelings scenario, and hurt a minority who read slowness as malfunction; its single clear rule for speed is "answer direct information requests at once", and it says nothing about minute-scale delays, sexting, or typing speed.

### Cited Findings
- What varied: deliberate silence of 0 to 16 s by strategy, short pauses at punctuation inside the streamed reply (0.1 to 0.3 s, 1 to 2 s at ellipses), strategy-specific status text, a breathing instruction before Holding silences, a 60-second inactivity check-in, and per-strategy content instructions; the control replied with only API latency under a generic prompt — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- What helped: human-likeness (r 0.36 and 0.30), smoothness (0.30 and 0.36), interactivity (0.48 and 0.43) in both scenarios; perceived listening (0.34) and affective trust (0.32) in the career scenario only; more emotion words (r 0.30), first-person pronouns (0.38), turns — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Fast is better for information requests: Immediate Response (0 s) is reserved for direct answers, used for about 22% of turns, and the authors state that a long silence in a simple information exchange would frustrate — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Slow is better for emotional disclosure and distress in their data (Resonate, Holding), with the caveat that in a crisis a long silence can feel like abandonment, so a visible status cue is recommended — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Participants reading the status bar still sometimes assumed the delay was a loading problem — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Pacing is a promissory note: content after a long pause must deliver, otherwise trust falls more than after an instant weak reply; use long pauses only when confident in the reply — [Jiang et al. 2026, Section 6.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Keep pacing state across turns (a holding state after distress should persist through a minor factual question) to avoid fast-slow oscillation — [Jiang et al. 2026, Section 6.2.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- In text-only interaction with a minimal identity, silence is ambiguous and may read as lag; a stronger persona may make pauses read charitably — [Jiang et al. 2026, Section 6.6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- The paper does not describe typing indicators, typing speed, or multi-minute delays; both arms used the same streaming transport — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Separately, repair initiations in human talk occur in the next turn and are resolved immediately; open initiators occur in next position 90.5% of the time — [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).

### Inferences
- Yuna's 1 to 4 minute first-reply delay is outside anything the study tested; the study neither supports nor undermines it. What transfers is the mechanism: a delay reads well only if (a) something on screen marks it as attention rather than absence, (b) the content that follows is worth the wait, and (c) direct questions are not made to wait. Yuna's persona (a person with a life) supplies a charitable reading of silence that the study's minimal counsellor lacked, which the authors themselves flag as a likely moderator.
- The strongest evidence-backed adjustment is a speed exception: when his message is a direct question or a correction, the delay before her first bubble should be at the short end, and the answer should come first; when his message is a disclosure, the longer delay is defensible and the first bubble should land on the feeling (Recognize or Resonate shape) rather than on information.
- A repair turn ("what do you mean?") is sequentially urgent in human talk (next turn, immediately); making a confused fan wait minutes compounds the trouble. Treat repair requests like direct questions for pacing.
- Nothing in either paper supports a sexting-specific pacing rule; any "faster during sexting" setting would rest on the practice-room evidence, not on these sources.

### Gaps
- No study found on reply latency in asynchronous romantic or creator-fan messaging, on typing-indicator effects for persona chat, or on gaps between message bubbles.

## 7. Example drafts for the practice room's example library (proposals)

### Takeaway
Three example shapes can be written for the library, modelled on the CA extracts: a clarification that restates plainly and resumes, a fact correction that accepts and repeats the corrected item, and a joke that landed wrong where she drops the tease frame and says what she meant; all are drafts for review, not tested lines.

### Cited Findings
- Model for clarification: muddled question or remark, "what do you mean?", a plainer version with the missing specific, then the thread resumes — [Schegloff 1997, Extracts 26 to 28](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf); [Kendrick 2015, Extract 19](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Model for correction: the corrected speaker repeats the corrected item and continues — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 20](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Model for a tease that misfires: teases live in repair forms and are met with laughter when taken as teases, and a protest in "what do you mean" form marks that a tease was taken as a put-down — [Kendrick 2015, Extract 24](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf); [Schegloff 1997, Extract 31](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf).
- Length rule: the fix about 1.2 times the trouble source — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).

### Inferences
- Draft A, clarification (the real case). Her: "I could use some of that right now." Him: "What do you mean?" Her fix: "that quiet you were describing. my flat's been chaos all week and I want ten minutes of it." Then resume: answer whatever he asked before, or continue. Notes for the writer: no sorry, no "I phrased that badly", one line, the word "that" is replaced by the thing it pointed at.
- Draft B, fact correction. Him: "I'm from Leeds, not Leicester lol." Her: "Leeds. right, the rain and the good pubs. so was it a Leeds pub where that happened?" Notes: repeat the corrected item once, light acknowledgement, pick the thread back up; no apology paragraph.
- Draft C, joke landed wrong. Her tease: "you'd last two minutes on my hike." Him: "what's that supposed to mean?" Her fix: "that I'd be dragging you up the last bit and enjoying it. it was me flirting, not a dig." Notes: drop the tease frame, name the intended action in plain words, one line, no "it was just a joke" defence, no second joke.
- Draft D, open-class in text. Her: a line that jumps topic. Him: "wait what". Her: restate the line with the link to what came before ("sorry, jumped. I meant the thing you said about your brother: that's why I asked"). Notes: open-class after a topic jump is a fit problem (Drew), so the fix supplies the connection, not a repeat.
- Draft E, she is the unsure one. Him: an ambiguous share. Her: one candidate understanding, not an open "what do you mean?" ("you mean the ex, or the flatmate?"), then wait.

### Gaps
- These are untested drafts; no corpus of fan-creator chat repairs was available to validate wording.

## 8. What Yuna could use (proposals)

### Takeaway
Split the engine's single repair rule into three handlers (confusion, correction, offence), decide between them with the cues from Section 4 plus the Claude reader's confidence, fix confusion with a one-line plain restatement that keeps the thread, give repairs and direct questions the short end of the reply delay, and let longer delays stand only where the next bubble will carry weight.

### Cited Findings
- Users need a way to repair the agent's turn and use natural forms when allowed — [Moore, An & Marrese 2024, abstract](https://research.ibm.com/publications/understanding-is-a-two-way-street-user-initiated-repair-on-agent-responses-and-hearing-in-conversational-interfaces).
- Repair form versus action cues, and the response as the decisive evidence — [Schegloff 1997](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf); [Kendrick 2015, Section 6](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Specificity and conservation principles — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).
- Immediate response for direct requests, promissory-note risk, state persistence, status cues — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).

### Inferences
- Proposal 1, classification: route his message by (a) form (open "huh?/what?/wait what"; "what do you mean (by X)?"; a quote of her words with a question mark; "you mean X?"; a correction of a fact; "why…?"; an assessment) and (b) action cues (does the X quote her or argue against her; was her line clear; was her line a verdict, tease or proposal; emphasis markers). Only the grounds-bearing or emphasis-marked cases go to the offence handler; "why" questions go to answering, not repair; assessments go to receipt.
- Proposal 2, confusion handler: one line, plainer restatement or the missing specific, optional "I mean", no apology or explanation of why, then continue the pending thread (his earlier question first). Never repeat the identical line.
- Proposal 3, correction handler: accept, repeat the corrected item once, continue; store the corrected fact in his fan memory so it is not repeated.
- Proposal 4, offence handler (the existing rule): keep "own it lightly in one line, no excuse"; but since the CA data show people first answer the repair and then meet the disagreement, allow a plain restatement of what she meant before the light owning when the reader is unsure whether he is confused or offended.
- Proposal 5, when she is unsure about his message: one candidate understanding, not an open "what do you mean?", and only one, because the specificity principle says the guess carries more of the work.
- Proposal 6, pacing: treat repair requests, direct questions and corrections as Immediate Response turns (short end of the delay, answer first); keep the longer delay for disclosures and keep a slow state through the next trivial question after a disclosure; make sure a long delay is never followed by a thin line.
- Proposal 7, practice room: add the five drafts above and a scorecard check "repair resolved in one turn without apology or cascade".

### Gaps
- None of these proposals has been tested; the papers give no numbers for persona chat, so the practice room's test chats remain the only validation path.

## 9. What not to infer

### Takeaway
Do not read Paper 2 as evidence that delays help in general, do not treat its listening and trust effects as robust across scenarios, do not cite Paper 1's nine types from this report, and do not transfer spoken-language repair frequencies or the "repeat" solution to text chat.

### Cited Findings
- Paper 2's effects bundle timing with content instructions and interface cues; the authors acknowledge content could not be controlled — [Jiang et al. 2026, Section 6.6.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Listening and affective trust effects appeared only in the career scenario; cognitive trust never moved — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Delays were 0.5 to 16 seconds within a synchronous session; participants were East Asian, mostly students aged 18 to 24; scenarios were role-play; the agent was a counsellor persona — [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134).
- Paper 1's taxonomy and findings could not be read; the nine types were not listed in any accessible source — [IBM Research page](https://research.ibm.com/publications/understanding-is-a-two-way-street-user-initiated-repair-on-agent-responses-and-hearing-in-conversational-interfaces).
- CA frequencies come from face-to-face and telephone talk; "what do you mean?" was 4 of 227 cases there — [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf).
- Repetition is the canonical fix for open requests in speech because they often signal hearing trouble — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759).

### Inferences
- Do not infer: that a 1 to 4 minute delay makes Yuna seem more attentive (untested range, different medium); that slower is better for romantic or sexual talk (no data); that frustration was measured (it was not); that status text reliably prevents a lag reading (it did not for some); that "more turns" means more engagement in Yuna's setting (the statistic is anomalous and the check-in may inflate it).
- Do not infer: that "what do you mean?" is usually a challenge (CA treats repair as the default and challenge as the special case, resolved by the response); that an apology is part of a good repair (no source models it); that a tease must be explained as a joke (the CA data show teases met with laughter, and the repair when it misfires is a plain statement of intent, which is my inference).
- Do not infer: that the secondary pattern list in Section 1 is Moore and Arar's nine types, or that Paper 1 studied LLM personas, intimacy, or sales.

### Gaps
- A human reader should open Paper 1 at its DOI and check the taxonomy and the per-type ideal responses before any engine rule is attributed to it.
