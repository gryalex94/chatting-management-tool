## Key question 1: Hentona et al. 2025 — design, the three willingness dimensions, what moved each, how the willingness-aware seller timed its pitch, and which annotation rules could be copied

### Takeaway
Hentona et al. built a 109-dialogue Wizard-of-Oz chat dataset in which the *user* rated every seller message on three separate willingness dimensions (keep talking, give information, accept the goal = buy) as positive / no effect / negative, plus a 7-point purchase-intention question before and after. Their core empirical claims: negatives are rare (3–6% of seller turns) but are the only labels that correlate with intention change (negatively); successful dialogues show rising goal-acceptance toward the end and roughly half to a third as many negatives; a GPT-3.5 fine-tuned with the labels and a turn-scheduled strategy (talk → elicit → close) raised the success rate from 0.23 to 0.44 in a 48-person test, while untuned GPT-4o reached 0.58. My re-analysis of the data confirms the paper's numbers and adds the overlap structure between the three dimensions and which kinds of seller lines drew negatives.

### Cited Findings

**Paper identity and scope**
- "User Willingness-aware Sales Talk Dataset", Asahi Hentona, Jun Baba, Shiki Sato (CyberAgent) and Reina Akama (Tohoku University), Proceedings of COLING 2025, Abu Dhabi, January 2025, pp. 11206–11217; also arXiv 2412.19490 — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742/); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19490)
- The paper defines three types of user willingness: (1) willingness to engage in / continue a dialogue (CD; equated with "user engagement" in open-domain dialogue research), (2) willingness to provide information (PI; the user's tendency to communicate needs and desires so the seller can match benefits), (3) willingness to accept the salesperson's objective (GA; readiness to embrace the system's primary objective, i.e. making a purchase) — [PDF, §1 and §5](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Envisioned deployment: a sales dialogue system on an e-commerce product page or ad landing page; the system speaks first; the user may leave at any time; the dialogue ends when the user leaves or buys; users are explicitly assumed to be not necessarily motivated to talk, disclose, or buy, and willingness is described as multifaceted and fluctuating within a dialogue (someone may love talking without intending to buy; a reluctant starter may become a buyer) — [PDF, §3.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)

**Design and N**
- Wizard of Oz: the user believes they chat with a "SalesBot"; the seller side is a human. Sellers: five crowd-recruited (Lancers) fluent Japanese speakers with sales experience, given two hours of guidance (talk-script crafting, dialogue practice, product knowledge) and told the user-side evaluation criteria with the instruction to aim for the highest ratings. Users: 109 crowd workers. Fictional wireless earphones, three models at 11,000 / 22,000 / 33,000 yen, shown on a web page beside a slurk-based chat tool; collection took about one month; participants were paid 1,500 yen/hour; ethics approval CAE-2023-06; users were debriefed afterwards that the partner was human — [PDF, §4.1–4.2, Appendix B, Ethics](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Table 1: 109 dialogues; 63 "success" dialogues; 3,289 utterances (mean 30.2 per dialogue, max 92, min 11); 1,144 user utterances (mean 10.5, max 41, min 3); 2,145 seller utterances (mean 19.7, max 52, min 7); 54,301 tokens (MeCab + UniDic) — [PDF, Table 1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- "Success" is defined as the 7-point purchase-intention rating after the dialogue being higher than before; 63 of 109 dialogues (57.8%) met it — [PDF, §4.3](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Appendix A: only 26 of 109 users had a pre-dialogue purchase intention below "4: Neither", and no user gave the lowest score of 1; the authors take this as evidence that the sample can show sales-talk effects — [PDF, Appendix A](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- The released file (JSON Lines, one dialogue per line) carries `dialogue_id`, `sales_id`, `user_id`, `user_dialogue_evals` (before/after purchase intention) and `utterances` with `speaker` ∈ {system, sales, user}, `message`, `timestamp` and `user_utterance_evals` carrying labels `CONTINUING_DIALOGUE`, `PROVIDING_INFORMATION`, `GOAL_ACCEPTANCE` with answers positive / neutral / negative; licence CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; the GitHub page reports the repository as archived (read-only) since 4 July 2026 — [GitHub README](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset)
- In the released data there are exactly 5 distinct `sales_id` values (SALES_0–4) and 109 distinct users; the file holds 3,684 lines of which 395 are system messages (instructions, product-card shares, purchase notices, end-of-dialogue notices); 2,145 seller + 1,144 user utterances match Table 1 [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)

**The exact annotation instrument (from the data file; my English rendering of the Japanese)**
- Each seller message carries the same three questions, each phrased "Did this message affect, *even a little* [emphasised in the original], your wish to … ?" with the object being, respectively, "keep the conversation going" (CD), "give information" (PI), "buy the product" (GA). The three answer options are: had a positive effect / had no effect at all / had a negative effect. User messages and system messages carry no labels [own reading of the data] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- The dialogue-level question (asked before and after) is "Regarding the listed product (wireless earphones), do you want to buy it at this moment?" on a 7-point scale whose anchors run: 1 not at all, 2 no, 3 not really, 4 neither, 5 a little, 6 yes, 7 very much [own reading of the data] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- The opening system message tells the user they may end the dialogue or buy at any time, must finish by pressing the end-dialogue or purchase button, and should keep within 30 minutes [own reading of the data] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- The JSAI 2024 precursor paper (98 dialogues, same setup) states the user-role participants annotated each seller utterance *after the dialogue ended*, and that the seller-role participants were separately asked to self-annotate which of their own utterances were intended to raise engagement (that seller-intent annotation is not in the released COLING file). It also reports 58/98 dialogues with increased purchase intent, mean dialogue length 18.7 min, and that avoiding engagement-lowering utterances matters more than producing engagement-raising ones — [JSAI 2024, J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/pjsai/JSAI2024/0/JSAI2024_3Xin240/_article/-char/ja/) (English abstract readable; Japanese body partly decoded, treat the "after the dialogue" detail as medium confidence)
- The NLP2025 domestic version's figure lists the dialogue-level evaluation as "purchase intention before/after (7-point)" plus "16 other items" that are not in the released file; it also notes the sellers were people with in-store sales experience who practised beforehand — [NLP2025 D4-2 PDF](https://www.anlp.jp/proceedings/annual_meeting/2025/pdf_dir/D4-2.pdf)

**What correlates with success (paper + own numbers)**
- The paper: the per-dialogue proportions of positive and neutral labels show almost no correlation with the improvement in purchase intention, while the proportion of negatives is negatively correlated (Figure 3 gives the coefficients only graphically). The authors' conclusion: to raise intent to buy it is crucial to avoid utterances that degrade willingness, rather than to try to produce utterances that improve it — [PDF, §5.1, Fig. 3](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- My coefficients for the same quantities: share of negatives vs intention change r = −0.47 (CD), −0.47 (PI), −0.38 (GA); share of positives r = +0.17, +0.11, +0.17 [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- The paper's turn-wise view (positive = +1, neutral = 0, negative = −1, dialogue length normalised to 1.0): dialogues that raised intention show a noticeable rise in willingness right after the start and just before the end, with GA rising substantially as the dialogue closes; dialogues that failed show all three scores falling shortly after the start and immediately before the end. Cumulative negatives in successful dialogues are about one-half to one-third of those in unsuccessful ones, and negative PI counts are lower in the middle of successful dialogues — [PDF, §5.2, Fig. 4–5](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- 47 of 109 dialogues ended with the user pressing the (fictional) purchase button [own computation]. Buyers vs non-buyers: pre-intention 4.47 vs 4.25; post-intention 5.66 vs 4.61; intention-up rate 74% vs 46%. Purchase rate by pre-intention: 2 → 3/8, 3 → 7/18, 4 → 12/31, 5 → 17/35, 6 → 6/12, 7 → 2/4 — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)

GA in the last three seller turns: buyers 56% positive / 6% negative, non-buyers 42% / 9%. In the *first* three seller turns non-buyers were slightly warmer (GA positive 37% vs 28%; CD 62% vs 57%; PI 60% vs 55%). Median relative position of the first GA-positive turn: buyers 0.24 of the dialogue, non-buyers 0.08 [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)

**When the human sellers pitched, and what happened [own computation; the paper has no such analysis]**
- The chat tool lets the seller "share" a product card (a system line "share from sales"). 61 of 109 dialogues contain at least one such share (mean 1.12 per dialogue); the first share came on average after 56% of the seller's turns (median 53%). Purchase rate by timing of the first share: first third 3/10 (30%), middle third 12/29 (41%), last third 13/22 (59%), no share at all 19/48 (40%). **Which kinds of seller lines raised or lowered each dimension [own keyword classes over seller text; baseline over all seller turns: CD 54% positive / 6% negative, PI 48/3, GA 42/6]**
- Questions of any kind (n=352): CD 55/4, PI 61/1, GA 28/2 — questions raise disclosure, rarely hurt, and do little for buying. Questions about the use situation ("where/when/how will you use them", n=89): CD 57/3, PI 58/1, GA 26/1. Budget questions (n=22): CD 59/14, PI 59/9, GA 41/14 — the highest negative rate of any question type — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Recommendations ("I recommend…", n=98): CD 62/6, PI 51/7, GA 56/11; by position, GA positive/negative = 44/7 in the first third (n=27), 57/14 in the middle (n=44), 67/11 in the last third (n=27). Soft "how about…?" checks (n=21): CD 71/0, PI 52/0, GA 52/0 — no negatives at all. Lines containing the word "purchase" (n=60): GA 53/2. Lines with a yen figure (n=105): GA 54/8 — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)

Bare "Thank you." turns (n=88): CD 51/1, PI 36/0, GA 33/0 and bare "Understood." turns (n=40): CD 30/2, PI 22/0, GA 20/0 — mostly inert. - Reading the negatively-labelled seller lines (18 per dimension, my translation), the recurring shapes are: stating a product's weakness or a risk unprompted ("canal-type earphones always carry a risk of falling out"; "unfortunately even the Pro can fall out"); refusing information ("sorry, we cannot compare with other makers' models"); empty or bot-like fillers ("That's right.", "Generating a response."); an upsell not grounded in what the user said ("if you care about sound, the top two models would satisfy you more"); money talk the user had not opened ("all models can be paid in three instalments"; a budget question answered negatively on all three dimensions); and dismissive reassurance ("no problem") — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)

**The willingness-aware systems and the 48-person evaluation**
- Four systems: (a) GPT-3.5 (gpt-3.5-turbo-0125) fine-tuned on the 63 successful dialogues only, no labels; (b) GPT-3.5W: fine-tuned on all 109 dialogues with attribute-conditioned SFT (SteerLM-style), the three willingness labels attached as attributes to every seller response, and at inference all three attributes set to "positive"; (c) GPT-3.5WD: same model, but the requested attributes follow the dialogue stage — turns 1–3 only CD positive, turns 4–6 only PI positive, from turn 7 only GA positive, others neutral; (d) untuned GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-05-13) as a reference — [PDF, §6.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- 48 crowd participants each talked with all four systems under the same procedure as data collection (pre/post intention, per-utterance labels), with counterbalanced order — [PDF, §6.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Table 3 (success rate = share of dialogues where purchase intent rose; mean turns): GPT-3.5 0.23 / 9.35; GPT-3.5W 0.33 / 9.23; GPT-3.5WD 0.44 / 9.08; GPT-4o 0.58 / 5.81. No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported — [PDF, Table 3](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Figure 6 (described in text): the baseline scored high willingness early but low at the end; W scored lower early and high at the end; WD scored higher than both in the early and final stages, matching its built-in schedule; GPT-4o scored higher throughout but its end-of-dialogue willingness was relatively low, which the authors flag as its improvement area (raising GA at the close) — [PDF, §7.1–7.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Failure analysis on 40 sampled dialogues (10 per model), two annotators: responses with "unclear intention" (example: user asks what differs from other products; the seller replies with a sentence about willingness to continue increasing, i.e. a leaked training attribute). Dialogues with ≥1 agreed unclear-intention seller utterance / successful dialogues out of 10: GPT-3.5 7 / 1; W 6 / 4; WD 3 / 4; GPT-4o 1 / 6 — [PDF, §7.3, Tables 4–5](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Stated strategy derived from the analysis: early — make the user comfortable and lift willingness so they do not leave; middle — raise and hold PI with probing questions about requirements; final — lift GA with closing talk that recommends products matching what was learned. Stated future work: a system that *dynamically switches* the target willingness type based on real-time user responses — [PDF, §5.2, §8](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Limitations the authors list: one product category; results may differ with price and user type; Japanese only; no real purchase, so genuine intent is uncertain; they plan a real-purchase demonstration — [PDF, Limitations](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Hentona's publication list shows no further sales-dialogue paper after the NLP2025 domestic version (his 2025–2026 work is on other topics), so the "dynamic switching" system has not been published as of this date — [Hentona's page](https://yaelanya.github.io/)

### Inferences
- The three dimensions are genuinely different signals, not one "engagement" meter: a third of the turns that made users want to keep talking did nothing for their wish to buy, and 1 in 20 seller turns raised the wish to buy without touching the wish to talk. Negatives, by contrast, travel together (two-thirds of CD-negatives are also GA-negatives).
- The data support "a friendly fan is not a ready fan" directly: non-buyers were slightly warmer in the first three turns, asked more questions and wrote more turns than buyers; what distinguished buyers was a late rise in goal-acceptance with few negatives, not more talk.
- The paper's seller never had a "readiness check" category; the nearest things in the data are "how about…?" lines (never negative, n=21) and the product-card share, which did better when it came later (59% vs 30%), with the caveat that late sharing is confounded with the user having stayed that long.
- "What moved each dimension" is not analysed in the paper; my keyword classes suggest: questions about the person move PI (and are safe), recommendations move GA (and carry the most risk after drawbacks), money questions are the riskiest questions, and fillers move nothing.
- The scheduled WD model shows that even a crude count-based schedule beats "always try to be positive on everything", but the authors themselves treat the schedule as a stand-in for reading the user's state turn by turn.

### Gaps
- The paper gives Figures 2–6 only graphically; the exact correlation coefficients and per-stage scores are not printed (my numbers fill some of this but are my computations, not theirs).
- No inter-rater reliability is possible or reported (each user rated only their own dialogue); no analysis by seller (5 wizards) or by user prior intent.
- Whether the per-utterance ratings were made live or after the dialogue is not stated in the COLING paper; the 2024 precursor says after the dialogue (medium confidence from partially decoded Japanese).
- The 16 extra dialogue-level questionnaire items mentioned in the NLP2025 version are not released and not described.
- No examples of the WD model's actual pitches, no count of turns before its first recommendation, and no per-model negative-label rates are published; "when it pitched" is known only from the attribute schedule (GA from turn 7, mean dialogue 9.08 turns).
- The arXiv version (2412.19490) was not diffed against the COLING camera-ready.

## Key question 2: Balducci, Marinova & Singh 2026 — communicative acts, turn-level receptivity, early vs late, conversion (abstract-only)

### Takeaway
The abstract establishes the frame and five numbers: salesperson speech in B2B lead calls is coded as linguistic acts (legitimizing, piquing, nudging) and paralinguistic acts (intensity, speech rate, pitch); these act on *lead receptivity*, a per-speaking-turn measure of openness to the sales message, which in turn drives conversation-level conversion; on 783 calls / 11,566 turns, +1 SD receptivity → +13.44% conversion likelihood; more specific nudges raise receptivity +11.82% early but lower it −81.40% late; faster speech is neutral early and −8.26% late. Nothing beyond this (the coding manual, the receptivity measure, the early/late cut, the acts' definitions, the conversion definition, tables) could be obtained.

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic: "EXPRESS: Ready to Convert? How to Talk with Sales Leads", Bitty Balducci (corresponding), Detelina Marinova, Jagdip Singh, Journal of Marketing Research, DOI 10.1177/00222437261481444, created/published online 21 August 2026; funders listed: OFR Symposium Young Scholar Award, ISBM Dissertation Competition Award, AMA Sales SIG Dissertation Competition Award; acceptance date not in the record — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444); [Sage landing page (blocked)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222437261481444)
- Access: not open access, no OA location, no PDF URL; 0 citations and no TL;DR in Semantic Scholar; keywords indexed: paralinguistic cues, speech rate, pitch, symbolic interactionism, sales conversations — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/00222437261481444); [Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/00222437261481444?fields=title,abstract,authors,year,venue,externalIds,citationCount,tldr,openAccessPdf,publicationDate)
- Abstract, sentence by sentence (paraphrased closely; full text in the Crossref record): lead calling is described as a vital part of B2B prospecting whose link to salesperson speech is understudied. Drawing on Symbolic Interactionism and a grounded analysis, the authors build a framework in which salesperson speech consists of linguistic acts — legitimizing, piquing, nudging — and paralinguistic acts — intensity, speech rate, pitch. They theorise that these communicative acts influence lead receptivity, defined as "a speaking turn-level indicator of a lead's openness to the sales message", and that receptivity shapes conversation-level conversion. The test uses 783 lead-calling conversations comprising 11,566 speaking turns. A one-standard-deviation increase in lead receptivity raises the likelihood of conversion by 13.44%. The effectiveness of the acts depends on whether and when they are deployed: more specific (versus less specific) nudges raise receptivity by 11.82% earlier in the conversation but reduce it by 81.40% later; speaking faster (versus slower) has a neutral effect earlier and an 8.26% reduction later. The authors present this as new insight into the conversion process and actionable guidance for lead calling — [Crossref record (abstract field)](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444); [OpenAlex record (abstract_inverted_index)](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/00222437261481444)
- Provenance: the paper is listed among JMR's accepted EXPRESS manuscripts — [JMR accepted manuscripts](https://journals.sagepub.com/toc/MRJ/0/ja). Balducci's 2019 Missouri CV (now returning 404; content from search snippets only) listed dissertation Essay 2 as "The Role of Voice in Salesperson Lead Calling Effectiveness" with Marinova and Singh, being prepared for JMR submission in November 2019; her LinkedIn announcement (search snippet) says the JMR paper comes from her dissertation — [search result pointing to the 2019 vita](https://business.missouri.edu/sites/default/files/profile-cv/Bitty_Balducci_Vita_1.6.2019.pdf)
- Jagdip Singh's October 2022 CV lists: Balducci, Marinova, Singh (2020), "Organizational Agent Voice in B2B Customer Interactions", 6th Annual Symposium of Organizational Frontlines, 13 Feb 2020; and under 2020 awards: AMA Sales SIG Dissertation Proposal Competition Winner (Bitty Balducci) and OFR Young Scholar Research Award Winner ("Voice in Marketing Interactions") — [Singh CV PDF](https://case.edu/weatherhead/sites/default/files/2022-10/Jagdip%20Singh%20CV.pdf)
- Balducci is an assistant professor of marketing at Washington State University's Carson College; the college describes her research as examining how sales interactions unfold using text mining, NLP and acoustic analysis of recorded conversations — [WSU Center for Professional Sales](https://business.wsu.edu/research-faculty/centers/professional-sales/about-us/)
- Related but *different* study by Balducci and Marinova (search summary only, not verified against the paper): an audio pipeline ("AudioMatic") applied to 3,900 sales prospecting calls, where a logistic model on the salesperson's verbal and vocal features predicted call outcomes with 88.08% accuracy, vocal features accounting for 39.2% of predictive value — [Marketing Letters article page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-025-09797-z)
- The same senior authors' earlier turn-level work on recorded frontline interactions (Marinova, Singh & Singh 2018, JMR; Singh, Marinova, Singh & Evans 2017/2018, JAMS "Customer Query Handling in Sales Interactions: A Dynamic Analysis of Salesperson Effectiveness") exists on Singh's research page; I did not read them in this session and cite them only as the methodological lineage of "dynamic, turn-by-turn" analysis — [Singh research page](http://faculty.weatherhead.case.edu/jxs16/section2.html)

### Inferences
- "Nudging" in this taxonomy is the act closest to Yuna's steer/offer; "piquing" (arousing curiosity/interest) is closest to tease; "legitimizing" (establishing why the call/caller deserves attention) is closest to the rapport/credibility opening. These mappings are my reading of the words; the paper's definitions are not available.
- The headline timing result runs *against* a naive "be concrete when closing" intuition: in these calls, concrete nudges helped early and hurt badly late. Whether "late" means after the lead has already shown receptivity or after resistance cannot be told from the abstract, so the direction of the lesson for a chat is uncertain.
- Receptivity is modelled as a mediator (acts → receptivity → conversion). That is the same architecture as Yuna's meters-then-move design, with the difference that receptivity is read from the *lead's* turn, not inferred from counts.
- The magnitude of −81.40% almost certainly refers to a relative change in a modelled quantity (odds or a scaled receptivity index), not that receptivity falls to a fifth; it should not be quoted as an effect size without the model.

### Gaps
- Full text inaccessible; no coding manual for legitimizing/piquing/nudging or for "specificity" of a nudge; no statement of how receptivity was measured per turn (human coding, lexicon, acoustic, or model) or validated.
- The early/late boundary (fixed turn number, proportion of the call, or an event such as the first nudge) is unknown.
- "Conversion" in lead calling is normally a booked meeting or qualified next step, not a purchase; the abstract does not say which.
- No information on the firm, industry, salespeople, call length, or controls; no result for legitimizing, piquing, intensity or pitch beyond their inclusion in the framework.
- No author-side copy (SSRN, OSF, personal or departmental page), no slides, no press release and no citing papers were found as of 22 Oct 2026; the WSU profile page and the podcast page surfaced by search returned 404.

## Key question 3: What turn-level readiness signals could Yuna's reader produce, separate from engagement?

### Takeaway
Both papers locate the signal in the *other party's* turn, not in a count: Hentona's labels are the user's own report of what each seller line did to three distinct wishes, and Balducci's receptivity is read per speaking turn from the lead. The Hentona data show which of the fan's behaviours should count toward "wants what she sells" versus "wants to keep talking", and that a stated decision is still not a purchase.

### Cited Findings
- Hentona's three wishes are defined as separable constructs (keep talking; disclose needs and desires; accept the seller's goal), and the paper notes that users can strongly want to talk without intending to buy — [PDF, §1, §3.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Given a seller turn that raised the wish to talk, the wish to buy rose on 65.8% and stayed flat on 33.5%; given a seller turn that left the wish to talk flat, the wish to buy still rose on 16.3% [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Buyers wrote fewer turns and asked fewer questions than non-buyers; buyers' goal-acceptance first turned positive later (median 0.24 of the dialogue vs 0.08) and was higher in the last three seller turns (56% vs 42% positive) [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Final user messages of buyers typically named the product and the act ("I'll go with the Air-Buds", "I'd like to buy the recommended earphones"), asked a last practical question (volume control, warranty instalments, built-in mic) or thanked; 15 non-buyers also wrote a decision sentence and did not click [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Non-buyers' final messages often contained hold wording ("let me think about it", "I'll consider", "I'll go try them when they're released", "I'll buy on Amazon"); 13 of 62 vs 1 of 47 buyers [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Balducci et al. define receptivity at the level of the lead's speaking turn and show it predicts conversion (+13.44% per SD) — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444)

### Inferences
- A reader for Yuna could produce three per-turn reads from his message, each as positive / no change / negative, mirroring the annotation: (a) *keeps talking* — reply length, a question back, a new topic, emoji/laughs (this is the existing engagement meter); (b) *tells her things* — he answers her question with specifics, volunteers preferences, where he is, what he likes, what he wants to see (this is "disclosure", currently folded into engagement/rapport); (c) *wants what she sells* — he asks what she has, asks the price, asks when, says he wants to see it, names a specific thing, reacts to a tease with "show me" (this should be the only direct input to the offer decision).
- The decisive late-stage signal in the data is not "he is still here and chatty" but "the last few of her lines moved his wish to buy and none of them moved it down". Yuna's reader could keep a short window (last 2–3 of her messages) of his GA reads and gate the offer on that window rather than on his message count.
- "Said yes" and "paid" must stay separate outcomes in the practice room (the data show a quarter of non-buyers verbally deciding); a decision sentence should raise readiness but not close the loop.

### Gaps
- Neither paper labels the *user's* turns; Hentona labels the seller's lines by their effect, so there is no validated lexicon of "ready" user phrasings — my reading of final messages is descriptive, Japanese, and from a one-off purchase scenario.
- How Balducci et al. operationalised receptivity per turn is unknown, so no coding rule can be copied from them.

## Key question 4: What does the research say about asking before offering, and about backing off after a decline?

### Takeaway
Neither paper tests a "readiness check before the offer", and neither studies declines. Hentona's analysis supports *questions about the person before any recommendation* (questions raise disclosure and almost never hurt; recommendations and money questions carry the negatives) and *avoiding any willingness-lowering line* as the main lever, which is the closest research support for "do not keep pushing after a no". Balducci's abstract adds only that the same act can flip sign depending on when it is used.

### Cited Findings
- Hentona's recommended stage strategy is: comfort first, probing questions on requirements in the middle, and a closing recommendation built on what was learned — i.e. elicitation before the pitch — [PDF, §5.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Seller questions had 1–4% negatives and 55–61% positives on CD/PI; budget questions had 9–14% negatives; recommendations had 6–11% negatives (GA 11%); drawback statements 14–27% negatives; soft "how about…?" checks 0% negatives (n=21) [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Recommendations made in the last third of a dialogue were rated GA-positive 67% of the time versus 44% in the first third [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- The paper's central lesson is that avoiding willingness-degrading utterances matters more than producing willingness-raising ones; a GA-negative line in the final third coincided with a purchase rate of 30% vs 49% [paper claim + own computation] — [PDF, §5.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf); [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Exploratory and weak: among 122 user messages containing hesitation or objection words (a keyword proxy that also catches "I'm considering model X"), the seller's next line averaged a summed score of +1.0 on the three dimensions; acknowledging replies (n=47) averaged +1.21 with those dialogues buying 34% of the time, informative replies (n=51) +0.57 / 47%, and a renewed recommendation or push (n=12) +2.17 / 67%. The proxy cannot separate true declines from positive deliberation, so no conclusion about "pushing after a no" should be drawn from it [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Balducci et al.: the effectiveness of communicative acts depends on whether and when they are deployed; specific nudges +11.82% early, −81.40% late — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444)

### Inferences
- The agency's own finding that a readiness check before an offer sold twice as well is consistent with Hentona's structure (a question-shaped line almost never lowers willingness, a recommendation sometimes does), but the papers do not test the check itself; the support is indirect.
- The cleanest research-backed rule for after a decline is negative: do not add a willingness-lowering line (a second push, a drawback-laden justification, a price defence). What *to* do instead (acknowledge, return to his topic, leave a door) is not tested by either paper.
- Balducci's early/late reversal suggests that a concrete push that works as an opener can backfire as a closer; for Yuna the analogous caution is that concreteness about price or content late in a conversation, after he has already shown receptivity or resistance, is where the risk sits — but this is a transfer across very different settings.

### Gaps
- No study here measures what happens after an explicit "no", nor the optimal wait before the next attempt; the "pause after a big spend" and "about one planned attempt a week" rules have no counterpart in either paper.
- The Hentona setting ends at the first purchase, so there is no data on repeat offers or on post-purchase talk.

## Key question 5: Which parts are specific to B2B calls or earphone sales and do not transfer?

### Takeaway
Hentona is a single-session, Japanese, text-chat, fictional-purchase setting with five trained sellers selling a technical product to strangers; Balducci is voice calls between companies whose conversion is almost certainly a next step rather than a payment, with half the framework being vocal features that do not exist in text. The transferable parts are the *measurement ideas* (three separate wishes rated per line; receptivity read per turn; stage-dependent effects), not the numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Hentona: fictional product, no transaction occurred even when the user "bought"; one category (earphones) chosen partly because technical products make users more susceptible to persuasive talk; Japanese language; authors state generalisation to other categories, prices, user types and languages is uncertain — [PDF, §4.1 fn 2, §4.2, Limitations](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Hentona: 30-minute cap, dialogues averaged about 18 minutes and ended at purchase or departure; the seller was told the rating criteria in advance and coached to maximise them — [data file system message](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json); [PDF, §4.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf); [JSAI 2024 (18.7 min mean)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/pjsai/JSAI2024/0/JSAI2024_3Xin240/_article/-char/ja/)
- Hentona: only 5 sellers produced all 2,145 seller lines; users had moderate prior intent (none at the floor) [own computation; paper Appendix A] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json); [PDF, Appendix A](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Balducci: B2B lead-calling conversations (voice); three of six act types are paralinguistic (intensity, speech rate, pitch); the study's indexed keywords are paralinguistic cues, speech rate, pitch — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444); [OpenAlex keywords](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/00222437261481444)

### Inferences
- Does not transfer from Hentona: the absolute label rates (a coached expert seller to a stranger with moderate intent), the "turn 7" schedule, the success definition (intention up, not money), the 18-minute single-session arc (Yuna's fans return over weeks; the "early/middle/late" stages are better read per conversation than per relationship), and the product-information content of the negatives (specs, instalments, comparisons).
- Does not transfer from Balducci: all voice findings (speech rate, pitch, intensity); the specific percentages; "conversion" as a next-step agreement; a cold first contact with a business buyer versus a subscriber who already pays and already likes her.
- Transfers from both: rate her lines by their effect on him, keep three wishes apart, treat negatives as the alarm, and expect the same move to have a different effect early versus late in a conversation.

### Gaps
- Without Balducci's full text, it is unknown whether their "early vs late" refers to clock time, turn index, or an event, so even the shape of the stage dependence is uncertain.

## What Yuna could use (proposals, not findings)

### Takeaway
The research supports changing *what the engine reads* (three separate per-turn effects of her last line on him, with negatives as the brake) and *what gates the offer* (his goal-acceptance in the last few turns, not his message number), and it supports keeping the soft check in front of the offer. Every item below is a proposal; none is tested in these papers in Yuna's setting.

### Cited Findings
- Hentona's three-dimension, per-utterance, positive/no-effect/negative instrument and the pre/post 7-point intent question are the full annotation scheme (no further guideline document exists in the repository) — [GitHub README](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset); [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- The stage-scheduled model (talk → elicit → close) beat the "always positive" model 0.44 vs 0.33 and the label-free model 0.23, and the authors' stated next step is to switch the target wish dynamically from the user's responses — [PDF, Table 3, §8](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Negatives, not positives, predict failure (r ≈ −0.4 to −0.5 vs +0.1 to +0.2); a late GA-negative halves the purchase rate (30% vs 49%); buyers' dialogues carried half the negatives of non-buyers' [paper claim + own computation] — [PDF, §5.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf); [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Questions about the person: 1–3% negatives; budget questions: 14% negatives; recommendations: 11% GA-negatives; drawbacks: 27% GA-negatives; soft checks: 0% [own computation] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Balducci: receptivity per turn mediates conversion (+13.44% per SD); the same act flips sign early vs late — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444)

### Inferences (each marked as a proposal)
- Proposal A — split the read. Keep `engagement` as "he keeps talking" (CD). Add a separate "he tells her things" read (PI: answers with specifics, volunteers wants, says where he is / what he's doing) and make "he wants what she sells" (GA: asks what she has, asks price or when, says show me, names a thing, reacts to a tease with a request) the only read that feeds `readiness` directly. Today `readiness` in `gauges.js` moves on his asks, buys, mood and session flags but not on a per-turn GA read; the two extra reads would unpick the engagement/disclosure/readiness conflation named in the engine notes.
- Proposal B — grade her own last line. After each of his replies, record what her previous message did to each of the three wishes (positive / no change / negative), as Hentona's users did for the seller. Keep a rolling window of the last three. This is the per-turn "receptivity" Balducci describe, read from his turn.
- Proposal C — negatives are the brake. If any of her last two lines reads negative on GA, or two of the last three read negative on anything, no steer or offer in this conversation; switch to repair (a curious question about him, comfort, a share that connects). Rationale: the only robust statistical signal in Hentona is the harm of negatives; the data show a late GA-negative cutting purchases from 49% to 30%.
- Proposal D — gate the offer on state, not count. Replace the message-number parts of the transition (the staged first-sale path's step counts and the "budget used ≥ 60%" trigger for the planned check in `plan.js`) with: offer allowed when the last 2–3 reads show GA positive at least twice and no negative; otherwise keep to CD/PI moves. Keep the hard rules (no selling to a sad or doubting man; pause after a big spend; one planned attempt a week) on top. Rationale: successful Hentona dialogues show GA rising late; the later product shares converted more (59% vs 30%, small n); the authors' own future work is exactly this switch.
- Proposal E — the soft check is the GA probe. Keep `readiness_check` ("want to see?", "are you alone?") in front of the offer, and read his answer as the GA label: a yes / when / how much → offer now; a deflection → one more get-to-know or share turn, then re-check next conversation; a no / not now / broke → GA negative, selling closed for this conversation (as rule B5 already does). Rationale: question-shaped lines almost never lowered willingness in Hentona (soft checks 0/21 negative), recommendations did (11%).
- Proposal F — early questions about him, never about money. Yuna's early curious/get-to-know questions should be about his life, mood and tastes (use-situation questions: 1% negative) and never about budget or "what would you pay" (14% negative); price should appear only inside the offer, once. This matches the engine's "few questions" rule and adds "no money questions before GA positive".
- Proposal G — no bare acknowledgements, no unprompted drawbacks. Bare "thanks"/"ok"-type lines were inert in Hentona (20–36% positive vs 42–54% baseline) and drawbacks were the most harmful class (27% GA-negative). Yuna's react move should always carry one connecting detail or door (already a rule), and disclaimers about what she does not do should be answered only when asked and then paired with what she does do.
- Proposal H — concreteness by stage (weak, from Balducci). Test in the practice room whether a concrete early door ("I'll show you something later if you're good") outperforms a vague one, and whether late concreteness (price repeated, a hard deadline) hurts after he has shown receptivity. Do not import the percentages; this is a hypothesis generated by a B2B voice study.
- Proposal I — measure before trusting. Log the three reads per turn alongside the practice-room outcomes (bought / didn't / ignored / wrote on) for a few hundred conversations and check that the late GA window predicts "bought" better than message count does. Balducci's design (receptivity as a mediator validated against conversion) is the template; the agency's 35k-chat finding on the check is the local prior.
- Proposal J — copy the annotation protocol for English practice chats. (1) Rate every Yuna message, not the chat; (2) three separate questions, each "did this line change, even a little, your wish to [keep talking / tell her things / buy]?" with answers positive / no effect / negative; (3) a 7-point "do you want to buy something from her right now?" before and after; (4) success = after > before, recorded separately from the click/payment; (5) the rater is the person playing the fan, not a third party; (6) flag any Yuna line whose intent two readers find unclear (Hentona's Table 5 signal); (7) add a seller-side tag of intended wish per line (the JSAI 2024 precursor collected this) so intent and effect can be compared.

### Gaps
- None of the proposals have an effect size in Yuna's setting; the Hentona percentages are from a different product, language and relationship length and should be used only as directions.
- The engine's "readiness drifts to a tier base" idea has no counterpart in either paper (both are single-conversation studies).

## What not to infer

### Takeaway
Neither paper shows that a readiness check causes sales, that engagement predicts purchase, that a specific nudge is good or bad for Yuna, or anything about declines, repeat offers, or intimate/creator selling; the strongest transferable claim is that willingness-lowering lines predict failure and that the wish to buy is a separate, late-building read from the wish to talk.

### Cited Findings
- Hentona's success measure is a self-reported intention change on a fictional product; the authors state it is uncertain how far genuine intentions are mirrored — [PDF, Limitations](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Hentona's model comparison has 48 users and no significance test; untuned GPT-4o beat all fine-tuned models (0.58) in fewer turns (5.81) — [PDF, Table 3](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)
- Hentona's positive-label shares barely correlate with intention gain (r ≈ 0.1–0.2) [own computation of the paper's Figure 3 quantities] — [data file](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)
- Balducci's results are known only from the abstract; the acts' definitions, the receptivity measure, the stage cut and the conversion definition are unavailable — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444)

### Inferences
- Do not infer that "more positive lines" sell: in Hentona, positives did not predict success; only the absence of negatives did. A scorer that rewards every warm line equally will over-rate chatty, non-buying fans.
- Do not infer that engagement or disclosure predicts buying: buyers talked less and asked less than non-buyers in the data; the late GA read is what differed.
- Do not infer that "pitch at turn 7" is a finding: it was a stand-in schedule the authors want to replace with reading the user.
- Do not infer that a stated "yes" closes the sale: a quarter of non-buyers said they had decided.
- Do not infer the sign of "specific nudges" for Yuna from Balducci: their nudges were in B2B voice calls with an unknown definition of specificity and stage; the lesson is "timing changes the sign", not which sign.
- Do not use any of Balducci's percentages (13.44%, 11.82%, −81.40%, −8.26%) as effect sizes outside their model; they are relative changes in an unspecified quantity.
- Do not read my keyword classes as causal: they are descriptive, confounded with dialogue stage, user intent and which of five sellers wrote the line, and based on Japanese surface words.
- Do not infer anything about after-decline behaviour, repeat contact cadence, post-purchase pauses, or creator/intimate contexts from either paper; neither studied them.
- Do not treat the Hentona system's label leak (a seller line reading "the willingness to continue dialogue is increasing") as a curiosity only: it is a warning that training a generator on attribute labels can surface the labels in the chat.

### Gaps
- No English-language replication of the Hentona instrument exists in the literature found here; no creator-economy or subscription-chat study uses either paper's measures.
- The Balducci full text may become available via a library or the authors; until then every claim about its coding and stage results is unverifiable.
