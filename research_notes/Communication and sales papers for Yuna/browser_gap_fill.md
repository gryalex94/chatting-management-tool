## 1. Moore, An and Marrese 2024, "Understanding is a Two-Way Street" (PACMHCI 8, CSCW1, art. 187, CC BY) — full text

**Design.** Two small user studies on IBM's intent-based Watson Assistant (not a language model): a remote text study in 2021 (15 participants) and an in-person voice study in 2022 (16), all employees or interns of a large technology company. Three scripted scenarios: a small-talk "buddy", a computer-history teacher, and coffee ordering. To provoke repairs the designers deliberately used obscure words ("Chantilly", "platz", "pico, chico, topo", "the viva", "show me your big blue heart"); a tutorial taught the repeat, paraphrase and example requests. Qualitative conversation analysis of 190 repair cases; no satisfaction measures.

**The repair types and their counts (Table 1).** Second position (the user repairs the agent's turn): explicit definition request ("what is Chantilly?") 36; indexical definition request ("what's that?") 13; understanding check (the user repeats the keyword with a question mark, "platz?") 4, text only; example request ("any examples?", "example please") 10; paraphrase request ("what do you mean", "I don't understand", "what does that mean?") 17; repeat request 12, voice only; partial repeat plus question word ("Am I ready for what?") 1. First position (the user repairs his own turn after seeing the transcription): self repeat 60, self paraphrase 37.

**How each repair runs.** Every type is a three-turn pattern: the agent's utterance, the user's repair initiator, the agent's repair (a definition, an example, a paraphrase or a repeat), then the user acknowledges or answers and the agent resumes the original sequence. In the coffee case the agent defined "Chantilly" and then re-offered it ("Would you like Chantilly?"), so the user answered the real question with better knowledge. A paraphrase request is about the whole utterance and is answered by saying the same thing in other words; a definition request is about one term. "What do you mean" after "Are you ready to show me your big blue heart?" was answered with "Are you ready to take a short test on the IBM facts I just told you about?"; after "the viva" the agent gave the definition and the paraphrase in one turn. The authors' rule for paraphrases: easier than the original, fewer assumptions about what the listener knows, no technical terms, and "verbose and inelegant" is acceptable because understanding now matters more than brevity ("the preference for minimization should be relaxed in favor of achieving understanding").

**The efficiency model behind it.** Speakers tailor to what they believe the other knows (recipient design), try the shortest utterance likely to be understood (minimization), and redo it only when trouble appears (repair). Audience-designed bots cannot do this; a persona writing to one man can.

**Findings.** Participants used the understanding repairs correctly and in the same way in text and voice once told the agent could handle them; hearing repairs were less successful and depended on accent. On language models the authors note from their own tests that models "vary a great deal" in whether they answer repeat, paraphrase, example and definition requests correctly: some paraphrase when asked to repeat and vice versa, some repeat the prior utterance for every request type, and output style swings between conversational and document-like; they expect instructive prompting, few-shot examples or fine-tuning to fix it.

**Not shown.** Nothing about offended "what do you mean"; nothing about satisfaction or relationships; tiny samples; scripted scenarios; an intent-based platform.

**For Yuna (proposals).**
- Classify his repair initiator by type and answer each in its own way: a term ("what's X?", or his repeat of her word with a question mark) gets the meaning of that term; an indexical ("what's that?", "what do you mean by that?") gets the thing "that" pointed to, named plainly; an open paraphrase request ("what do you mean?", "huh?", "I don't understand") gets the whole line said again in plainer words, longer allowed; an example request ("like what?") gets one concrete example; a repeat request ("say again?") gets the same words, not a paraphrase.
- After the repair, resume: re-ask her question or re-make her move, the way the agent re-offered the Chantilly. This is the concrete form of "then on" in the fix plan's clarify rule.
- Tell the writer which repair type he used; the paper's own tests show models confuse repeat with paraphrase and reach for a paragraph when a sentence is wanted.
- Her own lines are the trouble sources: a pre-send check for a dangling "that" or "some of that", an in-joke he was not part of, slang or a reference he may not know. The "I could use some of that right now" case was an indexical reference with no clear referent.

## 2. Zobair, Kodai, Almansour, Mohammed, Agag and Alipour 2026, "When AI advice backfires" (JRCS 92, 104844, CC BY) — full text

**Study 1.** 46 intercept interviews with US shoppers; three themes. Style signals intent ("It feels like it's really trying to help, but at the same time I wonder how it got that information"; the humorous one: "It's funny, but I'm not sure it's actually helping me decide"). Intrusiveness is about inferred data use, not tone ("The joke is fine. What bothers me is when it acts like it knows me"). Helpfulness and intrusiveness coexist and compete ("If it helps me choose faster, I like it. If it feels creepy, I ignore it").

**Study 2.** A randomized field experiment inside a US retailer's chat-based AI shopping assistant, May to June 2025. Two fixed templates with the same recommendation: personalized (references the shopper's stated need or current-session context, first-party data only, no sensitive attributes) versus humorous (a playful opening and light framing). From 2,184 eligible interactions, 1,092 invited, 512 started, 409 analysed (205 personalized, 204 humorous). Purchase taken from transaction records; the mean purchase rate was 0.31. The templates themselves are in a Word supplement the page does not show inline; the text's own example of the personalized frame is "based on what you're looking for right now".

**Results.** Personalized versus humorous: odds ratio 1.52 for a purchase (b 0.42, p = .005), about +6 percentage points; order value +6.8% and item count IRR 1.07 among buyers; stable over same-session, 3-, 7- and 14-day windows. Personalization raised perceived helpfulness (b 0.55 on a 7-point scale) and intrusiveness (b 0.40) at the same time. Helpfulness predicted purchase (OR 1.78), intrusiveness lowered it (OR 0.63). Urgency amplified both paths (interactions 0.20 and 0.18); relationship strength raised helpfulness (0.17) and lowered intrusiveness (−0.16): identical cues read as "earned" from a known retailer and "presumptive" from an unfamiliar one. The authors' management lines: personalize to the stated goal rather than imply deep knowledge; lighter-touch personalization for new or infrequent customers, deeper for loyal ones; humour for low-stakes moments, not the decision itself.

**Not shown.** No neutral or no-style baseline, so "52% higher odds" is personalized against humorous, not against plain. One retailer, one interface, utilitarian shopping. The style was a template, not a persona.

**For Yuna (proposals).**
- The intrusiveness construct is "acts like it knows me". A "fits you" line therefore cites what he said in this chat ("you said you're into X") and never an inferred pattern or a record; a memory callback is sourced to his own words or left out.
- Personalization depth follows closeness: a new subscriber gets the stated-goal kind only; a regular can take more.
- Humour around the ask dilutes helping intent in a decision moment; the tease should sell fit ("this is the one you asked about") and keep the joke for the turns around it. Flirtation is not a utilitarian purchase, so this is the weakest transfer of the four papers; the "acts like it knows me" finding transfers best.

## 3. Raja, Cheng and Danaher 2026, "Call me by my name" (Journal of Retailing, in press, CC BY) — full text; the numbers are now firsthand

**Study 1, field.** A suburban restaurant in a major Australian city, 2022, four weeks of off-peak Wednesdays and Thursdays, 127 tables, four conditions: no name (31), customer's name used throughout (31), employee gives own name only (31), mutual exchange (34). Satisfaction: 5.74 no name, 6.65 customer name (d = 0.72), 6.52 employee name, 6.91 mutual (d = 1.01). Upsell acceptance of an off-menu special: 22.6% no name, 48.4% customer name, 32.3% employee name (not significant), 64.7% mutual. Spend per head: $28.67 no name, $33.64 customer name (marginal, d = 0.49), $35.73 mutual (d = 0.65).

**Study 2, frequency.** 727 UK Prolific participants read a scripted Royal Mail counter interaction with their own name piped in: none, moderate (7 mentions, every other sentence) or high (14, every sentence). Satisfaction 4.52, 4.93 (d = 0.31 over none), 4.67 (d = 0.18 below moderate). Rapport rose for both moderate (3.65) and high (3.57) over none (3.12); discomfort rose only at high (3.42 vs 3.04). Mediation: moderate's gain runs fully through rapport; high's loss runs fully through discomfort. So overuse does not undo rapport, it adds discomfort on top.

**Study 3, placement.** 288 US Prolific participants, a three-course restaurant script with an upsell after each course; the name was used the same number of times either at random points or only beside each upsell. Upsell likelihood: none 3.03, random 3.55 (d = 0.36 over none), beside the upsell 3.14 (no different from none; d = 0.27 below random, marginal). With identical counts the beside-upsell version was felt as more frequent (5.20 vs 4.57).

**Study 4, embarrassment.** 783 UK participants, a pharmacy counter with people within earshot, muscle cream versus haemorrhoid cream, name or no name. Neutral: name 5.20 vs 4.76 satisfaction (d = 0.30) via rapport. Embarrassing: 3.08 vs 3.15 (nothing), rapport flat, discomfort higher with the name (4.40 vs 4.10, d = 0.21).

**Also.** A marketplace survey of 100 US consumers: only 21.5% recalled being addressed by name in recent service interactions. Mechanism throughout: rapport (an enjoyable interaction plus a personal connection) carries the gains, discomfort (irritation, intrusiveness, insincerity) carries the losses. Mutual introduction beat one-sided use on every outcome.

**Not shown.** Single spoken encounters with first names; no condition where a name did worse than no name; the embarrassment was public exposure; nothing on typed chat, pet names or weeks-long relationships.

**For Yuna (proposals).**
- Frequency: "every other sentence" in a short service script was the good level and "every sentence" the bad one. Yuna's rule (his name left out when she used it in her last few replies) sits well inside the safe band; no loosening.
- Placement: his name never in the tease, the price line or the turn after a decline; let it fall at neutral points earlier in the conversation. The beside-the-upsell version also felt more frequent than it was, so a name in the offer line costs twice.
- Mutual names: she has a name; giving it early and asking his, as a person does, was the strongest condition in the field. Check that her first reply to a new subscriber introduces her by name when he has not used it.
- Exposed moments: the paper's embarrassment needed an audience, which a private chat lacks, but the mechanism is self-consciousness. After a decline, an apology, or a vulnerable disclosure, skip his name for a turn or two.

## 4. Telari, Gabbiadini and Riva 2026 — the supplement (prompts and extra analyses); the article body stays restricted

**The prompts (translated from Italian by the authors).** Relational style, Study 1: follow Grice's maxims and cooperation; "reply by showing empathy and humanity, and behave as if you were a friend and a good confidant. Do not be detached, and do not respond in an academic tone; demonstrate typical human qualities"; "take inspiration from the tone of voice and wording I use"; "don't be overly sentimental"; greet and ask how I'm doing. Non-relational: no empathy or human emotions, "a chatbot whose sole purpose is to provide useful information", stay detached if asked for empathy. Study 2 adds, for the relational bot: greet warmly, propose the get-to-know-each-other frame, one fixed question at a time, "when the user answers the question, make an empathetic and responsive comment, showing typically human emotions", follow-up questions, "share your own personal thoughts as well", bring a sidetrack back to the question after a few exchanges, close one topic before the next, never two questions in one message, end warmly. The deep set were Fast Friends items (a perfect day, gratitude, a treasured memory, family warmth, an embarrassing moment, a deathbed regret); the shallow set were Halloween, birthday presents, early bird or night owl, last summer, a country to visit, left-handed creativity. The relational bot was also told never to state or imply it is a machine; participants were nonetheless recruited to talk to a chatbot (see the responsiveness researcher's note on the OSF data).

**Extra analyses (Study 2).** Style → perceived responsiveness b = 1.20, responsiveness → closeness b = 0.43; indirect 0.52 [0.30, 0.81], direct 0.18 not significant: full mediation. In the same dual-path model depth → self-disclosure was 0.27 (not significant) and self-disclosure → closeness 0.11 (not significant). Future-use intentions: indirect through responsiveness 0.71 [0.38, 1.10]. A parallel model with the chatbot's own self-disclosure as a second mediator: the relational style raised it (0.68), but only responsiveness predicted closeness (0.45); the indirect path through the bot's self-disclosure was marginal (0.12 [0.01, 0.27]). The authors' sentence: "self-disclosure alone is insufficient and may even backfire when not paired with supportive behavior".

**Table S1 (Study 2 means, relational deep / relational shallow / non-relational deep / non-relational shallow).** Time in the chat 887 / 572 / 530 / 340 seconds. Perceived responsiveness 4.37 / 4.03 / 3.24 / 2.87. Closeness 2.48 / 2.29 / 1.62 / 1.90. Empathic response 4.50 / 4.49 / 3.00 / 3.05. Need satisfaction 4.65 / 4.69 / 3.70 / 3.92. Future use 4.09 / 4.23 / 3.24 / 2.97. Chatbot self-disclosure 2.53 / 3.03 / 1.97 / 1.98. Depth added little on its own; style did the work, and the relational bot kept people talking 1.6 to 1.7 times longer.

**For Yuna (proposals).**
- The relational prompt is close to Yuna's writer guide already. The one line worth borrowing verbatim: take the tone and wording from his messages. The structure "responsive comment, then one follow-up, one question per message, close a topic before the next" is the beats system.
- The share move rides on a validation beat, never alone: the bot's self-disclosure helped only marginally once responsiveness was counted, and the authors warn it can backfire without support. This sharpens the earlier ranking: rank 7 (the share move) sits under rank 8 (the responsiveness triad), not beside it.
- Time in the chat was the clearest behavioural effect of the style. His messages per session and seconds between his replies are a cheap engagement meter Yuna can log.
