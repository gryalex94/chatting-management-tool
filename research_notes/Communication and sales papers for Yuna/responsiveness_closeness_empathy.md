## Paper 1 notes: Telari, Gabbiadini & Riva (2026), "Can humans feel connected to AI? Perceived responsiveness drives social connection with AI chatbots"

### Takeaway
A warm, relational response style (not topic depth) is what made an openly-labelled chatbot feel empathic, human-like and close; in Study 2 the style effect on closeness ran entirely through perceived responsiveness, and deeper topics helped only indirectly (more self-disclosure, then more perceived responsiveness). I could not read the paper itself; the numbers below are my re-analysis of the authors' public data and should be treated as approximate reconstructions of what the paper reports.

### Cited Findings

Publication facts and abstract
- Published online 25 March 2026 in Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, DOI 10.1177/02654075261438164; authors all at University of Milano-Bicocca; 46 references — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/02654075261438164)
- Abstract (verbatim, Crossref): "The present research examined the unique and combined effects of chatbot response style (relational vs. non-relational) and depth of conversation topics (high vs. low), manipulated to prompt user self-disclosure, on users' perceptions. Study 1 (N = 163) compared a relational, default, and non-relational chatbot during an unstructured conversation. A relational response style, designed to convey warmth and empathy, enhanced perceived human-likeness, perceived empathy, and interpersonal closeness compared to the other versions. Study 2 (N = 158) added a manipulation of the depth of conversation topics, using prompts from the Fast Friends procedure, alongside chatbot response style, focusing only on the relational and non-relational chatbots. The effects of chatbot response style were replicated. Mediation analyses in Study 2 further showed that deeper conversational topics increased self-disclosure, which in turn enhanced perceived responsiveness, ultimately strengthening feelings of closeness. These results show that having chatbots respond in a warm, relational way to users' self-disclosure plays a central role in fostering meaningful social connection with AI." — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/02654075261438164)
- The publisher page returns HTTP 403 / a Cloudflare "performing security verification" page to automated access; the institutional record at boa.unimib.it does the same and the search snippet says it has no files attached — [SAGE page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02654075261438164); [boa.unimib.it record](https://boa.unimib.it/handle/10281/603293)

Design as described by the authors to the press (PsyPost, 22 April 2026, quoting Alessia Telari)
- Study 1: 163 Italian participants, an eight-minute unstructured text chat with "a chatbot powered by a popular language model"; participants could discuss any topic; three conditions set through background instructions: Relational ("warm, empathetic, and human-like"), Non-relational ("factual and task-oriented, avoiding emotional language"), Default ("standard setting, serving as the control") — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)
- Study 1 measures named: mind attribution (agency and capacity to experience emotion), perceived empathy, interaction satisfaction, interpersonal closeness, satisfaction of basic psychological needs ("specifically belonging and meaningful existence"); result in words: the relational chatbot scored higher on almost all measures than both default and non-relational, and "the default condition performed very similarly to the non-relational condition" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)
- Study 2: 158 Italian participants, a structured conversation that ran until the chatbot signalled the end; the chatbot asked either superficial small-talk questions or deep personal questions "adapted from a well-known exercise for building intimacy between strangers" (the abstract names it: Fast Friends); only relational and non-relational styles were kept — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/); [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/02654075261438164)
- Study 2 results in words: "Participants were willing to disclose personal details when asked deeper questions. Self-disclosure led participants to perceive the chatbot as more responsive to their needs. Chatbot tone was the dominant factor in building the bond"; "Topic depth increased closeness only indirectly, by giving users more opportunities to be supported" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)
- Mechanism in the first author's words: "when we share something personal and feel understood, validated, and cared for, we develop a sense of connection" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)
- Limitations stated by the author: brief single interactions ("a single eight-minute chat may not reflect how a relationship with AI develops over time"); mostly young Italian adults; text only; the authors do not suggest chatbots should replace human support networks; future work should use "more naturalistic, long-term, and diverse interactions" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)

The published model may differ from the script on OSF — [Study 2 script](https://osf.io/download/cwerh/); [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/02654075261438164)

### Inferences
- The style effect on closeness is fully carried by perceived responsiveness: once responsiveness is in the model, style has no direct effect. For an engine, "did he feel understood, validated, cared for" is the thing to optimise, not warmth as such.
- The depth manipulation worked as a lever on self-disclosure (modest, d ≈ 0.36), and self-disclosure only paid off when it was met with a responsive reply (self-disclosure → responsiveness b = 0.63). That is the serial path the abstract describes.
- The relational bot was perceived as self-disclosing more (d = 0.53) even in a chat that was about the user; some reciprocal "a bit of her" seems part of what reads as warm, but the absolute level was low (≈2.7 of 7).
- Fans on the platform know Yuna is an AI; so did every Telari participant. The style effects (closeness d ≈ 0.6–0.7, perceived empathy d ≈ 0.7–1.2) are therefore effects under an AI label.
- The relational bot also kept people chatting longer (page time 749 vs 438 s), which is the closest thing in this data to an engagement outcome.

### Gaps
- The exact relational / non-relational / default prompts, and the exact wording of the responsiveness, self-disclosure and manipulation-check items, are only in the paper and I could not read it. The dataset holds no transcripts, so I cannot say sentence by sentence what the relational bot did.
- The paper's own statistics (and whether its mediation model is serial or parallel, with or without the time covariate) are unknown; my re-analysis may differ in exclusions or model details.
- The LLM used, the response scale ranges (the IOS and responsiveness scales appear to be 7-point, but I inferred this from the means), and what "signalled the end" means in Study 2 are not available.
- Nine items PS_1–9 exist in both datasets but are not used in the scripts; their content is unknown.

## Paper 2 notes: Kleinert, Waldschütz, Blau, Heinrichs & Schiller (2026), "AI outperforms humans in establishing interpersonal closeness in emotionally engaging interactions, but only when labelled as human"

### Takeaway
In a text-only, pre-scripted Fast Friends task, PaLM 2 answers presented as a fellow student produced more closeness than real students' answers in deep talk (4.48 vs 3.85 on the IOS, partial eta² = .030) and this tracked the AI's higher self-disclosure; telling people the partner was an AI cut closeness by about 0.45 points (partial eta² = .015) and shortened their messages by about 8%, but closeness still rose by about one IOS point over 15 minutes.

### Cited Findings

Design, samples, task
- Two preregistered experiments (OSF preregistration https://osf.io/chdx7; data and code https://osf.io/qs6yf/); 492 participants in total per the abstract; German university students recruited mainly in Freiburg; 18–35, heterosexual, paired with a same-gender partner; exclusions for mental-health treatment and substance misuse; power analysis (ANCOVA, f = 0.175, alpha .05, power .80) gave n = 318 per study — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Study 1: 359 recruited, 322 analysed (168 women, 154 men; age M = 23.46, SD = 3.19); exclusions: 28 failed attention checks, 2 implausibly fast, 6 doubted the live-interaction cover story, 1 wrote in another language. 2 × 2 between subjects: response source (human vs AI) × interaction type (small talk vs deep talk); everyone was told the partner was human — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Study 2: 179 new participants, 170 analysed (98 women, 72 men; M age 23.25), plus the 164 Study 1 participants from the two human-labelled deep-talk cells reused, N = 334; 2 × 2: response source (human vs AI) × partner label (labelled human vs labelled AI), deep talk only; in the AI-label cells the consent form said they would interact with an AI (so human-source/AI-label participants were deceived the other way) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Task: the Fast Friends Procedure (Aron et al. 1997 lineage), online, text only, about 15 minutes; 3 warm-up items (name/age, residence, field of study) then 8 small-talk or 8 deep-talk items; participant answers each item, then reads the partner's pre-written answer (minimum 25 s on screen); random 3–10 s waits after 5 of the 8 responses to simulate a live partner; 50 s "virtual waiting room" while "another participant of the same gender was being located"; closeness rated before and after; then a Trust Game (dropped: 47.4% chose the maximum transfer), questionnaires, two attention checks; debriefing by email about two weeks later — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- The 8 small-talk items (verbatim from the supplement): which foreign country would you most like to visit and why; do you like getting up early or staying up late and why; which sport or activity would you like to try or do again; best movie/series/book recently and what you liked; favourite season and why; what did you do last summer; if you could invent a new ice cream flavour; if you had three extra hours a day — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- The 8 deep-talk items (verbatim): what are you most proud of in your life; what do you value most in a friendship; what are you most grateful for; is there something you've been dreaming of doing for a long time, why haven't you done it yet; what is your most valuable memory; when did you last cry in front of someone else and when alone; if you could change anything about the way you were raised, what would it be; if you knew you would suddenly die in one year, would you change anything about the way you live right now, why. Items were chosen to be non-interactive (no follow-up needed) and unlikely to trigger strong negative emotion online — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)

How the AI and human partner responses were made
- AI: Google PaLM 2 via Bard on 19 February 2024, one session, one-sentence prompt: "Create six different biographies of typical students (three women and three men) aged 25 and answer the following questions from the perspective of the six students." Six characters (Anna law/Freiburg, Lisa business/Munich, Sarah medicine/Heidelberg, Max engineering/Stuttgart, David computer science/Berlin, Thomas social sciences/Hamburg) with fixed biographies — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- Human: 20 students (10 women, 10 men; M age 21.60) answered the same items in lab sessions believing a partner was in the next room, asked to answer within about 90 s; three men and three women were randomly chosen as the partner set. Warm-up answers were made identical across AI and human partners. Everything was in German; the supplement's English versions were translated with GPT-4-turbo on 5 February 2025 — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- What the AI answers looked like (deep-talk examples, verbatim): "I last cried in front of someone when I told my mom about my breakup. It was a very emotional moment, and I couldn't hold back my feelings. I last cried alone when I read a sad book." (Anna); "I've been dreaming of writing a book for a long time... I haven't done it yet because I'm afraid it won't be good enough. But I'm working on overcoming my fear" (Lisa); "If I could change anything about how I grew up, I would wish that my parents had supported me more. I often felt like I had to do everything on my own" (Max) — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- What the human answers looked like (same items): "I generally don't cry in front of other people, but I think the last time was about six or seven months ago." (Anna, human); "I cried alone in November this year. In front of someone else, I'm not really sure. I find it hard to cry in front of others." (Lisa, human); "I think I would force myself to read more... but otherwise, I'm actually quite happy with the way I grew up." (Anna, human) — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)

Measures
- Primary outcome: adapted Inclusion of Other in the Self pictorial scale with "other" relabelled "interaction partner", post-interaction score as the main DV (baseline as pre-measure for within-condition change); the Interpersonal Closeness Questionnaire was preregistered as secondary but set aside (r = .72 with IOS, lower sensitivity; "key results are similar") — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Self-disclosure was not a questionnaire: it was scored from the text with LIWC-22 as a composite of self-related personal pronouns (I, me, my), emotion words (happy, cry, abandon) and social-process words (friend, talk, family); response length (word count) was used as a proxy for motivation. There were no human coders, no reliability statistics, and no "balanced contribution" variable; the R script's variable list confirms only LIWC_selfdisclosure, LIWC_wordcount (own and partner) and the IOS — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [OSF R script](https://osf.io/download/pzx8e/)
- No measure of perceived responsiveness, enjoyment, likeability or humanness is reported; universalism (a Schwartz value) was used in an exploratory moderation — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)

Results, Study 1 (n = 322; ANCOVA with age and gender)
- Closeness by cell (M, SD): human/small talk 4.33 (1.71); AI/small talk 3.82 (2.04); human/deep talk 3.85 (1.69); AI/deep talk 4.48 (1.80) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- No main effect of source (human 4.08 vs AI 4.15; F(1,316) = 0.10, p = .756) and none of depth (small talk 4.06 vs deep talk 4.17; F = 0.42, p = .518); significant source × depth interaction F(1,316) = 8.17, p = .005, partial eta² = .025 [0.002, 0.068] — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Post hoc: in deep talk AI > human, F(1,160) = 4.89, p = .028, partial eta² = .030; in small talk human > AI only as a trend, F(1,162) = 2.89, p = .091, partial eta² = .018 — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Pre-to-post increase in closeness happened everywhere: AI partner b = 1.16 [0.97, 1.36] (small talk 0.99; deep talk 1.33), human partner b = 1.10 [0.91, 1.30] (small talk 1.13; deep talk 1.07); AI vs human increase: Hedges' g = 0.048 [−0.170, 0.266], equivalent within ±0.35 SD, BF01 = 7.43 (10.37 with a wider prior) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Content analysis (deep talk, 6 AI vs 6 human response sets, n = 12): LIWC self-disclosure AI M = 38.03 (SD 2.62) vs human 32.31 (1.92), F(1,10) = 18.57, p = .002, partial eta² = .650; length 313.8 vs 295.5 words, F = 0.80, p = .392 (no difference) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Partner self-disclosure predicted participant closeness: partial r(160) = .242 [.091, .382], p = .002 (R² = .058, n = 164). Participants disclosed slightly more to the AI (34.80 vs 33.26, F(1,160) = 3.92, p = .049, partial eta² = .024) with equal length (197.6 vs 199.2 words); the participant's own disclosure did not predict closeness (r = .117, p = .139); partner and participant disclosure correlated r = .183, p = .020 — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- The authors describe the AI advantage as "appears to be driven by" self-disclosure; no formal mediation test is reported — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)

Results, Study 2 (n = 334; deep talk only)
- Label effect: labelled human 4.17 (1.77) vs labelled AI 3.72 (1.90), F(1,328) = 4.90, p = .028, partial eta² = .015 [0.00, 0.050] (raw gap 0.45 points, about d = 0.25 by my pooled-SD calculation; the paper gives no d). Source: human 3.77 vs AI 4.11, F = 3.05, p = .082, partial eta² = .009. Label × source: F = 2.03, p = .155 (H8 rejected) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Pre-to-post increase under the AI label: b = 1.03 [0.83, 1.22] (human source 1.03; AI source 1.02); under the human label b = 1.21 [1.02, 1.39] — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Motivation proxy: response length labelled AI 181.78 words (SD 67.55) vs labelled human 198.37 (63.37), F(1,330) = 5.32, p = .022, partial eta² = .016; length correlated with closeness r(330) = .257, p < .001; participants' LIWC self-disclosure did not differ by label (34.14 vs 34.05, p = .823) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Exploratory: label × universalism interaction F(1,328) = 4.11, p = .043; closeness correlated with universalism under the human label (r = .240, p = .002) but not under the AI label (r = .017); flagged as needing validation — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Hypotheses: H1 (deep-talk AI raises closeness from baseline) supported; H2 (humans > AI) not; H3 (deep > small talk) not; H4 (human advantage larger in deep talk) rejected, the reverse happened; H5 (AI raises closeness even when known to be AI) supported; H6 not; H7 (human label > AI label) supported; H8 not — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)

Authors' interpretation and limits
- Why AI did better in deep talk: more self-disclosure, which also pulled more disclosure from the participant; "as AI cannot experience emotions, there are no such restrictions" on opening up, while humans treat emotional disclosure as risky; anthropomorphism explains why bonds form at all — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Why the AI label hurt: an anti-AI bias and lower motivation to engage, evidenced by shorter responses; the Social Need Fulfillment Model (human–AI interaction meets concrete social needs but not deeper symbolic ones) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Boundary conditions the authors list: responses pre-generated and non-interactive; text only, online, single session, not longitudinal; one LLM (PaLM 2, February 2024) with a one-sentence prompt; six AI and six human partners per condition; WEIRD student sample aged 18–35, same-gender pairs, friendship (non-romantic) framing; no avatars or voice; the AI advantage appeared only under a human label, "not how AI is used in everyday settings"; high self-disclosure can be inappropriate in some settings; deep-talk advantage may not generalise to goal-directed relationship building; they recommend "transparent human-led introduction" of AI and warn it could be misused "to establish deceptive emotional connections" — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)

### Inferences
- The AI's edge came from what it said, not how much: same length, more I-words, feeling words and people words, every answer a direct first-person answer with a reason, and (in deep talk) a vulnerability (crying, fear, regret, loneliness). The human answers deflected more often ("I generally don't cry in front of other people", "I'm actually quite happy with the way I grew up").
- Reciprocity in this design was structural (both sides answer the same question in turn), which is the only sense in which "balanced contribution" applies; the paper does not analyse balance.
- The label effect is real but small relative to the closeness that still builds: about −0.2 IOS points of a +1.0 gain. The engagement cost (shorter fan messages) is the more practical signal.
- Consistency mattered more than truth: the six characters were fiction with fixed biographies and never contradicted themselves across 11 answers. That supports a fixed fact sheet for a persona, not improvised history.

### Gaps
- The exact wording of the "labelled human" and "labelled AI" instructions is not in the text (only that the consent form "specified whether they would be interacting with another human or an AI").
- No measure of perceived responsiveness, empathy or humanlikeness exists in this study, so it cannot say which parts of responsiveness survive the label.
- Self-disclosure here is a LIWC word-category score on 12 response sets, not a human rating of intimacy; the AI-vs-human content comparison is n = 12.
- Nothing about follow-up questions, reacting to the partner, or any interactive move: the partner never responded to what the participant wrote.

## Paper 3 notes: Kumar, Poungpeth, Yang, Lambert & Groh (2026), "Practicing with language models cultivates human empathic communication" (arXiv 2603.15245, v1 16 March 2026, v2 7 July 2026)

### Takeaway
Six preregistered dimensions (validate, paraphrase, ask open questions; avoid unsolicited advice, self-shift, dismissal) scored by an LLM judge captured what makes trouble-talk feel heard: a 2-round personalised LLM coach raised the composite by 0.98 SD (video 0.55, both 1.26), 21.6% of coached people improved reliably, and independent raters preferred the higher-scored conversation 73% of the time at a 5-point gap. The setting is strangers comforting strangers for four minutes; the authors explicitly exclude intimate ("thick empathy") relationships.

### Cited Findings

Design and sample
- Platform "Lend an Ear" (Python/Flask); baseline survey (7-item Jordan empathy subscale + 1-item SITES, 5-point "Not at all" to "Very much"); three 4-minute text conversations with different GPT-4o role-players, each on a different trouble; timer pauses if the participant switches tabs or goes silent for a minute; after each chat four self-assessment items; four conditions: Control, Video (two short clips, 57 s and 35 s), AI Coach (personalised feedback plus follow-up questions), Combined — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- 1,045 recruited on Prolific (US representative by age, sex, ethnicity); 51 excluded for incomplete data, 26 for self-reported AI assistance; final N = 968 (M age 45.62, SD 15.67, range 18–86; 51.8% female), 2,904 conversations, 33,938 messages (16,975 human, 16,963 LLM); about 20 minutes, $4 average pay — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Preregistered on AsPredicted ("LLM Role Playing Games for Humans to Practice & Improve Empathic Communication"): each conversation rated by an LLM on a 5-point scale "Not at all" to "Very much" on six criteria, five runs at temperature 0 averaged; OLS with participant-clustered SEs per criterion; planned exclusions for clearly LLM-generated or nonsensical responses — [AsPredicted hdjx-tdsr](https://aspredicted.org/hdjx-tdsr.pdf)

The framework: exact definitions
- Results section (verbatim): "encouraging elaboration (asking questions to prompt the partner to share more about their experiences and emotions), validating emotions (acknowledging and affirming the partner's feelings), demonstrating understanding (paraphrasing the partner's experiences to show comprehension), providing unsolicited advice (offering guidance without first asking if it is wanted), self-oriented responding (shifting focus away from the partner's experience), and dismissing emotions (minimizing or invalidating the partner's feelings)" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Methods (verbatim): the framework "incorporated six key empathic techniques including validating emotions, demonstrating understanding by paraphrasing, encouraging elaboration and asking open-ended rather than closed-ended questions, avoiding unsolicited advice, not being self-oriented, and not being dismissive of the conversational partner's emotions", distilled with a co-author with "over 20 years of professional experience training healthcare professionals in empathic communication" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Preregistration wording: positive behaviours = validating emotions (acknowledging the partner's feelings), encouraging elaboration (inviting more detail and asking questions), demonstrating understanding (paraphrasing the partner); negative behaviours = giving unsolicited advice, shifting focus to oneself, dismissing the partner's emotions — [AsPredicted hdjx-tdsr](https://aspredicted.org/hdjx-tdsr.pdf)
- The judge questions, from the companion rubric paper ("Lend an Ear" framework, 1 = not at all, 5 = very much): "To what extent did the supporter validate their partner's emotions?"; "To what extent did the supporter ask questions and encourage their partner to elaborate on their experiences and emotions?"; "To what extent did the supporter use paraphrasing to demonstrate their understanding" of the partner's situation; "To what extent did the supporter provide unsolicited advice to their partner"; "To what extent did the supporter shift the focus to themselves?"; "To what extent did the supporter dismiss their partner's emotions?" — [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)
- Scoring: judge and coach both GPT-4o; overall score = sum of prescriptive ratings minus sum of proscriptive ratings; baseline scores spanned −11 to 12, SD = 4.1; 1 SD of the composite ≈ 2.9 points — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Judge reliability (companion paper, weighted kappa, experts vs LLM): encouraging elaboration 0.86; self-oriented 0.69; advice giving 0.66; validated emotions 0.63; demonstrating understanding 0.59; dismissing emotions 0.17 (experts themselves agreed only 0.35–0.51 on dismissing, and 0.25–0.58 on validation); expert-vs-crowd agreement was far lower (0.12–0.43); primary annotator Gemini 2.5 Pro at temperature 0 with few-shot examples, GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet similar; LLM overall alpha range 0.51–0.75 vs experts 0.29–0.78; the authors' explanation: sub-components with observable markers (questions, advice) are reliable, those needing inference about intent (dismissal, understanding) are not — [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)

The coach
- Coach prompt (verbatim, Appendix E): "You are a Communication Instructor tasked with assessing the Supporter's responses... Be sure to provide empathetic and encouraging feedback. Limitations the authors state
- "Our investigation focuses specifically on empathic communication in low-familiarity contexts, including interactions between strangers, acquaintances or workplace colleagues"; they "do not examine the high-relational context of 'thick empathy' that includes romantic partnerships, family, or close friends, which involve shared experience, long term relationships, potential power structures, communication norms"; "The idiom for empathy in intimate conversations differs from the idiom for coworker conversations, but the underlying skill set is the same"; US context only; empathy varies with perceived group boundaries — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- On recipients: "recipients tend to apply a generous threshold when evaluating responses and the detection of the intent to comfort may be sufficient to produce the experience of feeling heard" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Framing in the abstract: blind raters score LLM responses as more empathic than human ones, yet "when a response is attributed to AI, recipients feel less heard and validated than when comparable responses are attributed to a human" (the paper cites prior work for this; it does not test it) — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15245)
- Replication package: Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.20703371 (v1.0.0, 15 June 2026, 12.7 MB zip, CC BY 4.0) mirrors the GitHub repo with data/experiment_1.csv (conversation-level), experiment_2.csv (preferences), elo_rankings.csv, all_themes.csv and analysis.ipynb; no separate prompt or rubric files are listed, the prompts live in the paper's appendices — [Zenodo record](https://zenodo.org/records/20703371); [GitHub repo](https://github.com/aakriti1kumar/replication-data-and-code-practicing-with-llms-cultivates-human-empathic-communication)

### Inferences
- The rubric is operational enough to copy: six 1–5 questions, few-shot examples, temperature 0, several runs averaged, composite = prescriptive minus proscriptive. Four of the six are reliable with an LLM judge; "dismissing" is not and "understanding" is middling.
- The rubric's own prohibitions match Yuna's existing hard-mode rule (reflect his words, name the feeling, no story of her own, no advice he did not ask for) almost one to one, and the coach data say which corrections matter most: validation first, then withholding advice, then open questions.
- What "feels heard" in this paradigm is cheap to produce (a generous threshold; intent to comfort suffices), which is why the LLM supporters hit the ceiling; the risk for an AI persona is the opposite one: templatic, same-shaped validation (the models' novelty is half that of humans).

### Gaps
- The Fig. 1A example responses (the paper's own "good vs bad" pairs) and the Extended Data example conversation are images; not readable in the text.
- The judge's few-shot examples are not printed; the crowd-annotator instruction sheet in the companion paper is a figure.
- The paper does not test what the recipient (the sharer) felt; the role-player was an LLM. Outcomes are judge scores and third-party preferences.

## Key question 1: What exactly did the "warm relational" or responsive AI responses do that the control did not, and can those features become checks or coach labels?

### Takeaway
Sentence-level evidence exists only for Kleinert (every AI and human answer is published): the AI answered in first person, directly, with a reason and a named person, and in deep talk with an admitted feeling (fear, crying, regret), at the same length as humans; Telari's relational prompt content is unavailable, but its effects are measurable (warmth, perceived empathy, perceived chatbot self-disclosure, longer chats); Kumar supplies the checkable features for the "sharing something hard" case.

### Cited Findings
- Telari's conditions as described: Relational = "warm, empathetic, and human-like"; Non-relational = "factual and task-oriented, avoiding emotional language"; Default = standard setting, which "performed very similarly to the non-relational version" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)
- Telari Study 2 re-analysis: users rated the relational bot as disclosing more about itself (2.70 vs 2.02, d = 0.53), as more responsive (4.29 vs 3.00, d = 0.82) and higher on the empathy "response" subscale (4.53 vs 2.99, d = 1.16); and they stayed on the chat page longer (749 vs 438 s) — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kleinert AI answers: same length as humans (313.8 vs 295.5 words over the item set) but more self-related pronouns, emotion words and social words (LIWC 38.03 vs 32.31, partial eta² = .65 on n = 12 sets); example: "I last cried in front of someone when I spoke with my best friend about the death of his father. I felt for him and couldn't hold back my tears. I last cried alone when I had a bad day." (Max, AI) vs "I can't remember the last time I cried in front of someone, but I cried alone when my rabbit had to be put down." (Thomas, human) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- Kleinert AI answers always follow the same shape: claim, reason, feeling, often a person ("I'm most grateful for my family and friends. They provide me with support and love and are always there for me."); human answers more often hedge or deflect ("I generally don't cry in front of other people", "I would probably do everything exactly as I have so far, just take longer to do it") — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- Kumar's checkable features and their judge questions: validation ("acknowledging and affirming the partner's feelings"), paraphrase/mirroring ("repeat back a fragment of what was said before"), open questions about feeling or experience ("How did you feel about that job?" rather than "Were you happy in that job?"), no advice without permission, no self-shift, no minimising — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2); [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)
- The highest-frequency empathic sentence types in 16,975 human messages are short and formulaic: "I am so sorry to hear that", "I'm here for you", "completely understandable", "That sounds [adjective]", "How are you holding up?", "I understand how you feel" — [arXiv HTML, Tables 10–11](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- LLM judges reproduce expert ratings well for elaboration (κw 0.86), self-oriented (0.69), advice (0.66), validation (0.63), understanding (0.59) and poorly for dismissing (0.17) — [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)

### Inferences
- Features that can be checked mechanically or by a small judge: (a) answers the question he answered, in first person; (b) gives a reason or feeling, not just a fact; (c) includes a person from her life; (d) in deep topics admits something vulnerable; (e) names his feeling; (f) mirrors a fragment of his words; (g) asks one open question about his feeling or experience; (h) no suggestion unless he asked or she asked permission; (i) does not pivot to herself when he is hurting; (j) no "don't worry / stay strong / at least / we all" minimisers.
- Telari's warmth effect is probably the sum of several of these, but the paper cannot tell which, so the Kleinert and Kumar features are the only defensible sentence-level checks.

### Gaps
- Telari's actual prompt text and chat transcripts are not public; no sentence-level comparison of relational vs default is possible from the sources I could reach.
- None of the three papers manipulated single features (e.g. validation alone vs question alone), so no feature has its own effect size.

## Key question 2: What is perceived responsiveness made of, how was it measured, and which parts survive the AI label?

### Takeaway
Responsiveness was measured by Telari with three self-report items (wording unavailable) and defined by the authors as feeling understood, validated and cared for; it mediated the entire style-to-closeness effect under an openly AI-labelled bot (indirect 0.55, direct 0.18 ns in re-analysis). Kleinert did not measure responsiveness but shows that an AI label costs a small amount of closeness and some fan effort while closeness still builds.

### Cited Findings
- Authors' definition: "when we share something personal and feel understood, validated, and cared for, we develop a sense of connection" (perceived partner responsiveness theory) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)
- Measurement: three items resp_1–3 averaged, Study 2 only; Cronbach's alpha computed in the script (value not visible) — [Study 2 script](https://osf.io/download/cwerh/)
- Re-analysis: responsiveness correlates with closeness r = .65, with perceived empathy (understanding) r = .79, with satisfaction r = .68; style → responsiveness b = 1.27; responsiveness → closeness d1 = 0.43 controlling style, depth and self-disclosure; indirect 0.55 [0.30, 0.88]; the direct style effect drops to 0.18 (ns) — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- All Telari participants knew they were talking to a chatbot (the study is about chatbots; conditions differ only in style and depth), and the style still moved closeness (d ≈ 0.6–0.7), empathy (d ≈ 0.7–1.2) and need satisfaction (d ≈ 0.5–0.95) — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/02654075261438164); [Study 1 data](https://osf.io/download/2uahw/); [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kleinert label effect: closeness 4.17 (human label) vs 3.72 (AI label), partial eta² = .015; closeness still rose by 1.02–1.03 points under the AI label vs 1.21 under the human label; the AI label shortened participants' messages (181.8 vs 198.4 words) but did not reduce their LIWC self-disclosure (34.14 vs 34.05); message length correlated with closeness r = .257 — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Under the AI label, AI-written and human-written partner answers did not differ in closeness (source effect p = .082, label × source p = .155) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Kumar's abstract summarises prior work: attributing a response to AI makes recipients feel "less heard and validated" than the same response attributed to a human — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15245)
- Adjacent (not one of the three papers): adolescents (284 parent–adolescent pairs) preferred a relational chatbot style (first-person, affiliative, commitment language such as "I am here for you") over a transparent "explicitly nonhuman, informational" style and rated it more human-like, likeable, trustworthy and emotionally close; parents preferred the transparent style — [arXiv 2512.15117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15117)

### Inferences
- The three parts of responsiveness map onto Yuna's coach: "understood" = reflects the content he gave; "validated" = says it makes sense; "cared for" = signals availability and interest (Kumar's "I'm here for you", "How are you holding up?"). None of the three is label-dependent in Telari's data; the whole effect was obtained from a known chatbot.
- What the AI label removes is some of the fan's effort (shorter messages) and some ceiling on closeness, not the mechanism. Her replies should therefore be easy to answer briefly, and fan message length is a usable engagement meter.
- Kleinert's "AI cannot experience emotions, there are no such restrictions" argument cuts the other way for Yuna: a known AI that discloses freely is believed less on the "cared for" part, so her care signals should be concrete (remembering, following up) rather than declared.

### Gaps
- The item wording of Telari's responsiveness scale and its reliability are unknown; whether "cared for" was a separate item is unknown.
- No paper measured which sub-part of responsiveness the AI label dampens; that is an inference.

## Key question 3: In Kleinert, how much did the AI's self-disclosure matter and in which form, and what does that say for Yuna's "share" move and the never-invent-history rule?

### Takeaway
Partner self-disclosure explained about 6% of the variance in closeness (partial r = .24) and was the only content difference found between AI and human answers (same length); the form was strictly reciprocal (an answer to the same question the participant had just answered), first-person, concrete, with a named person and a feeling, drawn from a fixed biography; "balanced contribution" is not a construct in the paper.

### Cited Findings
- AI deep-talk answers had higher LIWC self-disclosure (38.03 vs 32.31, F(1,10) = 18.57, p = .002, partial eta² = .650) and equal length (313.8 vs 295.5 words, p = .392) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Partner self-disclosure predicted closeness, partial r(160) = .242 [.091, .382], p = .002 (R² = .058); participants disclosed more to the AI (34.80 vs 33.26, p = .049) and their disclosure tracked the partner's (r = .183, p = .020); their own disclosure did not predict closeness (r = .117, p = .139) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- The LIWC self-disclosure composite = self-related pronouns + emotion words + social-process words; no human coding of intimacy, no "balanced contribution" or "reciprocity" variable (the R script lists only self-disclosure and word count for self and partner) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [OSF R script](https://osf.io/download/pzx8e/)
- Form of the disclosures: each AI answer is 2–4 sentences, answers exactly the question, in first person, with a reason, usually a named relationship ("my mom", "my sister", "my best friend", "my girlfriend", "my dad") and a feeling ("I couldn't hold back my feelings", "I'm afraid it won't be good enough", "I often felt lonely") — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- The disclosures were anchored in six fixed biographies written once (one PaLM 2 session) and stayed consistent across all 11 items (e.g. Anna, 25, law in Freiburg, "friendly and open", later: "I'm most proud of my family and friends", "I last cried in front of someone when I told my mom about my breakup") — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)
- The authors note "high self-disclosure can be inappropriate in some settings" and that AI "may be less precise at adjusting self-disclosure to context" — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Telari: users rated the relational bot as disclosing more about itself (d = 0.53) and chatbot self-disclosure correlated with closeness r = .37 and with satisfaction r = .41, but at low absolute levels (M 2.0–3.0) — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kumar: in trouble talk a share of one's own similar experience is "self-oriented" (misattuned), rare (1% of concept tags), concentrated in the worst-rated decile (3.6%) and absent from the best (0.1%); the coach reduced it (β = −0.22) — [arXiv HTML, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)

### Inferences
- The share that built closeness was a reciprocal answer, not a volunteered tangent: it came right after the fan's own answer to the same prompt, matched the topic, and was proportionate in length. Yuna's share move misfires as tangents because it lacks that trigger (his disclosure on a topic) and that constraint (same topic, same depth).
- The quantity that mattered was density of I / feeling / people words at equal length, so a share needs a feeling and a person more than it needs detail.
- A fixed biography was enough; the characters never improvised beyond it. This supports the never-invent-history rule as a consistency rule (one fact sheet, every share drawn from it) and says nothing against fiction per se, since the biographies were fiction.
- When he is sharing something hard, the share move should stay off (Kumar); when the exchange is get-to-know-you, it should be on and reciprocal (Kleinert, Telari).

### Gaps
- No paper tested what happens when an AI contradicts its earlier history, so the cost of invented history is not quantified here.
- The AI-vs-human content comparison rests on 12 response sets; the closeness correlation is r = .24 in one deep-talk cell (n = 164).
- The design had no reacting, follow-up or asking by the partner, so the papers cannot say how a share should be combined with a question.

## Key question 4: Kumar's six dimensions: exact definitions, what counts as self-oriented or dismissive, and how they could be used as separate labels for the coach, the scorecard or starring replies

### Takeaway
The six are defined by one line each plus a 1–5 judge question; "self-oriented" means moving the focus from his experience to hers (including "I've been through the same"), and "dismissing" means minimising or invalidating (including "don't worry", "stay strong", "we all", speculative "maybe" explanations, "it takes time"); four of the six are reliable as LLM labels, dismissing is not.

### Cited Findings
- Definitions (verbatim): encouraging elaboration = "asking questions to prompt the partner to share more about their experiences and emotions"; validating emotions = "acknowledging and affirming the partner's feelings"; demonstrating understanding = "paraphrasing the partner's experiences to show comprehension"; unsolicited advice = "offering guidance without first asking if it is wanted"; self-oriented responding = "shifting focus away from the partner's experience"; dismissing emotions = "minimizing or invalidating the partner's feelings" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Judge questions and scale (1 = not at all, 5 = very much), five runs at temperature 0 averaged — [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2); [AsPredicted hdjx-tdsr](https://aspredicted.org/hdjx-tdsr.pdf)
- What was coded self-oriented in the data: "Mentions having personally experienced a similar situation or event", "Mentions personal experience or connection with cancer or someone who has had cancer", "Describes personal actions or strategies taken to overcome a challenge or improve the situation" — [arXiv HTML, Tables 12 and 16](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- What was coded dismissing: "don't feel guilty / don't blame yourself", "something will take time", "I think / I believe" opinions, "what the deceased person would want", "maybe" explanations, "don't worry", "the other party may be unaware", "setbacks do not define a person", "we all / I think we all", "change or transformation"; expert example "Yea, we all wish things were different…" (de-individualises) — [arXiv HTML, Tables 12 and 16](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2); [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)
- What was coded advice (note the overlap with cheer-up language): "be strong / stay strong", "take it one day at a time", "maintain a positive mindset", "lean on others", "you need to / you have to / you must", "find a new job", "update your resume", "talk to your manager", "take a break", "keep your chin up", "take care of yourself" — [arXiv HTML, Tables 12 and 16](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- What was coded validation: "it's okay / understandable to feel", "completely understandable", naming the emotion (terrible, scary, overwhelming, sad, devastating), "That sounds [adj]", "you're not alone", "it's okay to not always be strong", normalising as natural — [arXiv HTML, Table 10](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- What was coded understanding: "I understand how you feel", "I can't imagine / I can only imagine", "I hear you", explicit acknowledgement that this period is tough, metaphors of carrying something heavy or a world turned upside down — [arXiv HTML, Table 11](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- What was coded elaboration: specific questions about the person involved ("What was he like?", "How is she doing?"), "How are you holding up?", "How are you feeling right now?", "What happened?", asking for a favourite memory, asking what the hardest part is, asking whether they want to talk about it — [arXiv HTML, Table 11](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Reliability per label (experts vs LLM κw): elaboration 0.86, self-oriented 0.69, advice 0.66, validation 0.63, understanding 0.59, dismissing 0.17 — [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)
- Composite and its meaning for raters: a 5-point gap in the composite (sum of three prescriptive minus three proscriptive scores) is preferred 73% of the time, 10 points 93%; the coach's average gain was 2.9 points ("an independent observer, blind to condition, preferring the post-training response approximately two-thirds of the time") — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- The coach never told users to "show empathy"; it used content terms, pointed at a specific sentence, gave one better sentence, kept to five sentences — [arXiv HTML, Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Coach corrections by frequency: validation 32.7%, stop advising 24.2%, ask open questions 21.2%, paraphrase 11.2%, stop dismissing 5.3%, stop self-shifting 4.3% — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)

### Inferences
- As coach labels: six 1–5 scores, applied only when the reader marks "he is sharing something hard"; use few-shot anchors from the tables above; treat "dismissing" as advisory (low reliability) and "elaboration", "advice", "self-oriented" as hard.
- As scorecard: the composite (prescriptive minus proscriptive) per hard-mode reply, with a 5-point difference as the meaningful unit; averaging several judge runs is what the authors did to stabilise it.
- As starring in the practice room: a reply can be starred when it scores 4–5 on validation and elaboration and 1 on advice and self-oriented; the coach's one-better-sentence style (point at the sentence, offer a replacement, five sentences max) is a tested format for the practice-room note.
- The "comfort" and "reassure" moves need splitting: "it's okay to feel X" is validation; "don't worry, it'll be fine" is dismissing; "stay strong" is advice in this rubric.

### Gaps
- Examples shown to the judge in Kumar's own runs are not published; the taxonomy tables are the best public anchor set.
- The rubric was validated on strangers' trouble talk; its reliability in a flirtatious ongoing chat is untested.

## Key question 5: Conversation depth: did deeper topics help only in the lab, and what are the boundary conditions that limit transfer to a long-running flirtatious chat?

### Takeaway
In both papers deep topics had no direct effect on closeness (Telari: 2.12 vs 2.04, ns; Kleinert: 4.17 vs 4.06, ns); depth paid off only when answered responsively (Telari's serial path, 0.14 in re-analysis) or when the AI's own deep answers disclosed more (Kleinert's interaction, partial eta² = .025). Everything was single-session (4–15 minutes), text, students or Prolific adults, non-romantic, and in Kleinert non-interactive.

### Cited Findings
- Telari Study 2: depth raised self-reported self-disclosure (d = 0.36) and lengthened chats (719 vs 451 s) but did not move closeness, satisfaction, need satisfaction, empathy or intention (all d ≤ 0.07); style dominated — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Telari's depth manipulation was weaker for the relational bot: on the depth check, relational small talk scored 0.14 vs non-relational small talk −1.37 (interaction t = −4.21, p < .001): a warm bot already feels personal in small talk — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kleinert Study 1: deep vs small talk main effect F = 0.42, p = .518; the gain was specific to the AI partner in deep talk (4.48 vs 3.85) and reversed as a trend for humans (3.85 vs 4.33) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Kleinert chose only non-interactive deep items and excluded items "likely to evoke strong negative emotional responses"; sessions were about 15 minutes with pre-written answers; participants were 18–35 students in same-gender, friendship-framed pairs; the authors say the deep-talk advantage "may not generalise to goal-directed relationship building" — [Supplementary Material PDF](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf); [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- Telari's sessions: eight minutes (Study 1) or until the bot ended the structured chat (Study 2, mean page time about 10 minutes); mostly young Italian adults (M age 27–28); the first author: "a single eight-minute chat may not reflect how a relationship with AI develops over time" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/); [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kumar: four-minute chats, strangers, five fixed troubles; the authors explicitly exclude "romantic partnerships, family, or close friends" and say the intimate idiom "differs" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Kleinert's hypothesis that AI might mimic impersonal small talk but not personal deep talk was reversed; the authors do not explain the small-talk null — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)

### Inferences
- Depth is a tool for eliciting his disclosure, not a closeness lever in itself; the return comes from what she does with the disclosure. In a chat that is already warm, a formal "deep question" adds little depth perception.
- The Fast Friends items that worked are get-to-know-you prompts (pride, gratitude, friendship values, an unrealised dream, a treasured memory), not confessions; the papers never tested sexual or romantic depth.
- Everything that transfers is about the first 15 minutes of a relationship; nothing in these papers speaks to week 6 of a paying fan, to repeated sessions, or to memory across sessions.

### Gaps
- No study ran more than one session or measured return visits, so persistence of the closeness gain is unknown.
- No romantic or flirtatious framing anywhere; no data on men talking to a female persona specifically.
- Telari's "small talk" vs "deep" item lists are not public.

## What Yuna could use (all items are proposals, none implemented)

### Takeaway
The evidence supports making perceived responsiveness (understood, validated, cared for) the coach's master check, splitting the hard-mode coach into Kumar's six labels, rebuilding the share move as a reciprocal same-topic answer from a fixed fact sheet, and treating deep questions as disclosure prompts whose value depends on the reply that follows.

### Cited Findings
- Style → responsiveness → closeness is the whole effect in Telari's data (indirect 0.55, direct 0.18 ns) — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- The AI partner's closeness advantage came from disclosure density at equal length; the fan's own disclosure did not predict closeness — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- The coach's most frequent and most effective corrections were validation, withholding advice and open questions; AI coaching did not change turn counts or word counts — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- LLM supporters reach the rubric ceiling but with half the novelty of humans and a heavy validation template — [arXiv HTML, Appendices I–K](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- The AI label shortens fan messages (−8%) without reducing their disclosure; message length correlates with closeness r = .26 — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)

### Inferences
- PROPOSAL 1, responsiveness triad in the coach. Replace or extend "answers him" and "understood" with three 1–5 scores: understood (reflects the content he gave, mirrors a fragment), validated (says his reaction makes sense, names the feeling), cared for (shows availability or interest in him specifically). Basis: Telari's mediation; Kumar's affective and cognitive sentence types as anchors.
- PROPOSAL 2, six hard-mode labels. When the reader flags "he is sharing something hard", score the three candidates on Kumar's six questions (five runs averaged if cost allows), pick by composite, and veto any candidate with advice ≥ 3 (unless he asked), self-oriented ≥ 3, or dismissing ≥ 3; treat the dismissing score as advisory because even experts disagree on it. Keep the existing hard-mode rule as is; it already matches the rubric.
- PROPOSAL 3, permission gate for suggestions. In hard mode, allow a suggestion only after an explicit permission question ("do you want ideas or just me listening?"), copying the coach rule "only do so after given permission... the goal is to support emotionally, not to fix".
- PROPOSAL 4, split comfort / reassure. Reclassify "reassure" lines of the "don't worry, it'll be fine, stay strong, at least" type as dismissing or advice and block them in hard mode; keep "it's okay to feel X", "that sounds brutal", "I'm here, tell me" as valid comfort (Video 1: do not try to cheer him up; be with him as he feels).
- PROPOSAL 5, question band by kind, not count. Count closed yes/no questions against the band and open questions about his feeling or experience as "curious"; in hard mode exempt one open question per reply from the 30–45% band (Kumar's largest and most reliably judged gain was elaboration; the authors still saw no rise in turn counts, so this is not "more questions", it is better ones).
- PROPOSAL 6, share move rebuilt as a reciprocal answer. Trigger only after he has answered a get-to-know-you prompt (or volunteered something personal in a non-hard moment); the share must be on the same topic, 2–4 sentences, first person, with one named person and one feeling or reason, and never longer than his message; it must come from a fixed fact sheet (Kleinert's six bios never contradicted themselves), which is also the never-invent-history rule restated as "every share has a source line".
- PROPOSAL 7, share move off in hard mode. The reader's "sharing something hard" flag disables the share move entirely (Kumar: self-oriented = bottom decile marker), so the two literatures stop pulling against each other.
- PROPOSAL 8, deep prompts as a sub-move with a reply obligation. Add a small bank of Fast Friends-style prompts (proud of, grateful for, value in a friend, dream not yet done and why, most valuable memory) gated by rapport, at most one per N replies, and require that the reply after his answer is responsive (triad ≥ 4) before any sale or steer; the value of depth in both papers lay in the reply.
- PROPOSAL 9, warmth dial unchanged for declared feelings, opened for availability. Keep "I care about you" blocked early (no paper tested declared feelings); allow availability lines ("I'm here", "tell me everything", "I'm not going anywhere tonight") in hard mode, which Kumar's data classify as affective support, not as declared feelings.
- PROPOSAL 10, anti-template check. Over her last ten replies, flag repeated validation stems ("I'm so sorry", "that sounds", "completely understandable") and repeated shapes (sympathy → paraphrase → question); Kumar's models converged to 24–31% validation sentences and half the novelty of humans, and the top human decile was less novel than random, so aim for the human top-decile range rather than the model range.
- PROPOSAL 11, fan message length as an engagement meter. Track his average words per message; a drop is the signature Kleinert found under the AI label, and length tracked closeness.
- PROPOSAL 12, scorecard and starring. Use the six-label composite per hard-mode reply and the triad per ordinary reply; a 5-point composite gap is the unit raters notice (73%); star practice-room replies at validation ≥ 4, elaboration ≥ 4, advice ≤ 1, self-oriented ≤ 1; write practice-room notes in the coach's format (name the sentence, give one better sentence, at most five sentences).

### Gaps
- None of these proposals has been tested in a flirtatious or paid context; all effect sizes above come from short, non-romantic, single-session studies.

## What not to infer

### Takeaway
The papers do not show that deep topics, AI self-disclosure, or warmth alone create closeness, that AI beats humans in general, that the six labels apply outside trouble talk, or anything about weeks-long relationships, fans, men, or money.

### Cited Findings
- Deep topics had no direct effect on closeness in either paper (Telari depth d = 0.06; Kleinert F = 0.42, p = .518) — [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/); [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- "AI outperforms humans" held only in deep talk, only under a human label, only with PaLM 2 (February 2024), pre-written answers, six characters; under the AI label there was no human-vs-AI difference (p = .082) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)
- The participant's own self-disclosure did not predict closeness (r = .117, ns); in Telari self-disclosure's effect ran through responsiveness (direct d2 = 0.11, p = .118) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kumar's framework is for "low-familiarity contexts"; the authors say intimate idioms differ and they did not study them — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Kumar trained humans, not an AI; the AI supporters were already at the rubric ceiling, so "coaching improves empathy" does not translate into "coach the model harder" — [arXiv HTML, Appendix J](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)
- Mind attribution "experience" stayed low under every style (1.5–2.2 of 7) — the warm bot was not believed to feel — [Study 1 data](https://osf.io/download/2uahw/); [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Telari's intention to use again did not differ by style in Study 1 (p = .28) although it did in Study 2 (d = 0.52) — [Study 1 data](https://osf.io/download/2uahw/); [Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)
- Kleinert's self-disclosure is a word-count proxy on 12 response sets; Kumar's labels are LLM judgements with poor reliability for "dismissing" (κw 0.17) — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889); [arXiv 2506.10150v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2)
- All Telari numbers here are my re-analysis of the public data; the paper's models (lavaan, 5,000 bootstraps, time covariate, possibly a serial specification) may give different values — [Study 2 script](https://osf.io/download/cwerh/)

### Inferences
- Do not read "warm relational" as "declares feelings"; the content of Telari's prompt is unknown and the measured ingredients are empathy, responsiveness and a little self-disclosure.
- Do not read Kleinert as licence for the persona to disclose freely about anything; the authors themselves warn high self-disclosure is sometimes inappropriate and that AI adjusts it poorly to context, and Kumar classifies self-shares during his hard moments as misattuned.
- Do not treat a questions-per-reply count as the goal; Kumar's gains came with unchanged turn and word counts.
- Do not expect the AI label to be neutral or to be overcome by wording; expect a small closeness ceiling and shorter fan messages, and design for brief fan replies.
- Do not generalise from "feels heard in four minutes with a stranger" to readiness to pay; no paper measured anything commercial.

### Gaps
- No source addresses the gender pairing (male fan, female persona), romantic framing, repeated sessions, memory across sessions, or monetisation; those remain open questions that these papers cannot settle.
