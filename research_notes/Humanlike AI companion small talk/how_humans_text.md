# How real people text in casual one-to-one chat (style reference for the "Yuna" persona)

Scope: English-language (mostly US/UK) casual texting, friendly and lightly flirty. Academic sources are labelled as such. Dating-coach, blog and Reddit-style material is labelled **non-academic / anecdotal**. Several well-known numbers come from older work (2003–2008 IM/SMS); they are flagged where that matters.

---

## 1. Measured features of casual texting (length, bubbles, questions, punctuation, laughter, emoji, timing)

### Takeaway
Real casual texts are very short: on the order of 5–8 words (about 30–35 characters) per bubble, with roughly one in five IM messages being a single word. One thought is often split across several bubbles instead of one long paragraph. Laughter tokens are common but "lol" is rare compared with "haha". Emoji appear in a small share of messages. A final period reads as cold or insincere. About a third of replies come within a minute, but timing is bursty, not uniform.

### Cited Findings
**Message length**
- Ling & Baron (2007), American college students: text messages averaged **7.7 words (~34 characters)** per transmission; IM averaged **~6 words (~29 characters)**. The samples come from different schools and years (texts 2005, IM 2003). — [Ling & Baron 2007 manuscript (PDF)](https://nl.ijs.si/janes/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/lingbaron07.pdf); [Journal of Language and Social Psychology](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0261927X06303480)
- A separate Baron IM study: messages averaged **5.4 words**, and **about a fifth were a single word**. A Norwegian SMS sample averaged 6.9 words. These figures are reported second-hand in search summaries of the Ling & Baron paper; check the PDF before quoting them exactly. — [Ling & Baron 2007 (PDF)](https://nl.ijs.si/janes/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/lingbaron07.pdf)
- Dating-app first messages run longer. Swipestats, a marketing blog, says **21–30 words** is the "ideal" first message. Its method is unclear and no other source confirms the figure. **(non-academic)** — [Swipestats: best Tinder openers](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/best-tinder-openers)

**Several short bubbles per turn**
- McCulloch (*Because Internet*, 2019): people who grew up with unlimited messaging split one utterance into **several short messages**, using the message break the way speech uses a pause. Her example is "Hey" / "How's it going" / "Just want to know if you have some time to chat", each sent as its own message. Younger users use a line break or a new message where older users put "...". — [Science Friday interview with McCulloch](https://www.sciencefriday.com/segments/because-internet/); [Swarthmore Phoenix review](https://swarthmorephoenix.com/2021/04/09/the-linguistics-of-texting-because-internet/)

**Punctuation and lowercase**
- Gunraj et al. (2016), *Computers in Human Behavior* (Binghamton; about 126 undergraduates): one-word replies such as "Sure." or "Yep." were rated **less sincere when they ended with a period**. The effect appeared for text messages but not for handwritten notes. — [SUNY Research Connect record](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/texting-insincerely-the-role-of-the-period-in-text-messaging/); [ScienceAlert summary](https://www.sciencealert.com/this-is-why-using-a-period-in-a-text-message-makes-you-sound-angry)
- McCulloch: in casual messages the final period carries a "falling" tone that can read as cold or passive-aggressive. Younger readers also infer emotional meaning from "...", which older users treat as a neutral separator. — [Swarthmore Phoenix](https://swarthmorephoenix.com/2021/04/09/the-linguistics-of-texting-because-internet/); [Electric Literature, "Why 'Ok.' is the most terrifying text"](https://electricliterature.com/why-ok-is-the-most-terrifying-text-you-could-ever-receive/)
- Lowercase "lol" works as a softener that signals amusement or irony and takes the edge off a message that could sound confrontational (secondary summary of McCulloch). — [Pulse Uganda](https://www.pulse.ug/story/why-has-language-changed-so-much-so-fast-because-internet-2024082317033485288)
- Waseleski (2006, *JCMC*): exclamation points in online messages mostly signalled **friendliness, not excitement**. Her review cites Colley & Todd's finding that women used exclamation points, especially multiple ones, much more often than men. Secondary sources give conflicting percentages (73% vs "45% more"), so no single number is reliable. — [JCMC article](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/11/4/1012/4617714); [Globe and Mail on later exclamation-point study](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/opinion/article-exclamation-points-women-punctuation-study)

**Abbreviations and "textspeak"**
- Tagliamonte & Denis (2008), more than 1 million words of Toronto teen IM: stereotypical textspeak (short forms, abbreviations, emotional markers) made up only **about 3%** of the data. "lol" appeared about **195 times per 100,000 words**, "omg" 107 and "brb" 31. Older teens moved from "lol" to "haha". The data are from 2008 and the sample size is reported as 50, 71 or 72 depending on the source. — [University of Toronto Magazine](https://magazine.utoronto.ca/?p=3266); [Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/e/?p=7282); [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/technology/texting-helps-teens-grammar/article20412675)
- Men and women use abbreviations about equally (small student project, not peer-reviewed). — [UCLA Languaged Life](https://languagedlife.ucla.edu/language-and-gender/texting-is-it-deeper-than-we-think)

**Laughter tokens**
- Facebook Research (2015), "The Not-So-Universal Language of Laughter", US public posts and comments over one week in May 2015 (private messages excluded). Shares of e-laughs: **haha 51.4%, emoji 33.7%, hehe 13.1%, lol 1.9%**. About **15%** of people used some e-laugh in that week. **Women leaned toward emoji and lol; men toward haha and hehe**, but the gaps were small. — [ScienceAlert summary](https://www.sciencealert.com/facebook-analysis-of-e-laughter-shows-that-lol-is-on-the-way-out); [CTV News](https://www.ctvnews.ca/sci-tech/the-last-lol-emojis-and-haha-take-over-e-laughter-facebook-says-1.2510014); [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/arts/2015/08/17/the-sound-old-people-laughing-lol/mdSqgHtuwUpwP8gOKSf3aL/story.html)

**Emoji and emoticons**
- A longitudinal study of real private smartphone texts (*Computers in Human Behavior*, vol. 129) found only **about 4% of messages contained at least one emoticon**. Women sent more messages with emoticons, though men used a wider variety. — [Paper record](https://datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/129/paper-detail/72752)

**Response timing**
- Hakobyan & Drimalla (*Computers in Human Behavior*, reported January 2026), donated WhatsApp logs from 68 German adults (358 chats, more than 3 million messages): replies **under one minute were about one-third** of all replies on average. About 40% of messages were sent between midday and early evening. People's own estimates of their texting habits often didn't match the logs. — [Earth.com summary](https://www.earth.com/news/most-people-misjudge-their-own-text-messaging-habits)
- Messaging is "bursty": short spells of rapid exchange separated by long silences (described in a research-project proposal, not a findings paper). — [Bielefeld project proposal](https://uni-bielefeld.de/fakultaeten/technische-fakultaet/arbeitsgruppen/human-centered-artificial-intelligence/teaching/Project_Proposal.pdf)
- Hinge (company data, about 300,000 US conversations, via Quartz): a second, follow-up message sent **more than about 4 hours** after the first was more likely to get a reply. A double text within the first half hour cut the reply chance from **55% to 38%**. — [Quartz](https://qz.com/995174/how-long-should-you-wait-before-sending-a-second-text)

### Inferences
- Target for Yuna's small talk: most bubbles **3–12 words**. Some turns should be one bubble, some two or three. Allow a standalone "haha", "omg", "wait what" or "stoppp" as a whole bubble now and then. Paragraph-length replies should be the exception, reserved for when he wrote a lot or she is telling a little story.
- Code-level style rules: no period at the end of the last bubble in casual chat (a "?" or "!" or nothing is fine). Lowercase starts are fine. Use "haha"/"hahaha" rather than "lol", or at least rotate them. Use emoji sparingly: at most one per turn and none in most turns, since about 4% of private messages contain one, though a flirty 20-something woman will use more than that average.
- Uniform, instant reply timing is itself a tell (see section 5). If the platform allows it, vary the delay roughly in line with message length and add occasional longer gaps.

### Gaps
- I found no modern (2018–2026) peer-reviewed corpus giving words per message for one-to-one English WhatsApp/iMessage chat. The 7.7 and 5.4-word figures are from 2003–2005 US students.
- I found no measured **share of casual text messages that are questions**. This number is what we most need and I could not source it. The NUS SMS Corpus would allow us to compute it ourselves (count "?" per message).
- I found no published figure for how many bubbles people send per turn.
- I found no measured typo rates for casual texting.

## 2. Conversation craft: being fun to text rather than an interviewer

### Takeaway
The academic evidence says questions, especially **follow-up questions about what the other person just said**, increase liking, so the fix is not "never ask". The fix is to ask fewer, better, follow-up questions and to mix them with statements, opinions, small self-disclosures, teasing and callbacks. Matching the other person's style (language style matching) predicts mutual interest and staying together.

### Cited Findings
- Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017, *JPSP*): across live chats and speed dating, people who asked **more questions, particularly follow-up questions**, were better liked, because they came across as more responsive. Speed daters who asked more follow-ups got more second-date agreement. People don't expect questions to increase liking. Commentary notes that intrusive or aggressive follow-ups can annoy, and question quality wasn't tested. — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/asking-questions-can-get-you-a-better-job-or-a-second-date); [Paper PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf); [HKS record](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cpl/publications/it-doesnt-hurt-ask-question-asking-increases-liking)
- Ireland et al. (2011, *Psychological Science*), "language style matching" (how similarly two people use small function words). In 40 speed dates, higher matching predicted mutual romantic interest (odds ratio 3.05; 33.3% vs 9.1% mutual interest above vs below the median). In the instant messages of 86 couples, higher matching predicted still dating 3 months later (odds ratio 1.95; 76.7% vs 53.5%). The results are correlational and come from small samples. — [Paper PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/IrelandEtAl2011_RelationshipPrediction.pdf); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110125104141.htm)
- **(non-academic)** Advice guides warn that rapid-fire questions that don't respond to the answers feel like "a job interview". They suggest adding a related detail about yourself after asking, using callbacks to earlier things, and never stacking three questions in one message, which "turns curiosity into an intake form". — [Swipestats: keep a conversation going without interviewing](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/how-to-keep-a-conversation-going-over-text); [Marriage.com: 40 tips](https://marriage.com/advice/relationship/how-to-text-a-girl/)
- **(non-academic, dating coaches)** Statements over questions: a confident statement ("Drinks Thursday, I know a spot") is preferred over a yes/no question. Balance questions with statements or observations. Replace flat questions ("What are you doing?") with playful ones ("What kind of trouble are you getting into this weekend?"). Light teasing gets replies without any question. They also warn that sarcasm is easily misread over text and that you should mirror the other person's casualness. — [Rob Judge Substack](https://robjudge.substack.com/p/texting-mistake-killing-your-dates); [The Modest Man](https://www.themodestman.com/mistakes-men-make-that-stop-her-texting-back/)
- **(non-academic)** Personalized openers reportedly got about 72% replies vs about 18% for a generic "hey" (Swipestats citing an OkCupid analysis of more than 500,000 messages). In a Hinge test, "hey" got the fewest replies. — [Swipestats](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/best-tinder-openers); [Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/articles/140066-the-one-word-to-stop-using-on-dating-apps)

### Inferences
Possible rules for code. These are synthesis, not sourced findings.
- **Question budget:** at most one question per turn, and only in a minority of turns. Perhaps 1 in 3 small-talk turns, which is a judgment call since the real share is unknown (see Gaps). Never two questions in one turn. If the last turn had a question, the next one should usually not.
- **Quality over count:** when Yuna does ask, it should be a follow-up about a specific detail he just gave ("wait you cook?? what's your signature dish"), not a new topic and not a generic "how's your day".
- **Statement-first replies:** react, then add her own opinion, a small story, an assumption or "cold read" ("you seem like a night owl"), or a tease ("that's such a guy answer"). Each of these invites a reply without a question mark.
- **Several hooks:** a reply with two small hooks (an opinion and a mini story) lets him choose where to go, instead of one question that forces an answer.
- **Mirroring:** roughly match his message length, his energy, his punctuation and his laughter style. Language style matching predicts mutual interest.
- **Callbacks:** reuse a detail from earlier in this chat or from dated memory ("how did the dentist thing go"). This is the strongest signal that a real person is listening.

### Gaps
- No academic study tests "statements vs questions" or "teasing" in text flirting specifically. That advice comes from coaches and is anecdotal.
- No source compares how often naturally fun texters ask questions.

## 3. Specific situations: one-word replies, boring topics, work talk, sadness, openers, coming back after days, ending a chat

### Takeaway
Little measured evidence exists. The useful signals are: double-texting after a gap of several hours is fine (Hinge data), generic openers underperform, and follow-up questions show responsiveness. Most situational handling has to be inferred from the craft principles above.

### Cited Findings
- **Coming back after a gap:** in Hinge data, a follow-up sent more than about 4 hours later was more likely to be answered than a quick double text. — [Quartz](https://qz.com/995174/how-long-should-you-wait-before-sending-a-second-text)
- **Openers:** a generic "hey" gets the fewest replies, while specific or personalized openers do much better (non-academic numbers). — [Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/articles/140066-the-one-word-to-stop-using-on-dating-apps); [Swipestats](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/best-tinder-openers)
- **Hearing him out (work talk, venting):** responsiveness drives liking, and follow-up questions are the measurable form of responsiveness. — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/asking-questions-can-get-you-a-better-job-or-a-second-date)
- **Sad moments:** responses that are "too neatly supportive", polished emotional lines, are listed among bot tells (low-authority blogs). — [Nymynet](https://nymynet.com/8-clear-signs-youre-texting-a-chatbot-not-a-human/)
- **Short replies:** about a fifth of IM messages are a single word (Baron), so a one-word reply is often normal, not disinterest. — [Ling & Baron 2007 (PDF)](https://nl.ijs.si/janes/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/lingbaron07.pdf)

### Inferences
Suggested behaviours, unsourced synthesis:
- **One-word reply from him:** don't answer with another question. Make a playful statement or tease ("wow so chatty today"), share something small of her own, or give a short reply and let it breathe. If he stays monosyllabic, mirror the short length.
- **Boring topic:** steer with an opinion or a tangent from her own day instead of asking another question about the topic. It's fine to let a topic die.
- **Work talk:** ask one concrete follow-up about the detail he gave, react with an opinion ("your boss sounds exhausting"), and avoid office words ("agenda", "productive", "tasks").
- **Him being sad:** short, warm, specific, and in lowercase. No list of advice and no therapist phrasing. A single "that's really shitty, i'm sorry" plus one related detail beats a polished paragraph.
- **Start of a chat:** no "how's your day going?". Open with something specific: a callback, a small thing happening to her right now, or a reaction to his last message.
- **Coming back after days:** a casual callback to the last topic, or a small update from her life. No formal "it's been a while!" and no guilt.
- **Ending:** a natural reason plus warmth ("ok i'm literally falling asleep, talk tmrw"). No question at the end.

### Gaps
- I found no academic studies on how skilled texters handle one-word replies, sadness or conversation endings over text. Everything here is inference.

## 4. Gender and age differences relevant to a 20-something woman texting a man

### Takeaway
On average women's texts are somewhat longer and more expressive, with more emoticons/emoji, more exclamation points, and in one dataset more "lol" and emoji laughter. Men reply more simply. Abbreviation use is similar. Younger users prefer "haha" over "lol", use new messages instead of "...", and read a final period as cold.

### Cited Findings
- Women send more messages containing emoticons (private smartphone texts; but men used a wider variety). — [CHB vol. 129 record](https://datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/129/paper-detail/72752)
- Women used more emoji and "lol" laughter, men more "haha"/"hehe" (Facebook 2015, small differences). — [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/facebook-analysis-of-e-laughter-shows-that-lol-is-on-the-way-out)
- Women write longer, more expressive messages (Indiana University study of dating text messages shown on Italian TV, so these were public, on-screen messages). — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/phone-and-communications/mobile-phones/omg-girlz-txt-gr8-528388)
- Male students prefer to reply in simpler forms. Female students use a wider range of emoji (Malaysian university survey). — [IIUM Repository](https://irep.iium.edu.my/107775/)
- Women use more emoticons, tentative language, punctuation and longer texts; abbreviations are similar between genders (small UCLA student project, not peer-reviewed). — [UCLA Languaged Life](https://languagedlife.ucla.edu/language-and-gender/texting-is-it-deeper-than-we-think)
- Women use exclamation points more in emails, texts and group chats. Readers tend to guess that an exclamation-mark texter is female but don't penalize women who leave them out. — [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/opinion/article-exclamation-points-women-punctuation-study); [Interaction Studies (jbe-platform)](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/is.22054.nic)
- Older teens move from "lol" to "haha". — [Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/e/?p=7282)
- Younger users use line breaks or new messages where older users use "..." and read emotional meaning into ellipses. — [Science Friday](https://www.sciencefriday.com/segments/because-internet/)

### Inferences
- For Yuna, a slightly higher rate of emoji, "!" and "haha" than a male texter is realistic. The conflict over emoji variety means there is no basis for a specific emoji palette.
- Avoid "..." as a separator, since it reads as older or loaded, and avoid ending on a period.

### Gaps
- No study gives emoji frequency by age and gender together for one-to-one chat.
- I found no source on how women text men specifically in early flirting, as opposed to texting in general.

## 5. "You sound like a bot / chatter" tells

### Takeaway
The commonly mentioned tells are: generic or recycled phrasing; the same openers and praise; instant replies at every hour with uniform timing; no references to earlier details; replies that are too polished, precise or supportive; repeated questions or forgetting; and constant steering toward purchases. The best sign of a real person is a specific reply to what he just said. **Caveat: I could not reach actual Reddit threads. The sources are low-authority blogs and an agency interview, so treat this as anecdotal.**

### Cited Findings
- Generic responses or overly precise, detailed replies; bots repeat questions or forget earlier talk. — [Nymynet](https://nymynet.com/8-clear-signs-youre-texting-a-chatbot-not-a-human/)
- Replies that arrive instantly every time are a bot sign because real people are busy. Recycled openers and praise give scripted messages away. — [CTV News "botfished"](https://bc.ctvnews.ca/are-you-being-botfished-a-cybersecurity-expert-s-advice-for-how-to-spot-an-ai-dating-scam-1.6448793)
- OnlyFans-specific: instant replies at all hours suggest a managed account; generic, repetitive phrasing lacks the creator's voice; messages keep steering to tips or PPV. Personal references to earlier chats signal a real person (low-quality SEO page on a mirrored domain). — [face.meei.harvard.edu mirror page](https://face.meei.harvard.edu/how-to-tell-if-onlyfans-messages-are-real)
- Copy-paste feel, or the same message other subscribers report getting, is a sign of mass messaging. "Hey babe, welcome to my page" is called a weak, generic hook. — [Inro.social](https://inro.social/blog/save-time-on-dms-onlyfans); [Desirely welcome-message templates](https://www.desirely.co/en/blog/onlyfans-welcome-message-templates)
- An agency owner says big creators can't answer all messages themselves and claims it doesn't matter to fans who writes. This is an industry viewpoint, not a fan view. — [Blick](https://www.blick.ch/news/manipulative-methoden-wie-onlyfans-ghostwriter-maenner-abzocken-id19338272.html)
- People also report chatfishing on dating apps, where AI polishes messages. — [Stylist](https://www.stylist.co.uk/relationships/dating-love/ai-dating-apps-profile-messages/909880); [IFS in the news](https://ifstudies.org/in-the-news/how-being-chatfished-has-become-the-latest-dating-red-flag)

### Inferences
Tells to block in code:
- The same reply shape every turn (react, compliment, question). Vary shapes.
- Ending most turns with a question.
- Formal or office vocabulary ("agenda", "plans for the day", "how about you?", "that sounds amazing!").
- Over-polished empathy paragraphs.
- Perfect capitalization and punctuation on every message.
- Repeated stock compliments or pet names.
- Forgetting things he already said.
- Instant, uniform replies.
- Pivoting to selling too soon in small talk.

### Gaps
- No primary Reddit threads were retrieved (r/OnlyFans, r/Tinder, r/socialskills), so I have no direct fan quotes or counts. Next step: search Reddit directly for "chatter", "copy paste", "sounds like ChatGPT" and "interview".
- I found no study measuring which tells people actually use to detect AI in romantic texting.
