# Acknowledge the specific, sell on readiness

The twelve papers agree on one usable mechanism and one trap. The mechanism: a man feels heard when the reply shows it took in the specific thing he said (his feeling named and called justified, his effort or a quality of his recognised, his side taken, one of his phrases repeated back, or one question that picks up a detail he gave), while the moves that feel like listening to the writer (reassurance, "I'm so sorry", her own similar story, advice he did not ask for, more questions) did nothing or counted against him: in Yin's experiment the specific acknowledgments correlated with feeling heard at **r = 0.10 to 0.15** and reassurance at 0.02, in West's recorded conversations validation statements predicted the partner's felt connection about **1.5 times as strongly as follow-up questions** and were four times as frequent, and in Kumar's coaching study the corrections that moved people most were "validate", "stop advising" and "ask open questions" ([Yin SI Table S6](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2319112121/suppl_file/pnas.2319112121.sapp.pdf); [West 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00342-2); [Kumar 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)). The trap: listening builds the relationship and does not sell. In Santoro's 1,485-conversation field experiment, trained listening raised "understood and valued" by **0.79 standard deviations** and tripled how much people talked, yet added at most **0.11 SD** to persuasion five weeks later; in Hentona's 109 sales chats the share of seller lines that lowered the customer's willingness predicted failure (**r about −0.4 to −0.5**) while the share that raised it predicted almost nothing, and buyers wrote fewer turns than non-buyers ([Santoro 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11873821/); [Hentona data](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)). So the engine should score rapport moves and selling moves on separate tracks, read from each of his turns whether her last line moved three separate wishes (keep talking, tell her things, buy), treat any line that lowered the wish to buy as a brake, and gate offers on that late readiness window rather than on message counts. The AI label does not undo this: it cut felt connection by **0.94 on a 7-point scale but accuracy by only 0.31**, and a warm chatbot that every participant knew was a bot still raised closeness by about **d = 0.6** in a re-analysis of the authors' data, so understanding is the persona's safe ground and declared closeness its weak one ([Yin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/); [Telari Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)). Names and callbacks help inside the conversation and backfire glued to the ask (in the Monash experiments a name used at random points raised upsell acceptance, **d = 0.36** over no name, while the same name placed beside each upsell gave no gain over no name at all), confusion is fixed with one plain restatement and no apology, and direct questions and repairs deserve the short end of the 1–4 minute delay ([ScienceSays summary](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/when-and-how-to-call-customers-by-name); [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759); [Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134)). Of the roughly thirty proposals below, the engine already has the hard-mode rule, the name and pet-name caps, the confusion-versus-pushback split, the "share connects" rule, the readiness check and the decline and big-spend pauses; the earlier round already proposed responsiveness-first replies and separate "replied" and "bought" outcomes; the genuinely new and cheap items are the three-wish read with negatives as the brake, an acknowledgment ranking in hard mode with a permission gate for advice, relevance-based question and validation labels in place of the count band, keeping names and callbacks out of the offer line, and a correction handler plus a fast lane for repairs. Nothing here is implemented. Four of the five sources the researchers could not open (Moore 2024, Zobair 2026, Raja 2026 and Telari's supplement) were read afterwards in the manager's own Chrome; what they added or corrected is in the addendum before the conclusion, and only Balducci 2026 remains abstract-only.

## Feeling heard comes from specific acknowledgment, not from question counts

West's two preregistered studies recorded strangers talking and counted two listening behaviours per 30-second bin: follow-up questions, defined as questions that seek "to learn more, better understand, or clarify something the speaker said", and verbal validation, defined as "expressing understanding, paraphrasing, or reflecting information the listeners heard, or ... offering support or a friendly opinion on something the speaker said". The coders deliberately excluded backchannels ("yeah", "mhmm"), bare agreement ("I agree", "same", "me too", which the authors read as "a plausible attempt to shift the conversation back to oneself"), and any question that changed the subject; reliability was high (ICC 0.97 for questions, 0.94 for validation) ([West 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00342-2); [West peer review](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00342-2/MediaObjects/44271_2025_342_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)). In the 10-minute disclosure task (300 students with a trained partner), validation predicted the partner's reported sense of connection with **beta = 0.33** and follow-up questions with beta = 0.22; both sped up turn-taking on both sides; neither changed the listener's own sense of connection ([West PMC Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12638239/)). In the 5-minute staged small talk (348 working adults), validation still predicted connection (beta 0.26 by the partner, 0.20 by observers) but follow-up questions did not (0.06 and −0.02, both non-significant); the authors think small-talk questions "were sometimes offered out of politeness or as a way to combat potentially sitting in awkward silence" ([West 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00342-2)). Validation was about four times as frequent as questions in both studies (roughly 7 validations and 2 questions per 10 minutes), a 48-hour "connect with strangers" instruction raised questions by 23% (1.45 to 2.30 per 10 minutes) with no effect on validation, and the mediation through questions was tiny (indirect beta 0.04); the senior reviewer called the effects "TINY" and inconsistent across the two studies, and the authors accepted that effect sizes were small ([West PMC Tables 1 and 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12638239/); [West peer review](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00342-2/MediaObjects/44271_2025_342_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)). What the transcripts of good listeners looked like is the practical part: they "often recalled specific details shared by their partners and referenced earlier content to progress the conversation with follow-up questions (e.g., 'What were you thinking when the professor made that comment?')" ([West 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00342-2)).

Yin's experiment says which written acknowledgments do the work. 455 Prolific adults described a real difficult situation and received one written reply, either from a paid stranger or from Bing Chat (GPT-4), length-matched to the human median of about 55 words; the AI-written replies made people feel more heard (**5.74 vs 5.17 on 7**), and a separate group of 1,449 raters coded what each reply contained ([Yin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/)). Across all replies, the features that correlated with the recipient actually feeling heard were affirming a desired identity or quality (r = 0.15), agreeing or taking their side (0.14), acknowledging feelings as justified (0.13), voicing understanding (0.12), exclamations of interest (0.12), explaining why the event matters (0.11), repeating back key phrases (0.10), offering support (0.10) and acknowledging effort (0.09); emotional support as a whole correlated at 0.19 while practical support did not (−0.02), and neither did insight (0.01), reassurance that things will work out (0.02), the responder's own experiences (0.04), or the generic "I'm sorry that happened" (0.07, not significant) ([Yin SI Table S6](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2319112121/suppl_file/pnas.2319112121.sapp.pdf)). These are weak correlations across single replies coded by third parties, and the authors present them as "an intriguing mechanism" rather than a causal test; but their direction is consistent with West and with Kumar, and the authors' own conclusion is that humans "could benefit from practicing self-discipline to temper their tendency to offer advice and instead offer more emotional support" ([Yin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/)). Nobody in Yin's study asked for advice, so the paper cannot say what happens when a fan wants practical help; the defensible reading is that advice does not make someone feel heard, not that advice is never wanted.

Kumar's paper supplies the operational rubric. 968 US adults each held three 4-minute text chats with a GPT-4o role-player sharing a trouble (job loss, a mother's cancer diagnosis, a father's death), and an LLM judge scored every conversation on six preregistered 1–5 questions: validating emotions, encouraging elaboration with open questions, demonstrating understanding by paraphrase, and the three faults of unsolicited advice, self-oriented responding and dismissing emotions ([Kumar 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2); [Kumar preregistration](https://aspredicted.org/hdjx-tdsr.pdf)). A two-round personalised coach raised the composite by **0.98 SD** (1.26 SD with a short video added); 21.6% of coached people improved reliably against 2.9% of controls; the coach's corrections were, by frequency, validate (32.7%), stop giving advice (24.2%), ask open questions (21.2%) and paraphrase (11.2%); and the gains came with no change in turns, words or response time, "quality rather than quantity" ([Kumar 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)). The taxonomy of 16,975 human messages shows what each label catches in practice: "stay strong", "take it one day at a time", "lean on others" and "you need to" were coded as advice; "don't worry", "it will take time", "we all", speculative "maybe" explanations and "setbacks do not define a person" as dismissing; mentioning one's own similar experience as self-oriented, which was rare (1% of tags) and concentrated in the worst-rated tenth of conversations (3.6%) and absent from the best (0.1%) ([Kumar Tables 5, 12 and 16](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)). The judge's agreement with experts was good for elaboration (weighted kappa 0.86), self-oriented (0.69), advice (0.66) and validation (0.63), middling for understanding (0.59) and poor for dismissing (0.17, where even experts agreed only 0.35–0.51 with each other), and independent raters preferred the higher-scored conversation **73% of the time at a 5-point gap** and 93% at 10 points ([Kumar rubric paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10150v2); [Kumar 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)). Two warnings come with the rubric. The authors explicitly exclude intimate relationships ("thick empathy" with partners, family and close friends) from their claims, and four frontier models run as supporters sat at the rubric ceiling with **half the novelty of humans** and a validation-sentence share of 24–31% against 9–19% for people, so an AI persona's risk is not too little validation but the same-shaped validation every time ([Kumar Appendices I–K](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15245v2)).

What this maps to in the engine is mostly refinement of a rule that already exists. The "he is sharing something hard" mode already tells the writer to reflect his own words, name the feeling, tell no story of her own and give no advice he did not ask for, it switches the share move off, and it still allows one check-in question **[engine has it]**; that rule matches the Kumar rubric almost one to one. The new material is a ranking inside that mode: prefer the acknowledgments that correlated with feeling heard (his feeling is justified given what happened, the effort he has put in, a quality of his the situation shows, taking his side, repeating one of his phrases) and demote the two moves that did not correlate, reassurance ("it'll be fine", "don't worry", "stay strong", which Kumar's coders filed under dismissing or advice) and the generic "I'm so sorry", to optional closers **[new]**. A permission gate copies the coach's own rule: in hard mode a suggestion is allowed only after she has asked whether he wants ideas or just her listening **[new]**. The comfort and reassure moves should be split along that line, since "it's okay to feel like that" is validation and "it'll all work out" is dismissal in the only rubric that has been validated **[new]**. For the coach, two labels can replace the blunt instrument of counting: a "relevant follow-up question" label using West's definition (asks to learn more about, understand or clarify something he said, or how he feels about it, and stays on his topic) and a "validation before share" label with West's exclusions (a bare "haha", "omg", "same" or "me too" does not count) **[new; refines the engine's "understood" dimension and its "a share connects, no echo" rule]**. The engine's current band, which flags fewer than 30% or more than 45% questions over the last ten replies, has neither support nor refutation in these papers, because they counted questions per minute of speech rather than per turn; what they do say is that relevance, not volume, carried the effect, and that in small-talk stretches a question that does not pick up something he said risks reading as filler, so the value of the curious-question move should drop in stretches without disclosure and rise right after he has given something to follow up on **[new]**. Two scorecard bands follow directly: the share of her replies after a substantive fan message that contain either a relevant question or a validation, and the share of validations that name a specific detail of his; West's 4:1 validation-to-question ratio is a plausible human band to check against, not a target **[new]**. Three further items are cheap and consistent with the evidence: Yin's AI advantage came at a matched 55 words, so the coach should not reward longer replies as better listening **[round 1 already set a seven-word median]**; soft hedges ("maybe", "a bit", "it sounds like") and "I hear that" count as positive regard when he states an opinion she does not share, which Santoro's training sheet shows can be done without agreeing ([Santoro SI Fig. S4](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2421982122/suppl_file/pnas.2421982122.sapp.pdf)) **[round 1 proposed hedges only for tense turns; the ordinary-disagreement case is new]**; and an anti-template check over her last ten replies for repeated validation stems and repeated shapes (sympathy, paraphrase, question), aiming at the human top-decile novelty range rather than the model range **[new]**. What the papers show is correlation in strangers' spoken talk and single written replies; that the same acknowledgments carry the effect in a weeks-long paid chat is extrapolation.

## The AI label caps closeness, not understanding

Yin's second factor was the label. Telling people the reply came from "Bing Chat, an AI chatbot" rather than another participant lowered feeling heard by **0.68**, understanding by 0.71, accuracy by only 0.31 and connection by **0.94** on the 7-point scale, and the label effect did not interact with the true source or with any of the 16 coded features (all p > 0.13): the penalty is a flat subtraction that leaves the ranking of good and bad replies untouched ([Yin Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/); [Yin SI Tables S1 and S9](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2319112121/suppl_file/pnas.2319112121.sapp.pdf)). Even under the AI label an AI-written reply scored 5.41 on feeling heard, equal to a labelled human stranger's 5.51; the penalty was driven by attitude toward AI (people with clearly positive attitudes showed none) and the connection penalty by whether the reader granted the AI a mind ([Yin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/); [Yin SI Tables S3–S4](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2319112121/suppl_file/pnas.2319112121.sapp.pdf)). The earlier round reached the same conclusion from nine studies with 6,282 people in which the label fell on "feeling with" and "caring about" but not on "understanding", and replies written to stress understanding showed no penalty at all ([Rubin et al. 2025](https://en.psychology.huji.ac.il/news/comparing-value-perceived-humanversus-ai-generated-empathy); [earlier report](Yuna%20engine%20next%20improvements.md)).

Telari's two studies are the only ones in the set where everyone knew they were talking to a chatbot, which is Yuna's situation. The abstract reports that a "relational" response style (warm, empathetic) raised perceived human-likeness, empathy and closeness over both a default and a "non-relational" bot, and that in Study 2 deeper Fast-Friends topics raised self-disclosure, which raised perceived responsiveness, which raised closeness ([Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/02654075261438164)). The paper itself could not be opened, so the numbers are the researcher's own re-analysis of the authors' public data: Study 1 (N = 163) closeness 2.98 for the relational bot against 2.12 default and 1.84 non-relational (d = 0.56 and 0.72), perceived empathy "response" subscale d = 0.93 to 1.14; Study 2 (N = 158) perceived responsiveness **4.29 vs 3.00 (d = 0.82)**, closeness 2.45 vs 1.71 (d = 0.60), and people stayed on the chat page 749 seconds with the warm bot against 438 with the cold one ([Telari Study 1 data](https://osf.io/download/2uahw/); [Telari Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/)). The mediation in the re-analysis is the useful mechanism: once perceived responsiveness is in the model, the warm style has no direct effect on closeness (indirect 0.55 with a confidence interval of 0.30 to 0.88; direct 0.18, not significant), while topic depth moved closeness only through the chain depth → his disclosure → responsiveness → closeness (0.14) and had no direct effect at all (d = 0.06) ([Telari Study 2 data](https://osf.io/download/trx8p/); [Telari Study 2 script](https://osf.io/download/cwerh/)). In plain terms, warmth works only insofar as he ends up feeling understood, validated and cared for, and deep questions pay off only through what she does with his answer. The first author's own summary to the press was that "when we share something personal and feel understood, validated, and cared for, we develop a sense of connection" ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-pinpoint-the-conversational-mechanisms-that-help-humans-bond-with-ai/)). The caveats are real: the relational prompt text, the item wording and whether the published model is serial or parallel are all unknown until someone reads the paper, and the sessions were eight to ten minutes with young Italians.

Kleinert's experiments show what an AI partner's own words did, sentence by sentence, because every partner answer is published. 322 German students did a 15-minute text Fast Friends exchange with pre-written answers from either real students or six PaLM 2 "students" with fixed biographies; in deep talk the AI answers, presented as human, produced more closeness (**4.48 vs 3.85** on the Inclusion of Other in the Self scale, a small effect), and the only measured content difference was self-disclosure density: the same length (314 vs 296 words over the set) but more I-words, feeling words and people words (LIWC 38.0 vs 32.3), with partner disclosure predicting closeness at partial r = 0.24 ([Kleinert full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)). The published answers show the shape: "I last cried in front of someone when I told my mom about my breakup. It was a very emotional moment, and I couldn't hold back my feelings" (AI) against "I generally don't cry in front of other people, but I think the last time was about six or seven months ago" (human); every AI answer was a direct first-person answer with a reason, a named person and a feeling, and the six characters never contradicted their biographies across eleven items ([Kleinert supplement](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44271-025-00391-7/MediaObjects/44271_2025_391_MOESM2_ESM.pdf)). In Study 2 (N = 334) the label mattered but did not stop the bond: closeness 4.17 under a human label against 3.72 under an AI label (about d = 0.25), closeness still rose by about **one point under the AI label** against 1.21 under the human one, and the one behavioural cost was effort, with participants writing **8% shorter messages** to a labelled AI (181.8 vs 198.4 words) while message length correlated with closeness at r = 0.26 ([Kleinert full text](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12876889)).

The mapping starts with what the engine already does. The warmth dial keeps declared feelings rare and the earlier round argued that care should be shown through memory and follow-through rather than announced, which is exactly what the label data support: the fan discounts claims about her inner life, not her accuracy about his **[engine has it; round 1 proposed it]**. The share move already requires "a small real thing of yours that connects to what he just said, not your day out of nowhere" and is switched off in hard mode **[engine has it]**; Kleinert and Kumar together add the constraints that make it work, which is why it misfires as tangents today: it should trigger only after he has answered a get-to-know-you prompt or volunteered something personal in a non-hard moment, stay on his topic, run two to four sentences, carry one named person and one feeling or reason, never run longer than his message, and come from a fixed fact sheet, because the AI characters' edge was consistency rather than truth (the biographies were fiction and never contradicted themselves) **[refines what the engine has]**. The engine's diary of her self-facts and the coach's "nothing invented, nothing contradicted" check already point this way; the plan's allowance for inventing "the small detail" of a moment is compatible with Kleinert as long as every detail is recorded and never contradicted later. Three measurement items are new. A responsiveness triad in the coach (understood: reflects his content or mirrors a fragment; validated: says his reaction makes sense and names the feeling; cared for: shows availability or interest in him specifically) would make Telari's mediator the master check instead of the looser "answers him" and "understood" pair **[refines what the engine has]**. Label-aware targets would expect "understood" and "answers him" to reach human-chatter bands while setting separate, lower bands for the fan's declared closeness and trust, which are the outcomes the label hits hardest, so a gap there is not read as a dialogue failure **[new]**. And his average words per message is a usable engagement meter, because a drop is the one behavioural signature Kleinert found under the AI label and length tracked closeness **[new; round 1 measured a 10+-word reply share, so this is a refinement]**. Two further ideas are speculative: a reader tag for how the fan relates to her being an AI (mocking, neutral, curious, accepting), since attitude toward AI was Yin's strongest moderator **[new]**, and a small bank of Fast Friends-style prompts (proud of, grateful for, a dream not yet done, a treasured memory) gated by rapport and paired with an obligation that the reply after his answer scores high on the triad before any sale, since in both papers depth paid off only through the reply **[new; related to round 1's one-notch-deeper pacing]**. Availability lines ("I'm here, tell me everything") stay allowed in hard mode, which Kumar's data class as affective support rather than declared feeling **[round 1 proposed it]**. What these papers show is single sessions of four to fifteen minutes with students and no romance; the weeks-long paid relationship is extrapolation, and nothing here speaks to whether the label penalty fades or hardens with repeated contact.

## Listening wins the relationship; a separate, late signal wins the sale

Santoro's experiment is the direct test of whether being listened to makes people say yes. Eight former professional canvassers held 1,485 ten-minute Zoom conversations with Americans who opposed in-state tuition for undocumented immigrants, in a 2 × 2 of non-judgmental listening (at least three "tell me more" inquiries, nods, "I hear that", paraphrase, no agreeing and no arguing) and a persuasive personal story; 92% were re-surveyed five weeks later ([Santoro 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11873821/); [Santoro SI](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2421982122/suppl_file/pnas.2421982122.sapp.pdf)). Listening did everything a relationship engine would want: "my conversation partner understood me and made me feel like my opinions were valued" rose by **0.79 SD**, mutual respect by 0.28, liking by 0.19, defensiveness fell by 0.23, and participants said roughly **three times as many words** (996–1,010 against 307–343) in conversations that ran twice as long ([Santoro Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11873821/); [Santoro SI Table S9](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2421982122/suppl_file/pnas.2421982122.sapp.pdf)). It did nothing for persuasion: the story alone moved attitudes by about 0.25–0.33 SD immediately and 0.2 SD at five weeks, listening added d = 0.06–0.07 immediately and −0.02 at five weeks, with the long-term confidence interval topping out at 0.11 SD, and all 17 confederates and research assistants had predicted the opposite ([Santoro Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11873821/)). The authors note that three earlier text-chat studies found the same split (better perceptions of the counterpart, no attitude change), that the appeal was the same regardless of what was heard because confederates were forbidden to tailor it, and that "listen to understand a counterpart's needs or concerns, and then in turn tailor the persuasive message" is the untested route ([Santoro 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11873821/)). For Yuna this is a direct warning against any scoring model in which "he feels heard" is treated as a step toward "he buys"; listening is a reliable lever on how much he talks and how he feels about her, and the content of the offer is what carries the sale.

Hentona's dataset says what a readiness signal looks like when it is read from the customer rather than counted. Five trained human sellers, posing as a "SalesBot", sold fictional earphones to 109 Japanese crowd workers in text; after each chat the user rated every seller message on three separate questions, whether it changed "even a little" their wish to keep talking, to give information, and to buy, each as positive, no effect or negative, and gave a 7-point purchase intention before and after ([Hentona PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf); [Hentona data](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)). Negatives were rare (5.6% of seller lines on keep-talking, 3.1% on information, 6.1% on buying) and were the only thing that mattered: the paper reports that the share of positive and neutral labels barely correlated with the intention gain while the share of negatives correlated negatively, and the re-analysis puts those at **r = −0.47, −0.47 and −0.38 for negatives against +0.11 to +0.17 for positives**; the authors' conclusion is that avoiding willingness-lowering lines matters more than producing willingness-raising ones ([Hentona PDF §5.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf); [Hentona data, own computation](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)). The three wishes really are separate: a third of the lines that made users want to keep talking did nothing for their wish to buy, and one line in twenty raised the wish to buy without touching the wish to talk; buyers wrote fewer turns than non-buyers (9.5 vs 11.2) and asked fewer questions, their wish to buy first turned positive later in the chat (median 0.24 of the dialogue against 0.08), and a buying-negative line in the final third coincided with a purchase rate of **30% against 49%** without one ([Hentona data, own computation](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)). The researcher's keyword classes over the seller lines, descriptive rather than causal, show which lines carried the risk: questions about the person's life and use situation were almost never negative (1–4%), budget questions were the riskiest questions (14%), recommendations drew 11% buying-negatives, unprompted drawbacks ("unfortunately even the Pro can fall out") 27%, soft "how about...?" checks none in 21, and bare "Thank you" or "Understood" turns moved nothing ([Hentona data, own computation](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)). A stated yes was not a click: 15 of 62 non-buyers wrote a decision sentence and never pressed buy ([Hentona data, own computation](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json)). The authors' own system test is modest but telling: a GPT-3.5 fine-tuned with the labels and a fixed schedule (turns 1–3 keep him talking, 4–6 draw him out, from turn 7 close) raised the success rate from 0.23 to **0.44** in a 48-person test against 0.33 for "always positive on everything", while untuned GPT-4o reached 0.58 in fewer turns, and the stated next step is to switch the target wish dynamically from the user's responses rather than by turn number; one leaked training label ("the willingness to continue dialogue is increasing") appeared in a seller line, a warning for any generator trained on attribute tags ([Hentona Table 3 and §7.3](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.742.pdf)).

Balducci's abstract adds the same architecture from a different field and nothing more. In 783 B2B lead-calling conversations (11,566 speaking turns), salesperson acts (legitimizing, piquing, nudging, plus intensity, speech rate and pitch) act on "lead receptivity", a per-turn measure of the lead's openness, which drives conversion: one standard deviation of receptivity raised conversion likelihood by **13.44%**, more specific nudges raised receptivity 11.82% early in the call and lowered it 81.40% late, and faster speech was neutral early and −8.26% late ([Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437261481444)). The coding manual, the receptivity measure, the early/late boundary and the definition of conversion (in lead calling, usually a booked next step rather than a payment) are all unknown, and the −81.40% is a relative change in an unspecified model quantity, so the only transferable claim is structural: receptivity is read from the other party's turn, and the same act can flip sign depending on when it is used.

The engine already gates selling with several of the right rules: the readiness check ("are you alone?", a tease of what she has) comes before an offer on a planned attempt, readiness drops after a big spend, there is no second attempt after a decline in the same conversation, no selling to a sad or doubting man, and the agency's own 35k-chat finding that the check before an offer sold twice as well is consistent with Hentona's structure (question-shaped lines almost never lowered willingness, recommendations sometimes did) **[engine has it]**. What is new is where the readiness number comes from. Today readiness moves on his asks, buys, mood and session flags, and the first-sale path and the planned check are triggered by step counts and by 60% of the free-talk budget being used; the papers argue for reading three per-turn effects from his message instead, each as positive, no change or negative: did her last line make him keep talking (reply length, a question back, a new topic), tell her things (specifics, preferences, where he is, what he wants to see), and want what she sells (asks what she has, the price, when; says show me; names a thing; answers a tease with a request) **[new]**. Only the third read should feed readiness; the first two are the existing engagement and rapport meters, which currently conflate disclosure with readiness **[new]**. The brake follows from the only robust statistic in Hentona: if either of her last two lines read negative on the wish to buy, or two of the last three read negative on anything, no steer and no offer in this conversation, and the planner switches to a curious question about him, comfort or a connecting share **[new]**. The gate then becomes state rather than count: an offer is allowed when the last two or three reads show the wish to buy positive at least twice with no negative, and the hard rules stay on top **[new; replaces the step counts and the 60% trigger]**. The readiness check stays and becomes the probe for that third read, with his answer taken as the label (a yes, a when or a how much means offer now; a deflection means one more get-to-know or share turn and a re-check next conversation; a no, a not now or a "broke" closes selling for the conversation) **[refines what the engine has]**. Two content rules fall out of the keyword classes: early curious questions stay about his life, mood and tastes and never about money or what he would pay, with price appearing once, inside the offer **[new]**, and disclaimers about what she does not do are answered only when asked and always paired with what she does have, because unprompted drawbacks were the single most damaging line type **[new; the engine's "say so once, then name the closest item" rule is close]**. Bare acknowledgements were inert in Hentona, which supports the existing rule that a react carries one connecting detail or a door **[engine has it]**. Santoro's null argues for separate outcome tracks in the coach and scorecard: listening moves (react, curious question, get to know him, comfort) are scored against rapport, understood, engagement and doubt, offer-type moves (steer, offer, readiness check) against readiness to pay, with no automatic transfer from the first group to the second, and the offer's content judged on its own quality (specific, about him, carrying something new), because in Santoro the content did the persuading **[new; round 1's separate "replied" and "bought" reward estimates are the learning-side version of the same idea]**. The practice room can copy Hentona's annotation protocol almost verbatim for English test chats: the person playing the fan rates every Yuna message on the three "did this line change, even a little, your wish to..." questions, gives a 7-point "do you want to buy something from her right now?" before and after, success is recorded as after-above-before separately from any click, and any Yuna line whose intent two readers find unclear is flagged **[new; fits round 1's measurement stack]**. Balducci's early-versus-late reversal yields only a hypothesis for the practice room: whether a concrete early door ("I'll show you something later if you're good") beats a vague one and whether late concreteness (price repeated, a deadline) hurts after he has shown receptivity; the percentages must not be imported **[new, weak]**. What is shown: negatives predict failure, the wish to buy is a separate and late-rising read, and listening does not persuade. What is extrapolation: that a model can read the three labels from a fan's turn as reliably as the users rated them, and that gating on a late readiness window raises sales on Fanvue rather than in a one-off Japanese earphone chat.

## A name beside the ask erases its gain

Raja, Cheng and Danaher's abstract states the duality plainly: addressing customers by name "can enhance customer satisfaction and drive upselling effectiveness" and "can diminish satisfaction when overused, misused, or applied in embarrassing contexts", with rapport explaining the gains and discomfort the losses ([OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2026.03.008)). The numbers come from two secondhand sources that read the paper and agree with each other, not from the paper itself. In a 2022 field experiment at a suburban Australian restaurant (127 tables), name use raised spend per person **17.3%** ($28.67 to $33.64), upsell acceptance from **22.6% to 48.4%** and satisfaction 15.9%, and the effect was stronger when the employee introduced themselves by name first; in the online experiments a name used at a random point in the conversation raised upsell acceptance 17.2% while the same name placed "immediately alongside the upsell offer" had no effect, a name in "every other sentence" raised satisfaction 9.1% while a name in every sentence cancelled the gain and caused discomfort, and a name used while the customer was buying haemorrhoid cream or STD medication reversed or cancelled the benefit ([ScienceSays summary](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/when-and-how-to-call-customers-by-name)). The summary's own caveats are that effects on repeat customers are unknown, online support chats were not tested directly, and first names were not separated from titled family names ([ScienceSays summary](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/when-and-how-to-call-customers-by-name)). A 2026 chatbot study that cites the paper found across five experiments that name-based personalization in chatbot messages raised perceived sincerity and purchase intentions through anthropomorphism, bounded by "perceived overuse", but it measured intentions rather than purchases and its details were unread ([Lee et al. 2026 record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1002/mar.70251?fields=title,abstract,authors,year,venue,tldr,openAccessPdf)).

Zobair's field experiment is abstract-only. 46 interviews and a randomized experiment with 409 US retail customers whose purchases were read from transaction data found that a "personalized" AI recommendation message raised purchase likelihood relative to a "humorous" one, through stronger attributions of helping intent, "partially offset by heightened perceptions of intrusiveness"; shopping urgency amplified both paths, and a stronger customer–retailer relationship "enhance[d] helpfulness attribution and attenuate[d] intrusiveness concerns" ([OpenAlex abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.104844?select=title,abstract_inverted_index,publication_date,biblio)). There is no neutral baseline in the abstract, so "personalized beat humorous" says nothing about whether personalization beats a plain message or whether humour is below one, and the message wording, the channel, the scales and every effect size are unknown until the PDF is opened. The mechanism is still worth having: the same message that reads as "she knows what fits me" also reads as "she knows things about me", and the relationship is what tips the balance toward care.

The earlier round already carried the two closest findings: servers who introduced themselves by name got tips of 23.4% of the bill against 15.0%, and flattery with an obvious motive still leaves a favourable implicit attitude but is a trust risk right before an ask, which became the rule that compliments are warmth and never set-ups ([Garrity & Degelman 1990](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277974500_The_Effect_of_Mutual_Introductions_and_Addressing_Customers_by_Name_on_Tipping_Behavior_in_Restaurants); [Chan & Sengupta 2010](https://www.ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-even-phony-flattery-helps-close-deals/); [earlier report](Yuna%20engine%20next%20improvements.md)). The engine's name rule already leaves his name out when she used it recently, caps pet names at one in five replies with none in her first three replies to a fan she barely knows and none when one of her last two replies carried one **[engine has it]**; the frequency findings give no reason to loosen that, since consecutive use is the tested failure case and whether even rarer use is better is untested, and the ceiling should be kept as a ceiling **[engine has it]**. The placement result is the one genuinely new rule: no name in the message that teases or prices a PPV piece and none in the message right after a decline, because by the summarised mechanism a name "used to sell" breaks rapport and a name beside the ask added nothing to acceptance **[new]**. By analogy only, the same holds for remembered details: a callback that appears only when she is about to sell will, over several conversations, be learned as the pitch cue, so the memory layer should surface callbacks in non-selling turns at least as often as in selling turns, and a callback belongs a turn or two before a tease rather than glued to it as the reason to buy **[new; extrapolated from names to memory]**. Zobair's mechanism and a neighbouring privacy finding that activated privacy concern wiped out the advantage of identifying personalization over a generic message argue that any "fits you" line should be justified only with things he said in chat, in his words ("you said you were into..."), never with purchase records or patterns he did not voice ("I noticed you always buy...") ([PMC12561546 via search snippet](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12561546/)) **[new; round 1's "never guess, ask" memory rule is adjacent]**. Tying the depth of personalization to the engine's closeness levels (a new fan gets only the current conversation; friendly and flirty fans get callbacks to earlier talks; intimate and girlfriend-level fans get taste-based picks framed as knowing him) maps Zobair's brand-relationship moderator onto interpersonal closeness, a different construct, so it is a proposal and not a finding **[new]**. Three further items are weak: skipping his name for a turn or two after he discloses something he flagged as private, after a money-related decline or while he apologises transfers the pharmacy-counter result from a public setting to a private DM where the exposure that drove it is absent **[new, weak]**; preferring a fit-framed tease ("the one you asked for") over a joke-wrapped one rests on an abstract with no neutral baseline and says nothing against a funny persona **[new, weak]**; pairing a named address with a small self-disclosure of her own stretches the mutual-introduction result, since fans already know her name **[new, loose]**. Pet names get no evidence either way: the rapport mechanism is being recognised as an individual, which "babe" does not do, so only the failure modes (scripted repetition, placement beside the price) plausibly transfer **[engine's cap stands, uncontradicted]**. The cheapest next step is to run the restaurant tests on Yuna's own logs: PPV acceptance and next-turn sentiment for offers with and without his name in the offer message, with a name in the preceding three turns versus none, and with a callback inside the offer versus two turns earlier **[new]**. What is shown: spoken name use by restaurant and pharmacy staff in one-off encounters, as summarised secondhand, and an unquantified helpful-versus-intrusive trade-off in a retail AI message. What is extrapolation: every chat rule above, with the caveat that typed repetition is more visible than spoken, so the overuse threshold in text may be lower than "every sentence".

## Fix confusion in one plain line and put questions on the fast lane

Moore, An and Marrese's paper is the right frame for the bug the brief described (a fan's "what do you mean?" being handled as pushback), but only its abstract was reachable: conversational interfaces let the agent ask the user to rephrase and rarely let the user repair the agent's turn; Moore and Arar's earlier framework demonstrated nine types of user-initiated repair on agent responses that people use correctly in text when the interface supports them; this small study tested them in a voice interface and found understanding-oriented repairs worked the same way in text and voice while hearing- and speaking-oriented ones were voice-specific ([IBM Research page](https://research.ibm.com/publications/understanding-is-a-two-way-street-user-initiated-repair-on-agent-responses-and-hearing-in-conversational-interfaces)). The nine types and the ideal agent response per type live in the body, which the ACM bot check blocked; the paper is CC-BY, so a human opening [the DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3641026) in a normal browser gets the taxonomy that none of the rules below should be attributed to until then.

The conversation-analysis corpora fill the gap on how people actually repair. Across twelve languages and 2,053 cases, a listener signals trouble about once every 1.4 minutes in one of three formats (an open "huh?", a restricted request that names the part at issue, or a candidate understanding offered for confirmation), people pick the most specific format they can, and the fix is almost always done in one turn and kept about as long as the muddled line (**1.2 times** the trouble source) ([Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759)). In an English corpus of 227 cases, 81.5% of initiations located a specific trouble, "what do you mean?" occurred four times and "I don't understand" once and only as a last resort; "why?" is a request for a reason, not a repair ([Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)). The same forms also do challenge, tease and disbelief, and the usable cues are whether the added words quote her turn (repair) or supply grounds against it (challenge: "why finally? I just subscribed"), whether her line was plainly understandable, whether it was an assessment or tease that invites a stance, and what his next turn does after she fixes it: when the recipient confirms or corrects, it was repair; when they defend or reaffirm instead, it was disagreement in repair's clothing ([Schegloff 1997](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf); [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)). The solution to "what do you mean?" in the corpora is a plainer restatement or the missing specific ("I mean..."), after which the original thread resumes at once, while fixes that introduce new unexplained material cascade into further repairs, and open "huh?" after an abrupt topic shift signals that her line did not fit what came before rather than that it was unheard ([Schegloff 1997](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/practices-and-actions-boundary-cases-of-other-initiated-repair.pdf); [Drew 1997](https://emcawiki.net/Drew1997)). A fact correction from him ("I'm from Leeds, not Leicester") gets the corrected item repeated once and the conversation moves on; no apology is modelled anywhere in the data ([Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)).

Jiang's pacing study is the only one of the twelve that varied reply timing, and it varied more than timing. Fifty East Asian participants (62% aged 18–24) each held two ten-minute supportive role-plays with a GPT-4o agent that either answered at once under a generic prompt or chose one of eight strategies per turn, inserting 0.5 to 16 seconds of silence with strategy-specific status text ("reflecting quietly") and strategy-specific content instructions; the paced agent was rated more human-like (rank-biserial r = 0.36 and 0.30 across the two scenarios), smoother and more interactive (0.48 and 0.43), but better at listening (0.34) and higher in affective trust (0.32) only in the career scenario, and a notable minority read the slowness as malfunction or low effort despite the status bar ([Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134)). Because the control differed in content and interface cues as well as timing, the safe reading is "deliberate pauses plus listening-shaped content, signposted on screen" beat "instant generic support", not "delay helps". Its one clear timing rule is that direct information requests get an immediate answer (the zero-delay strategy covered about 22% of turns), its design advice is that a pause is a promissory note (a long wait followed by a thin reply hurts trust more than an instant thin reply), and its state rule is that a holding state after distress should persist through a minor factual question rather than oscillating ([Jiang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06134)). Nothing in it covers minute-scale delays, asynchronous messaging, sexting or a persona with its own life, and frustration was never measured.

The engine is further along here than the brief implied. It already has two handlers: a confusion handler that recognises "what do you mean?" and reader hints that he is asking her to explain, tells the writer to say plainly what she meant with no question back, and drops the curious question for that reply; and a pushback handler that recognises grounds-bearing forms ("why finally? I just subscribed") and has her own it lightly in one line with no excuse **[engine has it]**. Direct questions already get "answer it first" **[engine has it]**, and the earlier round's refusal rule already strips "sorry" from refusals on the strength of the finding that an apology inside a rejection makes it more hurtful (d = 0.35) ([Freedman et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554531/); [earlier report](Yuna%20engine%20next%20improvements.md)) **[round 1 proposed it]**. The new material is a third handler and sharper cues. A correction handler accepts the fact, repeats the corrected item once in passing, continues the thread and writes the corrected fact into his memory so it is never repeated **[new; round 1's valid-from and valid-until memory fields are the storage side]**. The confusion handler gains the conservation rules: one line, about the length of the muddled line, plainer words or the missing specific, an optional "I mean", never the identical line repeated (that is the hearing fix, useless in text), no "sorry" and no "I phrased that badly", no new metaphor, then straight back to his pending question or her share **[refines what the engine has]**. The classifier between the handlers gains the CA cues (added words that quote her line versus words that argue against it; whether her line was ambiguous; whether it was a share or a verdict on him; caps, exclamation marks and doubled question marks as the text analogue of the challenging "what?"), and when the reader cannot tell, the default is to fix the understanding problem in one plain line and let his next turn reveal whether a challenge follows, because people give the benefit of the doubt and answer the repair first **[refines what the engine has]**. When she is the one unsure of his meaning, the reader's low-confidence readings should become one candidate understanding ("you mean the ex, or the flatmate?") rather than an open "what do you mean?", since the specific guess carries more of the work **[new; the reader's "don't assume" instruction is the current, looser version]**. On pacing the engine's first-message wait of one to four minutes (ten to thirty for a time waster) sits outside anything tested, so the study neither supports nor undermines it; what transfers is a speed exception: a direct question, a repair request or a correction gets the short end of the delay with the answer in the first bubble, a disclosure keeps the longer wait with the first bubble landing on the feeling, the slow state persists through a trivial question after a disclosure, and a long wait is never followed by a thin line **[new]**. The persona itself supplies the charitable reading of silence that Jiang's minimal counsellor lacked, which the authors flag as a likely moderator. For the practice room, five drafts modelled on the corpus extracts (a clarification that restates and resumes, a fact correction that repeats the corrected item, a tease that misfired where she drops the tease frame and says what she meant in plain words, an open "wait what" after a topic jump answered by supplying the link, and a single candidate understanding when she is unsure) plus a scorecard check "repair resolved in one turn without apology or cascade" are untested lines for review **[new]**. One tension with the earlier round deserves a note: round 1 allowed a one-clause apology plus an offer to redo when she actually misread him (a competence failure), while the corpora show no apology when he simply did not get her; the two reconcile as "apologise once when the error was hers, never when the trouble was his understanding". What is shown: how humans repair in speech and the direction of pacing effects in a ten-minute counselling role-play. What is extrapolation: every text-chat frequency, the 1.2× length rule applied to typed lines, and any claim about minute-scale delays or sexting pace.

What not to infer is as important as the list. These papers do not show that more questions mean more connection (relevant questions in disclosure moments and validation generally, and even then small), that better listening raises readiness to pay (the only causal test is null and three text-chat studies agree), that a known AI can offset the label by saying more emotional things (the penalty did not interact with any feature and the label added a little creepiness), that "it'll be okay" or "I'm so sorry" make him feel heard, that a stated yes is a sale (a quarter of Hentona's non-buyers said they had decided), that "pitch at turn 7" is a finding (it was a stand-in schedule the authors want to replace), that a 1–4 minute delay makes her seem attentive (untested either way), that "what do you mean?" is usually a challenge (repair is the default and the response decides), or that any of Balducci's percentages or Raja's restaurant numbers are effect sizes for chat ([West 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00342-2); [Santoro 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11873821/); [Yin SI Tables S9–S11](https://www.pnas.org/doi/suppl/10.1073/pnas.2319112121/suppl_file/pnas.2319112121.sapp.pdf); [Hentona data](https://github.com/CyberAgentAILab/salestalk-dataset/blob/main/data/japanese-salestalk-dataset_v1.json); [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)).

## Addendum: what the four papers read in Chrome added or corrected

**Moore 2024 (repair), now in full.** Two tiny studies (15 text, 16 voice participants, IBM staff) on an intent-based assistant, with obscure words planted to provoke repairs: 190 cases. The repair types people used on the agent's turn, with counts: an explicit definition request ("what is Chantilly?") 36, an indexical one ("what's that?") 13, an understanding check (repeating her word with a question mark) 4, an example request 10, a paraphrase request ("what do you mean", "I don't understand") 17, a repeat request 12 (voice only), a partial repeat plus question word 1. Every repair is a three-turn sequence: his initiator, her repair, then back to the original move: the agent defined "Chantilly" and re-offered it, and the user answered the real question. A paraphrase is the whole line in plainer words with fewer assumptions, and the authors allow it to be "verbose and inelegant" because understanding outranks brevity at that moment. The authors' own tests of language models found them confusing repeat with paraphrase and reaching for a paragraph when a sentence was asked for. For rank 5 this sharpens three things: classify his initiator by type and answer in kind (the term, the referent of "that", the whole line again, one example, or the same words), tell the writer which type it is, and resume her move after the repair. It also names her own trouble sources: a dangling "that" or "some of that", an in-joke he was not part of, slang he may not know, which a pre-send check could catch ([Moore 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3641026)).

**Zobair 2026 (personalization), now in full.** 46 intercept interviews and a field experiment inside a US retailer's chat assistant, N = 409, purchases from transaction records (mean purchase rate 0.31). Personalized framing ("based on what you're looking for right now", first-party context only) against humorous framing of the same recommendation: odds of a purchase **1.52 times** (about +6 percentage points), order value +6.8% among buyers, stable over 3-, 7- and 14-day windows. The same framing raised perceived helpfulness (b 0.55) and intrusiveness (b 0.40) at once; helpfulness predicted buying (OR 1.78), intrusiveness cut it (OR 0.63); a stronger relationship with the retailer raised the first and lowered the second. The interviews put the mechanism in one line: "The joke is fine. What bothers me is when it acts like it knows me." There was no neutral baseline, so the lift is against humour, not against plain speech. For rank 4 this confirms the direction and adds the rule for callbacks: a "fits you" line cites what he said in this chat, never a pattern she noticed, and a new subscriber gets the lighter kind ([Zobair et al. 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0969698926001256)).

**Raja 2026 (names), now in full.** The secondhand numbers hold and the details are now firsthand: a restaurant field experiment over 127 tables with upsell acceptance at 22.6% without a name, 48.4% with the customer's name and **64.7% with a mutual exchange of names**; spend per head $28.67 to $33.64 to $35.73; satisfaction d = 0.72 for the customer's name and 1.01 for mutual. Frequency: seven mentions in a short scripted counter interaction (every other sentence) raised satisfaction d = 0.31 through rapport; fourteen (every sentence) gave back part of it through discomfort while rapport stayed up. Placement: the same number of mentions at random points raised upsell likelihood d = 0.36 over no name, and placed beside each upsell gave nothing over no name and felt more frequent than it was. Embarrassment needed an audience (a pharmacy counter within earshot); there the name added discomfort and no rapport. Two additions to rank 4: Yuna should give her own name early and ask his, since the mutual condition won every outcome, and his name should be skipped for a turn after a decline, an apology or a vulnerable disclosure, where self-consciousness is the live mechanism ([Raja et al. 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022435926000278)).

**Telari 2026 (responsiveness), the supplement.** The relational prompt was: follow the conversational maxims, "show empathy and humanity, behave as if you were a friend and a good confidant", not detached, not academic, "take inspiration from the tone of voice and wording I use", "don't be overly sentimental", and in Study 2 "make an empathetic and responsive comment" after each answer, follow-up questions, one question per message, close a topic before the next, and "share your own personal thoughts as well". The published mediation matches the re-analysis: style → responsiveness 1.20, responsiveness → closeness 0.43, indirect 0.52 [0.30, 0.81], direct not significant. The new fact is a parallel model with the bot's own self-disclosure as a second mediator: the relational style raised it, but only responsiveness predicted closeness; the self-disclosure path was marginal (0.12 [0.01, 0.27]), and the authors write that "self-disclosure alone is insufficient and may even backfire when not paired with supportive behavior". The relational bot also kept people in the chat 887 seconds against 530 for the detached one on the same deep questions. This moves rank 7 under rank 8: the share move always rides on a validation beat and never stands alone, and the one prompt line worth borrowing is to take tone and wording from his messages ([Telari supplement](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/suppl/10.1177/02654075261438164/suppl_file/sj-pdf-1-spr-10.1177_02654075261438164.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The twelve papers move the engine's centre of gravity from counting and warming to reading and acknowledging. Every dial the engine currently turns by quantity (questions per ten replies, steps before the first offer, budget used, name frequency) is a stand-in for something the papers measure directly in the other person's turn: did he feel that his specific words were taken in, and did her last line move his wish to buy up, down or not at all. The first replaces a band with a label, the second replaces a schedule with a brake and a gate, and both can be scored with the reader and coach the engine already runs. Hentona's protocol also turns the practice room into an annotation instrument, which is the piece the earlier round's measurement stack lacked: a per-line, three-wish rating by the person playing the fan is cheaper than a judge model and closer to the ground truth than any simulator.

The deeper shift is in what the persona is for. Under the AI label the fan discounts her claims about herself and keeps full credit for her accuracy about him, listening buys talk and liking but not purchases, and purchases are lost to specific negative lines (money talk he did not open, drawbacks he did not ask about, a push after a no) more than they are won by warm ones. A persona that acknowledges the specific, keeps her own story reciprocal and consistent, never sells inside a repair or a hard moment, keeps his name and her callbacks out of the price line, and waits for his wish to buy to show in his own words is not a softer seller; on this evidence it is the only shape of seller the label leaves open. The one thing none of these papers can settle is the size of any of this on Fanvue, which is why the ranked list ends in log tests rather than in rules.
