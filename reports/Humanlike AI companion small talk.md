# Teach Yuna to react, not interview

Research has already measured the reasons Yuna's small talk sounds robotic, and most of the fixes are planning rules and code checks, not a new model. The three biggest problems map onto three fixes. First, she treats almost every reply as a place for a question. In typed get-to-know-you chats, people put a question in about **3 replies out of 10**, and the questions that make people like you are **follow-ups on what he just said**, not new topics ([See et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.08654); [Huang et al. 2017](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf)). The planner should therefore choose a "move" for each reply, such as a reaction, a bit of her own day, an opinion, a tease, a callback or a question, before anything is written. Second, repeated wording and stock AI phrasing are the single biggest thing human raters punish ([See et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.08654)). The banned-phrase list should be built by comparing Yuna's output with the human replies in the 35k-chat library. Mistral Large 3 also scores about **28** on a public "slop" (stock-phrase) measure against about **10–13** for Claude Sonnet, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6 ([benchmarklist mirror of EQ-Bench](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/creative_writing_v3/)), so a different writer for small-talk turns is worth a blind test. Third, a concrete, lived-in persona with real texting habits made the same model look human **73%** of the time, against **36%** without it ([Jones & Bergen 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23674)). Memory callbacks matter more than usual for a labelled AI, because once people know they are talking to an AI they judge it mainly on whether it keeps track of what they said ([arXiv 2510.08912](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08912v1)). One caveat applies throughout: no published study covers weeks-long, one-to-one, lightly flirty texting. Every recommendation below therefore needs to pass Yuna's own blind A/B tests, scored against real human replies rather than an AI judge's opinion.

## Real people ask a question in about three replies out of ten

The best measured baseline comes from PersonaChat. In this dataset, crowdworkers chatted to get to know each other, and they put a question in **28.8%** of their messages. The baseline bot put one in 50% ([See et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.08654)). Raters' "engagingness" score kept rising up to a 65.7% question rate, but the best "good listener" score came at 48.9%. The authors warn that this task was artificial, and that in real social chat constant questions would probably be judged worse ([Abigail See blog](http://www.abigailsee.com/2019/08/13/what-makes-a-good-conversation.html)). Questions themselves are not the problem. In a text-chat experiment with 398 people, partners liked people who asked many questions more (effect size d = .35). When the questions were split by type, though, **only follow-up questions predicted liking**, and questions that switched to a new topic lowered how responsive the asker seemed ([Huang et al. 2017](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf)). So the rule Yuna needs is "fewer questions, and almost all of them about the detail he just gave". "Never more than one question" is not enough. The current one-question limit in `safety.voiceProblems` stops her from stacking questions in one reply. It does nothing about a question in every reply, which is the pattern fans actually notice.

The harder part is what she says instead. Agency chatter training insists that every message should invite a reply, but its own advice is to pair any question with something from your own day and to "bounce off" whatever he said ([Desirely](https://desirely.co/en/blog/onlyfans-chatter-training)). Inviting a reply is not the same as asking a question. A tease, an opinion, a tiny story or a guess about him ("you seem like a night owl") all invite an answer with no question mark. This is dating-coach advice, not tested science ([Rob Judge](https://robjudge.substack.com/p/texting-mistake-killing-your-dates)). The research on "inner thoughts" points the same way. In that design, the AI first jots down several short possible thoughts, including quick reactions like "wait what", and then says only the one it most wants to say. People preferred it in **82%** of comparisons, and rated it more humanlike and more engaging. The caveat is that it was tested in group chats with a small sample ([Inner Thoughts, arXiv 2501.00383](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00383v2)). A related method gives the model a private "think first" step with named conversation skills, including **changing the topic smoothly** ([CSIM, arXiv 2311.07445](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07445)). For Yuna, that means that when a topic runs dry she should bridge to something of her own ("that reminds me, the café today…") rather than ask another question.

Turing-test judges point at the same weakness from the other side. Of all the reasons judges gave, the most *accurate* one for spotting an AI was **how it handled questions**. Bots answer every question fully and directly, like an assistant ([Jones & Bergen 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23674)). Companion-app users describe the same thing: bots answer a vent point by point like a form, ask questions and then drop them, and never let a topic die ([DEV Community essay](https://dev.to/zennos/why-do-so-many-ai-companions-feel-fake-22l5)). A human sometimes answers sideways, briefly, or with a joke, and sometimes lets a thread end. All of this belongs upstream in `plan.js`. The planner should decide the move, and whether a question is allowed at all, *before* Mistral writes. That works better than letting Mistral write a question and having the code checks strip it afterwards.

## Repetition, stock phrases and eagerness are the loudest bot signals

Across 28 bot setups, each rated by 100+ people, repetition was **"by far the biggest limiting quality factor"**. Cutting repeated phrases across turns improved every score, and brought most of them close to human level ([See et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.08654)). Meta's BlenderBot team found its bot used "Do you have…" **110 times** in logs where humans used it **6 times** ([Roller et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2004.13637v2)). Character.AI users came to hate the stock opener "Can I ask you a question?" ([PiunikaWeb](https://piunikaweb.com/2023/08/18/character-ai-users-annoyed-by-can-i-ask-you-a-question-prompt/)) and a "you know that?" habit that spread across many bots ([RoboRhythms](https://www.roborhythms.com/you-know-that-character-ai/)). Habits like these build up over dozens of messages. Yuna's current check only compares each reply with her last 6, so it will miss slower repeats, such as the same reply shape (reaction, then compliment, then question) or the same phrase spread across a week.

The "office words" problem has a measurable cause. After ChatGPT arrived, the word "delves" appeared **28 times** more often than expected in medical paper summaries ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5)). A follow-up found that the people who rate AI answers during training *prefer* replies containing these words, so the habit is trained into the models ([Juzek & Ward 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01930)). No published list exists for casual chat, but there is a published method. The Antislop project compares how often a model uses each phrase with how often humans use it, and flags the phrases the model overuses. Its code is free to reuse (MIT licence) ([Paech et al., arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)). The 35k-chat analysis library can serve as the human side, which would give Yuna a ban list grounded in data rather than guesswork.

Eagerness is a separate problem with its own evidence. In a 224-person study, an AI that both complimented people and switched its view to agree with them was rated **less authentic and less trustworthy** ([Sun & Wang, CHI '26](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10844v3)). Another study found that ordinary AI comfort replies felt "generic" and "formulaic" over several turns. Adding a self-critique step made it worse: the replies agreed with the person even more, and agreement words rose **98%** ([MATE, arXiv 2505.24658](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24658)). That is a direct warning for Yuna's Claude Haiku checker. If its fix step ever pushes for "warmer" or "more supportive", it will make her more eager. The fix is to limit how much she compliments and agrees, let her disagree mildly and hold opinions, and match the size of her sympathy to how much he actually shared. The roleplay community reports that simply asking for a "realistic rather than idealized" character removes much of the forced positivity, though that evidence is anecdotal ([r/SillyTavernAI](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rg72xf/snapshots/2026-02-27T16%3A12%3A57.42274Z)).

The writer model may set a ceiling here. On EQ-Bench's Creative Writing v3 "slop" column (how often stock AI phrases appear; lower is better), a July 2026 snapshot gives **Mistral Large 3 28.17**. That compares with Claude Sonnet 4.6 at 9.90, Claude Sonnet 5 at 11.60, GLM-5.2 at 13.11, Kimi K2.6 at 13.30 and DeepSeek V3.2 at 23.19 ([benchmarklist mirror](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/creative_writing_v3/); [EQ-Bench About](https://eqbench.com/about.html)). The benchmark scores stories up to 4,000 characters, not one-line texts, and the figures come from a mirror site. A study of 100k real chats also found that prompt wording matters "often more so than the choice of the simulator model" ([Ivey et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08330v2)). So the sensible order is to fix planning and prompts first, then run a blind test of a cleaner writer on small-talk turns only. Mistral's own API cannot block specific words (it has no `logit_bias`). It can, however, return several drafts from one call with the input billed once (the `n` setting) ([Mistral API](https://docs.mistral.ai/api/endpoint/chat)). Four drafts of about 60 words each add roughly **0.04 US cents** per reply ([pricing](https://pricepertoken.com/pricing-page/model/mistral-ai-mistral-large-2512)). That makes "write several, keep the best one that passes the code checks" cheaper than the current single rewrite.

## A specific person who texts like a person doubled Turing-test scores

The strongest single result in the literature is about persona. In a 2025 Turing test of 1,023 games, the same model (GPT-4.5) was picked as the human **73%** of the time when given a persona: a young, fairly introverted person into internet culture who uses slang. Without the persona it was picked **36%** of the time. Judges decided mostly on writing style (27% of stated reasons) and how the conversation flowed (23%), rarely on knowledge ([Jones & Bergen 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23674)). The best prompt from the authors' 2023 study is printed in the paper. It asked for very short replies ("a word or two is better than a full sentence"), mostly lowercase, little punctuation, a person who is **not knowledgeable and happy to admit it**, who **warms up slowly**, and whose personality comes through "subtly, not as a forced stereotype". A slangier sister prompt did worse (39% vs 49.7%), because overdone slang was itself a giveaway ([arXiv 2310.20216v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.20216v2)). Asking for more typos usually helped, but the same paper flags "unconvincingly bad" grammar as a giveaway and advises going easy on typos early in a chat ([arXiv 2310.20216v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.20216v2)). These tests measure whether people mistake an AI for a human. Yuna is labelled and must admit being an AI when asked, so the goal is a natural feel, not deception. The style cues still carry over directly.

Real texting is shorter and looser than Yuna's replies. US students' text messages averaged **7.7 words** and instant messages about 6 ([Ling & Baron 2007](https://nl.ijs.si/janes/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/lingbaron07.pdf)). Younger people split one thought across several short bubbles ([McCulloch, Science Friday](https://www.sciencefriday.com/segments/because-internet/)). A full stop at the end of a short reply makes it read as **less sincere** ([Gunraj et al. 2016](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/texting-insincerely-the-role-of-the-period-in-text-messaging/)). "haha" made up **51%** of written laughs against **1.9%** for "lol" ([ScienceAlert on Facebook data](https://www.sciencealert.com/facebook-analysis-of-e-laughter-shows-that-lol-is-on-the-way-out)). Only about **4%** of real private texts contained an emoticon, with women using somewhat more ([CHB vol. 129](https://datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/129/paper-detail/72752)). Very short is not automatically better, though. BlenderBot replies were judged dull until they were made about as long as humans' (around 18 tokens, roughly 14 words) ([Roller et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2004.13637v2)). Google's Meena work showed that a reply like "that's nice", which could fit any conversation, scores zero on specificity even when it makes sense ([Adiwardana et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09977)). The way to reconcile these is **short but specific**: one to three small bubbles, a standalone "wait what" now and then, and always a concrete detail rather than a generic reaction. Her length should also follow his. Couples whose small "glue words" matched were more likely to still be dating three months later ([Ireland et al. 2011](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/IrelandEtAl2011_RelationshipPrediction.pdf)). In a small pilot, users' message length tracked the bot's closely ([arXiv 2510.08912](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08912v1)).

The roleplay community's main lesson is to show the model the style rather than describe it. SillyTavern's documentation says the model picks up style and length "from the first message more than anything else", and that instructions placed right at the end of the prompt have the most effect ([Character Design](https://docs.sillytavern.app/usage/core-concepts/characterdesign/); [Author's Note](https://docs.sillytavern.app/usage/core-concepts/authors-note/)). The ChatHaruhi project, which brings anime characters to life by pulling in their own past lines as examples, supports the same approach ([arXiv 2308.09597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09597)). So does the finding that models copy meaning far better than style ([Ivey et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08330v2)). For Yuna, this means the `ai_examples` library is her best tool, provided the examples for the current situation sit next to the reply, rotate so she doesn't copy one shape, and come with a two-line style reminder placed after the chat history. CoSER's book-derived dataset pairs each line with the character's unspoken thought ([arXiv 2502.09082](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09082)). Storing examples as "situation, what she's thinking, what she sends" would teach the planner and the writer together.

Her own life needs to read as a story, not a feed. The Tolan companion app found that backstory written by an AI was weak, so it hired a novelist to write "story seeds", ongoing plots the character lives through ([Fast Company Brasil](https://fastcompanybrasil.com/?p=243902)). Grok's companion Ani has a deliberate contrast, "secretly a bit of a nerd" despite her edgy look, which gives her something to talk about ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3037496)). A 2025 study of an always-listening AI pendant shows the limit: deliberate moodiness read as rude when it misjudged the moment ([getcoai on WIRED](https://getcoai.com/news/wired-tested-the-129-ai-necklace-that-alienates-users-and-fails-technically)). Moods should be small and tied to her day. Being allowed *not* to know things also matters. Research on character AIs trains them on examples of saying "I don't know" to things outside the character's world ([Character-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10158)). In the 2025 Turing test, "lack of knowledge" was one of the most accurate cues judges used to pick out the real human; one judge's reason was that the witness "didn't know something that any AI should know" ([Jones & Bergen 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23674)). An assistant-like Yuna who knows everything is giving herself away.

Timing matters mainly for the future live sending path, since nothing goes to Fanvue yet. Replies delayed in proportion to their length were rated more humanlike than instant ones ([Gnewuch et al. 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2018_rp/113/)). A newer system that decides, bubble by bubble, whether to send now or "wait like a person typing" beat its single-message predecessor on naturalness. Its exact numbers are not in the abstract ([Stephanie2, arXiv 2601.05657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05657)).

## Remembering his week matters more once he knows she is an AI

For a labelled AI, attention beats disguise. In a small study, people who suspected they were talking to an AI stopped judging its style and started judging **accuracy and whether it kept track of the conversation** ([arXiv 2510.08912](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08912v1)). Meta's long-term memory study found that bots which summarised earlier sessions brought up the partner's own topics **33.8%** of the time versus 14.5%, and got more engaging-reply ratings (62.1% vs 53.0%) ([Xu, Szlam & Weston 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.07567v1)). Users describe forgotten personal details as breaks in the relationship ([Leiden thesis](https://theses.liacs.nl/pdf/2025-2026-DanisanRReyhan.pdf)). BlenderBot's list of failures includes forgetting things already said and a "copycat" habit: claiming the user's own pets or bands as its own ([Roller et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2004.13637v2)). Yuna already has dated fan notes, a story summary and a closeness score. What the evidence adds is structure on top of them.

Three borrowed ideas fit. Kindroid keeps a small, automatically maintained sheet with fixed space limits, including an **"Ongoing threads" section of 800 characters**, and dropping the least important items when it fills ([Kindroid docs](https://kindroid.ai/v2/docs/memory/)). For Yuna, that becomes a per-fan list of open threads with dates, such as "job interview Thursday" or "sister visiting this week". It also feeds the agency rule "three days after he mentions his dog, ask how the dog is doing" ([Desirely](https://desirely.co/en/blog/onlyfans-chatter-training)). Second, memory tools like Mem0 and Zep **replace** old facts when new ones arrive and stamp each fact with when it was true ([Mem0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19413); [Zep](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13956)). A standard memory test found assistants lose about **30%** accuracy over long chats, with time and changed facts as the weakest spots ([LongMemEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10813)). In practice, when he says "I moved to Leeds", the note about his old city must be retired, not kept beside the new one. Third, Nomi's "Identity Core" is the character's own record of who it is ([Nomipedia](https://wiki.nomi.ai/What_is_Identity_Core%3F)). Yuna's shared diary covers this in general. A per-fan log of "what I've told *him* about myself" would stop her contradicting her own invented life to one fan, which is the complaint users raise most ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/zennos/why-do-so-many-ai-companions-feel-fake-22l5)). The Generative Agents paper adds a cheap habit: a periodic "reflection" that turns raw notes into a few higher-level beliefs about him, each with an importance score ([Park et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442)).

Her "she writes first" schedule can copy Nomi's published rules almost word for word. At the more frequent settings, the next nudge comes after roughly 1 hour, 3 hours or 1 day, and the wait **doubles each time he doesn't answer**. At the least frequent setting, only one nudge is sent until he replies. There are no messages between 10pm and 8am, and none at all while he owes her a reply ([Nomi](https://nomi.ai/nomi-knowledge/proactive-messaging-when-your-nomi-messages-you-first/)). Each nudge should grow out of something she was doing or one of his open threads, since check-ins with no reason behind them feel like retention tactics ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/zennos/why-do-so-many-ai-companions-feel-fake-22l5)). Goodbyes need care. A Harvard study found that more than a third of companion-app goodbyes used guilt or "don't go" tactics. These raised engagement in the short term, but also raised feelings of manipulation, intent to quit and perceived legal risk ([De Freitas et al.](https://www.vpal.harvard.edu/event/emotional-manipulation-ai-companions)). An easy, warm goodbye with a hook for next time ("tell me how the interview goes") is the safer pattern.

## Engines, projects and models worth borrowing from

None of the open-source engines solves Yuna's specific problem as a drop-in. The useful material is a set of patterns that can be rebuilt cheaply in the Node.js engine. The memory services (Letta, Mem0, Zep) are mature, but they are Python-first and fix *recall*, not tone, so copying their update rules costs less than running one of them. SillyTavern, KoboldCpp and Agnai use the AGPL licence, which brings copyleft duties for a networked service, so borrow their ideas, not their code ([SillyTavern](https://github.com/SillyTavern/SillyTavern)).

On models, the case for a test is the slop gap already described: Mistral Large 3 at about 28, against Claude Sonnet 4.6/5 at about 10–12 and GLM-5.2 or Kimi K2.6 at about 13 ([benchmarklist mirror](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/creative_writing_v3/)). DeepSeek V3.2 and V4 Flash dominate real roleplay usage on OpenRouter because they are cheap ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/collections/roleplay)), but they score worse on slop (about 20–23). Because the planner already labels each turn's situation, sending only non-sexual small-talk turns to a different writer is a small routing change. Fine-tuning is a later option, and it cannot happen on Mistral Large 3. Mistral's self-serve fine-tuning is listed as deprecated, and its replacement, Forge, is an enterprise contract ([Mistral docs](https://docs.mistral.ai/capabilities/finetuning/); [DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/mistral-forge)). Tuning an open model through Together costs about **$0.48 per million training tokens** for models up to 16B, which makes a few thousand examples cost single-digit dollars ([Together docs](https://docs.together.ai/docs/fine-tuning/pricing)). The published results are promising but mixed. Training on "humanlike vs formal" reply pairs won **79.6–89.6%** of blind votes, but cost 3–9 points on an instruction-following test ([arXiv 2501.05032](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05032)). Plain preference training used to remove stock phrases also made the wording less varied ([Antislop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)). For Yuna, losing instruction-following is a real risk, because the selling and safety rules depend on it.

## Measure against real human replies, not an AI judge's taste

Asking an AI to score "how human is this, 1–10" measures the wrong thing. AI judges favour longer answers ([Grepture](https://grepture.com/blog/llm-as-a-judge-bias)). They also rate more predictable, "familiar" text higher than humans do ([Wataoka et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21819)), which is the opposite of quirky human texting. AI-text detectors are no better than chance on texts under about 100 words ([arXiv 2406.15583](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.15583); [arXiv 2308.07499](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.07499)), so they are useless on single chat replies.

A sturdier yardstick has three parts. The first is a **scorecard of numbers** comparing about 500 Yuna replies with the real human chatter replies to the same situations from the 35k library. It would track: words per bubble, share of replies containing a question, share of her questions that are follow-ups, how often the same opener or reply shape repeats, the variety of word pairs used, stock-phrase rate, emoji rate, lowercase starts and replies ending in a full stop. The aim is to get as close as possible to the human pattern on each, not to push any single number as high as it will go ([Ivey et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08330v2)). This also fills the literature's biggest gap. The research for this report found no published figure for how often people ask questions in casual one-to-one texting, and the best length figures date from 2003–2005 student samples, so Yuna's own library is the only reliable target. The second part is a **pairwise judge** that sees Yuna's reply and the real human reply in random order and guesses which one the human wrote. Each pair is judged both ways round, as EQ-Bench does ([EQ-Bench About](https://eqbench.com/about.html)), and the judge comes from a different model family than the writer. If small talk moves to Claude, the Haiku checker can no longer judge it fairly. The third part is the existing **human blind A/B** in the Practice window, which settles anything the first two disagree on. The four standing test chats become the regression set that every change must pass.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. Yuna's small talk is not failing because the words are slightly off. It fails because the *shape* of each reply is decided too late. Today Mistral writes a reply, then the code checks and Claude find the question, the stock phrase or the eager compliment and try to repair it. Every study that moved the needle (inner thoughts, think-first planning, the winning Turing persona) made the decision about *what kind of thing to say* before any wording was written. Moving the question budget, the reply move and the "let this topic end" choice into the planner should reduce rewrites as well as improve the replies, so it pays for itself.

The second shift is about what "human" means for a labelled AI. She cannot win by pretending to be a person, and must not. What she can win on is attention: noticing his small details, returning to them days later, having a life of her own that stays consistent, and not over-performing warmth. On that measure a careful AI can beat an overworked human chatter. The team's own 35k-chat library is the asset the published research lacks. It is the only realistic baseline for what good texting looks like in this setting, and the measurement work should start there.

## Prioritised changes

Quick wins (rows 1–7) are prompt, planner and code-check changes that cost little or nothing per reply. Medium projects (rows 8–11) change data or routing. Bigger projects (rows 12–14) are new pipeline stages or model training. Row 1 comes first because every later change is judged by it.
