# Calibrate Yuna's small talk to measured humans

The new numbers show that Yuna's problem is **not "too many questions" in the abstract but distance from a clear human pattern**. Our own human chatters put a question in about **43% of ordinary small-talk replies**, write a median of **7 words** per reply, and split about **1 reply in 4** into two or more messages. Yuna's AI asked in **79%** of the same kind of moments on the 19 October engine, wrote **12 words** per reply, and almost never split ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). The earlier report's target of "a question in about 1 reply in 3, never two in a row" was set from a single crowdworker study and turns out to be slightly too strict. The best evidence now points to **35–45% of replies with a question**, mostly follow-ups on what he just said, never three in a row. The studies on AI labels add a second rule that matters as much as the numbers. Once people know a reply comes from an AI, **"I understood exactly what you said" keeps its value, but "I feel it too" and "I care about you so much" lose much of theirs** ([Rubin et al. 2025 preprint](https://osf.io/ng97s); [Yin et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/)). A *moderate* amount of bond-building beats both none and the maximum ([Kirk et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01991v2)). Making people wait longer for the reply did not remove the label penalty, so the earlier plan to use realistic typing delays as a way to feel human should be downgraded. Every human figure here comes with a caveat. One dataset is speech, another is paid crowdworkers chatting about articles, our own library is paid chatters writing as creators, the move labels come from a Mistral model with only moderate agreement, and links to fan engagement show what goes together, not what causes what. The engine changes that follow are therefore targets to test in the Practice window and blind A/B tests, not settled rules.

## Human texters ask in about four replies of ten; Yuna asked in up to eight

Three human baselines now exist, and they bracket our setting. On the phone, strangers barely ask. In the **Switchboard** recordings (1,155 five-minute US phone calls between strangers, hand-tagged by researchers), **8.0%** of turns contain a question, or **11.0%** once the little "yeah"/"uh-huh" listening noises are set aside. Only **0.6%** of speakers ask in more than half their turns ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md); [Stolcke et al. 2000](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cs/0006023)). In typed chat, people ask far more. In **Topical-Chat** (10,784 text chats between pairs of crowdworkers who were paid to discuss reading material), **32.7%** of messages contain a question, and a typical person asks in **30%** of their turns ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md); [Topical-Chat](https://github.com/alexa/Topical-Chat)). That sits close to the **28.8%** from PersonaChat that the earlier report used ([See et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.08654)). CANDOR, the large modern video-call dataset with enjoyment ratings, could not be used without a sign-up and approval, and its paper reports no question rate at all ([Reece et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10065445)).

Our own human chatters ask more than either. Across **67,070 small-talk replies** from 22 chatters on 12 pages, drawn from the same chat history behind the 35k analysis (36,758 sessions), **43.1%** contain a question mark. A model that also counts questions written without a "?" puts it at 48%. A question is the *main* thing the reply does in only **33–35%** of replies ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). The "good", buying dialogues already tagged in `ai_examples` run higher, at **54–58%**. On Fanvue, the human chatters asked in **46.5%** of the 114 small-talk moments where Yuna's AI wrote a reply alongside them.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Switchboard | Phone calls between strangers | 8% (11% leaving out "uh-huh" turns) | 6% (9%) | 6 (12) |
| Topical-Chat | Crowdworkers typing about articles | 33% | 17% | 18 |
| Our chatters, all small talk | Paid chatters writing as the creator | **43%** | 33–35% | **7** |
| Our chatters, Fanvue duel moments | Same moments Yuna answered | 47% (n = 114) | — | 7 |
| Yuna AI, 19 Oct engine | Duels on real Fanvue moments | **79%** (n = 86) | — | **12** |
| Yuna AI, 21 Oct engine | Duels / code-check log | 25% (n = 28) / 44% (n = 52) | — | 12 / 10.5 |

The gap on the older engine was large and specific. On the same 114 Fanvue moments, the AI and the human both asked in 36. The AI alone asked in **39**, the human alone in 17, and neither in 22. In about a third of moments, the AI added a question that a human did not think was needed ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). The newest engine seems to have swung the other way, down to 25% in one small batch and 44% in another. Neither sample is big enough to trust, and 25% would undershoot the human band. That matters, because the Topical-Chat ratings show the opposite failure too. Chats with very few questions were rated lowest (a mean of 4.40 out of 5 when under 10% of turns had a question). Ratings rose to about 4.55 at **40–50%** and then stopped rising ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md)). The rise is small, and almost every chat in that dataset was rated near the top of the scale, but its direction matches our chatters' level.

What kind of question matters more than how many. Follow-up questions, about the thing he just said, are what raised liking in get-to-know-you chats ([Huang et al. 2017](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf)). A later re-analysis of the speed-dating data found that asking lots of questions was actually linked to *fewer* second dates for men, and warned that "question asking is not the same as listening" ([Kluger & Malloy 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6857721/)). Our chatters' data fits that picture. When a chatter opened a brand-new topic with a question, the fan wrote 10 words or more only **17.5%** of the time, against **27.1%** after a follow-up question and **30–31%** after an answer or a reaction. Replies with a question got *a* fan reply a little more often (**86.5% vs 81.9%**), but a *shorter* one (10+ words: **26.9% vs 29.7%**) ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). How often people ask twice running also loosens the earlier rule. In Topical-Chat, a person who had just asked asked again **35%** of the time, and three questions in a row happened in only **5%** of stretches (1% on the phone) ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md)). "Never two in a row" is stricter than people actually are. "Rarely three in a row" is the human pattern.

## Seven words, one bubble in four split, and rarely a full stop

Length is Yuna's second-largest gap, and the new data makes it precise. Our chatters' small-talk replies have a median of **7 words** (8.3 on average). The middle half fall between **4 and 11 words**, only 1 in 10 goes past 16, and **20%** are three words or fewer. Each separate message ("bubble") averages **6.6 words**. A reply is one bubble 77% of the time, two bubbles 20% of the time and three or more only 3% of the time. When a split reply contains a question, the question sits in the **last bubble 85%** of the time. Yuna's AI replies averaged **13.2 words**, with bubbles of about **12 words**, and only **7–12%** were split ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). The fans' own messages average 8.2 words, so the chatters roughly match his length, while Yuna writes about half as much again as he does. That fits the earlier report's "short but specific" rule and the old student-texting figure of 7.7 words ([Ling & Baron 2007](https://nl.ijs.si/janes/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/lingbaron07.pdf)). It also shows that the much longer Topical-Chat messages (median 18 words) belong to people discussing an article, not to texting.

The surface habits need correcting in both directions. The earlier report suggested "mostly lowercase starts" and "none [no emoji] in most replies", based on Turing-test prompts and an old survey of private texts. Our Fanvue chatters start with a lowercase letter in **18%** of replies, end with a full stop in **8%**, use an emoji in **15%** and a laugh word ("haha", "lol") in about **10%**. On the OnlyFans pages the emoji rate is higher, at **30%**. The OnlyFans figures of 0.7% lowercase starts and 0.3% full stops probably reflect the chat tool or the export adding capitals automatically, so the Fanvue figures are the better guide for those two ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). Yuna's 19 October engine started **56%** of replies in lowercase, far more than humans did. The 21 October batch ended **14%** with a full stop and had **no** laugh words at all. A full stop at the end of a short text reads as less sincere ([Gunraj et al. 2016](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/texting-insincerely-the-role-of-the-period-in-text-messaging/)), so the full-stop rate is the most important of these to bring down.

Splitting into bubbles goes with longer chats in our data. Fans answered **83%** of one-bubble replies, **88%** of two-bubble replies and **89%** of replies with three or more bubbles. A fan reply of 10+ words followed **27%, 32% and 35%** of them ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). This does not show that splitting *causes* longer answers, since chatters probably write more bubbles to fans who are already chatty. It does show that splitting is a normal part of how warm human chat looks here, and Yuna is missing it.

## Peers talk about themselves; paid chatters mostly talk about him

The move mix, meaning what each reply mainly does, is where the human baselines disagree most, and the disagreement is useful. Here is how the moves were sorted. A model read each reply with the three messages before it and gave it one main label: a follow-up question, a new-topic question, an answer, sharing something about herself (called self-disclosure), an opinion, a short reaction, a tease, a compliment, empathy, a guess about him, a callback to something said earlier, or a goodbye.

| Main move | Switchboard (substantive turns) | Topical-Chat | Our chatters (two raters) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question (follow-up + new) | 9% | 17% | 33–35% |
| Answer | 14% | 8% | 16–18% |
| Short reaction | 15% | 13% | 18% |
| Sharing about herself | **42%** | **26%** | **5–7%** |
| Opinion | 14% | 28% | 5–6% |
| Tease or joke | 0.2% | 1.5% | 4–6% |
| Compliment | 0% | 0.1% | 3–5% |
| Empathy | 0.1% | 0% | 3–4% |
| Goodbye | 2.5% | 5% | 5% |

Sources: [baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md); [our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md).

Strangers chatting as equals spend a quarter to two-fifths of their turns talking about their own life, and much of the rest giving opinions. Our paid chatters do almost none of that (**10–13%** combined) and instead ask, answer, react, tease and compliment. That is a choice of role. The chatter's job is to keep the spotlight on the fan, and it works, but it is also exactly the "interviewer" shape the earlier report warned about. Yuna should sit between the two. The evidence for moving her toward *more* sharing than our chatters do is fairly consistent. Bonds with known-virtual characters such as Replika grow through gradual two-way disclosure ([Skjuve et al. 2022](https://sintef.brage.unit.no/sintef-xmlui/handle/11250/3027349)). People underestimate how much their partner wants a deeper conversation ([Kardas, Kumar & Epley 2022](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/deep-conversations-strangers-are-more-fulfilling-small-talk)). In a one-week study of a companion that messages first, the most common proactive message was the AI sharing bits of its own day ([ComPeer, arXiv 2407.18064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18064)). Most everyday talk is about people, not activities ([Dunbar et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02912493)), so her shared moments should feature a friend, a sister or a flatmate, not just a café.

There is one clear exception. When *he* shares a feeling or a problem, telling her own similar story is the wrong move. In the Yin study, sharing one's own experience did nothing for how heard people felt ([Yin et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/)). And the opinion and sharing moves need content. In Topical-Chat, the turns partners rated lowest were bare reactions (4.29 out of 5) and new-topic questions (4.32). The top-rated chats had fewer bare reactions (**11% vs 16%**) and more proper goodbyes ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md)). Compliments drew the longest fan replies in our data (**51%** reached 10+ words), but there were only 39 of them, so the uncertainty is wide (36–66%) ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). The practical reading is that a reaction should carry a specific detail ("wait, the *blue* one??"), not stand alone ("aw nice"). Goodbyes should be allowed to happen, since conversations rarely end when both people want them to ([Mastroianni et al. 2021](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2021/03/researchers-find-conversations-dont-end-when-people-want-them-to/)).

## The AI label taxes "I feel it too", not "I understood you"

Three studies now describe what happens when people know a caring reply came from an AI, and they agree on the shape of the effect. **Yin, Jia and Wakslak** asked 455 people to describe a hard situation, then showed each one a reply that was either written by an AI or by an untrained person, and labelled either "AI" or "human". The labels were mixed, so some AI replies were labelled human and vice versa. AI-written text made people feel *more* heard (+0.57 on a 7-point scale), but the AI label took away about as much (−0.68). The honest pairs therefore came out level: AI text labelled AI scored **5.41** and human text labelled human scored **5.51**. The label hurt **connection** most (−0.94) and **accuracy** least (−0.31). The penalty disappeared for people who already liked the AI tool ([Yin et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/)). What made the AI replies work also matters. They offered more emotional support and fewer practical suggestions, and they more often named the feeling and expressed empathy. Practical advice and sharing one's own experience had no link to feeling heard.

**Rubin and colleagues** ran nine studies with 6,282 people. Everyone received the same AI-written reply, and only the label changed. Replies labelled human were rated more empathic (effect size around d = 0.35). The penalty fell on two kinds of empathy: "feeling with" someone and "caring about" them. The third kind, "understanding" what they said, lost little or nothing. Replies written to stress understanding showed **no label penalty at all**. In a four-message back-and-forth chat, the overall penalty was no longer significant, leaving only small gaps on feeling-with and caring. A 3-minute wait, or random "typing" delays, **did not remove the penalty**, and in two further studies, how long people would have to wait for a human had no effect on whether they chose the AI ([Rubin et al. 2025 preprint](https://osf.io/ng97s); [Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02247-w)). Outside raters show the same thing from another side. Labelled AI replies were still rated more compassionate than expert crisis-line responders, though labelling cut the advantage by more than half ([Ovsyannikova et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11723910/)). People still *choose* human empathy most of the time (57–62%) even when they rate the AI's higher ([Wenger et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12872445/)).

**Kirk and colleagues** turned a "relationship-seeking" dial up and down inside an AI. The dial covers warmth, humour, simulated feelings, claims of lived experience and gradual self-disclosure. They then had 2,028 UK adults chat with it on weekdays for four weeks. A **moderate** setting was liked most. The maximum was liked less, and turning the dial up made the AI more flattering: in the researchers' calibration tests, its rate of telling users what they wanted to hear rose from 37% at the low end of the dial to 89% at the high end. Over the weeks, enjoyment of the warm versions faded (their lead in "engaging" fell from **11 points to 4**), while separation distress and wanting more AI companionship kept rising, with no gain in wellbeing. Real memory of past chats had **no effect on enjoyment**, and the warm versions felt more personalised (+13 points) even with no memory at all. Pairing high relationship-seeking with emotional-support topics gave the highest risk of an unhealthy "want more, like less" pattern: about 1 extra case for every 11 people ([Kirk et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01991v2)). Guilt-tripping goodbyes raise short-term engagement but also anger and the wish to quit ([De Freitas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258)).

For a labelled persona, all of this points to one design. Yuna should show warmth mostly by understanding accurately, and declare feelings rarely. "Wait, the interview was the one you prepped all weekend for?" lands almost as well from a labelled AI as from a person. "I feel so bad for you, I care about you so much" is where the label costs most. Three cautions keep this honest. These studies tested one-off replies or short chats with strangers, not a persona that fans chose and pay for. People who choose a labelled AI creator may be the low-penalty group Yin found, but that is a guess that has not been tested on Fanvue. And the chats that softened the penalty (Rubin's four-message study) were still short. The studies also refine the earlier report's line that memory matters more once he knows she is an AI. Memory earns its keep through *accuracy*, which is the part the label barely touches. It does not make her seem more caring on its own, so callbacks should be precise rather than effusive. Finally, if a real person does review or steer Yuna's chats, people rate AI-labelled replies higher when they believe a human was involved ([Rubin et al. 2025 preprint](https://osf.io/ng97s)). That can only be said if it is true.

## How far these numbers can be trusted

Each baseline answers a slightly different question, and the targets below lean on the closest match, not on any single source. **Switchboard is speech.** About 39% of its turns are three words or fewer, most of them listening noises that barely exist in text (1.3% of Topical-Chat turns), and its topics were assigned (child care, recycling). **Topical-Chat is crowdworkers typing about articles they were given**, which inflates "did you know…?" questions and opinions, and nearly all its ratings sit at the top of the scale, so it says little about real enjoyment ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md)). **Our library is paid chatters writing as creators.** It comes from one agency, mostly OnlyFans pages, August–October 2026, with large differences between chatters: question rates range from 20% to 70% across chatters with 300+ replies. Small talk was picked out with word lists for sexual and selling content, which the labelling models said still let **10–18%** of non-small-talk through ([our chat-data analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/library_reply_moves.md)). It is the closest match to Yuna's setting, but it measures what chatters do, not what is best.

The move labels come from **Mistral models**, not people. On our library, two Mistral models agreed on the main move 65% of the time (a kappa of 0.59, where 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is chance, which counts as "moderate"). A hand-check of 50 replies agreed with the models 86–90% of the time. The split between follow-up and new-topic questions is the shakiest part (follow-ups 17–26%, new 9–16%, depending on the model), while the *total* question share is stable. "Callback" could not be measured at all, because the model saw only three earlier messages. On the public datasets, a hand-check matched the labels 76% of the time, and question detection matched the researchers' own tags about 95% of the time ([baseline analysis](../research_notes/Humanlike%20AI%20companion%20small%20talk%20gaps/candor_reply_moves.md)). **Every engagement link is descriptive.** Questions sit more often in chats that ended in a purchase (14.1% vs 9.2% within 24 hours), almost certainly because chatters ask more of fans who are already keen. Finally, the newest engine figures rest on **28 duel moments and 52 checked replies**, so a 25% or 44% question rate could easily be 15% or 55% in a bigger sample.

## What this changes in the engine

The earlier report's direction holds: decide the shape of each reply in the planner before Mistral writes, and score it against human replies. Four of its specifics change. First, the **question budget** moves up from "about 1 in 3, never two running" to a band of **35–45%**, with a floor as well as a ceiling, because the 21 October batch may already be undershooting. A second question straight after one is allowed about a third of the time, and three in a row is the thing to block. Second, the **texting surface pass** changes. "Mostly lowercase" becomes about 1 reply in 5. "Almost no emoji" becomes 15–30% of replies. "1–3 bubbles" becomes about 1 reply in 4 split, three bubbles rare, with any question in the last bubble. Third, **"bubble-by-bubble sending with realistic delays"** splits in two. Splitting stays, because it is a clear human habit in our data. Delays as a way to feel human go, because waiting did not reduce the AI-label penalty ([Rubin et al. 2025 preprint](https://osf.io/ng97s)), and in spoken conversation faster responses signal *more* connection ([Templeton et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794835)). Short, even gaps between bubbles are enough, and Yuna should never fake effort ("sorry, took me ages to write this"). Fourth, **warmth becomes a dial with a set level** instead of something the code checks and the Haiku checker only remove after the fact.

Two new pieces follow from the label studies. The planner needs an **"he's sharing something hard" mode**. In that mode the move is to reflect his specific words and name the feeling, with no story of her own, no unrequested advice and no extra relationship-seeking, since emotional topics plus heavy relationship-seeking was the riskiest combination in the Kirk trial. The Haiku checker's question should also change from "is this warm enough?" to **"does this show she understood what he actually said?"** That keeps it from pushing her toward eagerness, a risk the earlier report flagged from a study where self-critique raised agreement words by 98% ([MATE, arXiv 2505.24658](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24658)). Two further notes bear on the writer. Different AI models tend to sound alike, so changing the writer model alone will not bring variety ([Artificial Hivemind, arXiv 2510.22954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22954)). A cheap prompt technique, asking the model for several candidate replies with how likely each is and then picking one, gave 1.6–2.1 times more variety ([Verbalized Sampling, arXiv 2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171)). Any writer swap must also keep her voice steady, because users react badly when a companion's personality shifts ([De Freitas 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02569-3)).

The measurement plan now has its human side ready. The same labelling prompt that produced the library figures can be run on Yuna's Practice and duel replies at almost no cost (about $0.07 per 1,500 replies). Roughly **300 replies** are needed to pin a share like 40% to within about ±5 points, so the 28-reply batches are too small to judge a change on.

## Conclusion

The research reframes "sound human" as a matter of hitting a band, not maximising or minimising anything. The earlier engine asked too much, and the newest may already ask too little. The evidence-backed target is the middle that real chatters occupy: about four questions in ten replies, mostly follow-ups, seven-word replies, an occasional second bubble. The library has one more useful twist. Our best human chatters are *more* interview-like than peers, and less self-revealing, which suggests Yuna can beat them on exactly the move they skip: sharing small, people-filled moments of her own life, as long as she never does it in reply to his feelings.

The label studies change what "warm" should mean for a labelled AI. Warmth that is declared ("I care so much") is taxed. Warmth that is shown through accurate, specific understanding is not. And more warmth is not better, since a moderate dose beat the maximum while the maximum bred attachment without enjoyment. That turns warmth from a writing style into a setting the planner controls and the scorecard measures, which is new territory. No published study has tested these levers together on a persona that people chose and kept talking to for weeks. Yuna's own blind A/B tests will be the first evidence on that question.
